12.2 Women, Youth, and Informality

Vietnam's labor market looks stable in headline statistics, but its deeper problems are concentrated in women, youth, informal workers, and workers outside social insurance. These groups reveal the difference between employment and decent work. A worker may be employed, but still lack a written contract, social insurance, maternity protection, unemployment insurance, training, legal protection, or bargaining power.

12.2.1 Women: High Participation, Unequal Conditions

Female labor participation has long been one of Vietnam's development strengths. Women work in agriculture, household businesses, garments, footwear, electronics, services, retail, education, health, public administration, and export manufacturing. This has supported household income, poverty reduction, and manufacturing competitiveness.

The strength should not hide the constraints. Women often face care burdens, occupational segmentation, lower access to leadership, and weaker protection in informal work. The ILO and the Institute of Labour Science and Social Affairs found that informal employment accounted for 68.5 percent of total employment in 2021. Men had a higher informal employment rate than women, but women in informal employment faced disproportionate vulnerabilities because they were more concentrated in own-account work and contributing family work, had less income security, and carried heavier unpaid care responsibilities.[1]

This is administratively important because labor-market policy, social insurance policy, childcare policy, education policy, and gender equality policy cannot be separated. If childcare is limited, women may choose informal or flexible work even when formal employment would bring higher protection. If social insurance does not reach household businesses, street vendors, family workers, or part-time workers, women's work remains invisible to the welfare state. If training is not accessible to women with care responsibilities, skill upgrading reinforces inequality.

12.2.2 Youth: The School-to-Work Bottleneck

Youth unemployment is higher than total unemployment. WDI/ILO modeled data put Vietnam's youth unemployment rate above the total unemployment rate throughout recent years, and NSO quarterly reporting in 2025 showed youth unemployment remaining several times the overall working-age rate.[2] This does not mean Vietnam has a mass youth unemployment crisis in the same way as some economies. It means the transition from school to stable work is uneven.

The youth problem has three layers. The first is skills mismatch. Young workers may complete schooling without the technical, digital, language, teamwork, and problem-solving skills required by firms. The second is credential mismatch. Employers may demand certificates or experience that new entrants do not have, while vocational pathways remain lower status than university pathways. The third is job-quality mismatch. Young people may find work, but often in informal services, platform work, casual construction, family business, or factory jobs with limited promotion prospects.

Youth labor policy therefore cannot be solved by unemployment programs alone. It requires career guidance, vocational training, apprenticeships, employer partnerships, internship quality control, public employment services, digital labor-market platforms, and local coordination between schools and industrial zones. The state must help young workers move into productive career ladders rather than cycling through short-term jobs.

12.2.3 Informality as the Central Labor-Market Constraint

Informality is the key structural weakness of Vietnam's labor market. OECD's 2025 Economic Survey reports that Vietnam had 33.6 million informal workers in 2021, equal to 68.5 percent of total employment. In practical terms, two-thirds of workers were employed without a labor contract, labor-law protection, and social insurance.[3]

Informality is not only an agricultural problem. OECD notes that informal workers are heavily concentrated in agriculture, forestry, and fisheries, but also in retail trade, construction, transport, and manufacturing activities.[3] Many work in household businesses, small private enterprises, own-account activities, contributing family work, and casual jobs. These workers can be economically active and socially important, but they are weakly connected to tax, insurance, labor protection, training, and productivity policy.

This matters for public administration. A large informal labor force narrows the tax base, weakens social insurance contributions, reduces labor-law enforcement, complicates occupational safety regulation, and makes income support harder during shocks. It also weakens productivity upgrading because informal firms and workers often lack scale, finance, technology, training, and access to formal supply chains.

12.2.4 Social Insurance and the Missing Middle

Social insurance is the bridge between labor markets and the welfare state. Yet coverage remains incomplete. VnEconomy reported that by the end of October 2025 more than 20.4 million people participated in social insurance, citing Vietnam Social Insurance.[4] Compared with the 2025 labor force of 53.5 million, this is only about 38 percent. OECD also reports that only about 40 percent of the 15-60 age group pays social insurance contributions.[3]

The gap is the "missing middle" of Vietnam's labor market. Formal employees in larger firms and the public sector are more likely to be covered. Poor households may receive targeted social assistance. But many informal workers, household-business workers, own-account workers, migrants, and workers in small enterprises fall between contributory insurance and non-contributory assistance.

The 2024 Law on Social Insurance, effective from July 1, 2025, is an important reform. It expands compulsory social insurance coverage to additional categories, including some part-time workers and business household heads under government regulations, and it seeks to make the system more inclusive.[5] But legal expansion does not automatically produce effective coverage. Contributions must be affordable, registration must be simple, enforcement must be credible, employers must comply, and workers must trust that benefits are worth the cost.

12.2.5 Why Formalization Is Difficult

Formalization is hard because informal work is not only a legal category. It is a survival strategy. Many household businesses remain informal because registration costs, tax compliance, accounting requirements, and social insurance contributions feel burdensome relative to income. Some employers avoid formal contracts to reduce labor costs. Some workers accept informality because they need flexibility, immediate cash income, or work near home. Migrants may not know how to access benefits or may not expect to stay long enough to contribute.

The state faces a policy trade-off. Strict enforcement can push firms and workers into compliance, but if done abruptly it can reduce jobs or drive activity further underground. Purely voluntary formalization is often too weak. Vietnam therefore needs a mixed strategy: simplified business registration, lower administrative burdens for micro and small firms, targeted contribution subsidies for low-income workers, stronger inspection of larger employers, digital registration, portable benefits, and visible benefit quality.

12.2.6 Policy Implications

Vietnam should treat women, youth, and informal workers as central to the next development stage, not as marginal social categories. For women, the priorities are childcare, maternity protection, skills access, safe transport, equal opportunity, and social insurance coverage for part-time and informal workers. For youth, the priorities are school-to-work pathways, apprenticeships, vocational status, employer-linked training, and reliable labor-market information. For informal workers, the priorities are formalization incentives, simplified registration, social insurance affordability, and productivity support for household and microenterprises.

The deeper issue is that Vietnam's administrative state has been very effective at mobilizing labor for growth, but less effective at converting all work into protected and productive employment. The next-stage labor-market agenda must therefore combine growth policy with social protection policy. Otherwise Vietnam may continue to produce jobs while leaving too many workers outside the institutions that make work secure.
