13.3 Water, Sanitation, Nutrition, and Welfare

ASEAN comparative lens (2026). Vietnam should be read in ASEAN comparison as ASEAN's most prominent party-led, export-manufacturing upgrading case. Unlike the Philippines' service-remittance profile, Thailand's older industrial-tourism base, Malaysia's resource-industrial federal structure, or Indonesia's archipelagic domestic-market scale, Vietnam's comparative issue is how a socialist party-state manages very open global production networks. Water, sanitation, nutrition, and welfare are often treated as residual social policies after education and health. In Vietnam they should be read as core state-capacity functions. Clean water affects disease, school attendance, women's time use, and child development. Sanitation affects public health and environmental quality. Nutrition affects learning, labor productivity, and intergenerational mobility. Welfare systems determine whether households can survive illness, unemployment, disasters, old age, or price shocks without falling back into poverty. These are not separate policy boxes. They are the everyday infrastructure of human development.

Vietnam's national averages show major progress, but the remaining gaps are administratively difficult. The easy phase is broad expansion. The hard phase is reaching remote villages, ethnic minority communities, informal settlements, disaster-prone areas, migrant-worker neighborhoods, and schools or clinics that lack reliable maintenance finance. The 2025-2026 policy problem is therefore not whether Vietnam recognizes the importance of welfare. It is whether national commitments can be translated into targeted, locally maintained, climate-resilient services.

!Water, sanitation, nutrition, and welfare gaps

The first issue is WASH: water, sanitation, and hygiene. UNICEF's Viet Nam WASH profile states that more than seven million children still lack access to clean water and sanitation at schools; almost half of the population cannot access safely managed drinking water; nearly three million people still practice open defecation; and about 16 million people lack hygienic latrines. These figures are striking because they coexist with Vietnam's image as a successful middle-income performer. They show that infrastructure access is not the same as safe, reliable, and inclusive service quality.

WASH is also a local-government problem. Rural water schemes need financing for maintenance, technical capacity, water-quality monitoring, tariff design, and accountability. School toilets and handwashing facilities require recurrent budgets, not only capital projects. Commune and district authorities must coordinate with education, health, environment, and construction agencies. In rapidly urbanizing areas, sanitation connects to wastewater treatment, land use, drainage, and industrial pollution. In the Mekong Delta and central coastal regions, WASH also connects to climate adaptation because salinity, flooding, drought, and storms can disrupt household water sources.

The second issue is nutrition. Vietnam has reduced poverty and improved child survival, but malnutrition has not disappeared. UNICEF reports that about 1.8 million children under five are stunted, more than one-third of ethnic minority children under five are stunted, more than 200,000 children suffer severe wasting annually, and nearly one in five children and adolescents are overweight or obese. This is a "triple burden" of malnutrition: undernutrition, micronutrient deficiency, and rising overweight or obesity. It reflects uneven development, changing diets, urbanization, food marketing, poverty, ethnicity, geography, and school-meal quality.

Nutrition policy matters for administrative analysis because it converts social inequality into future learning and productivity gaps. A stunted child is more likely to face cognitive and health disadvantages. A child with micronutrient deficiency may struggle in school even if enrolled. An overweight adolescent may face later noncommunicable disease risks. Nutrition therefore links the welfare chapter to the education, health, labor, and productivity chapters. It is also an intersectoral issue: health workers, schools, agriculture, social assistance, food safety agencies, local governments, and disaster-response systems all shape outcomes.

UNICEF's 2025 impact reporting shows the direction of policy experimentation: school nutrition guidance, a new tax on sugary drinks, nutrition education, iron-folic acid supplementation for adolescent girls, obesity-prevention pilots, and treatment support for children with acute malnutrition. These are important because they move beyond a narrow poverty-food model. Vietnam's next nutrition agenda must simultaneously prevent wasting and stunting in vulnerable communities while managing urban diet-related risks.

The third issue is welfare and social assistance. NSO reported that the preliminary 2025 Household Living Standards Survey placed average monthly per-capita household income at about VND 5.9 million and the multidimensional poverty rate at about 1.3 percent. These are strong achievements. But low headline poverty can conceal vulnerability. Informal workers, elderly people without adequate pensions, migrants, ethnic minorities, people with disabilities, households in climate-risk areas, and families facing medical expenses can still experience severe insecurity. A country can nearly eliminate extreme poverty and still have a welfare state that is thin, fragmented, and reactive.

The 2025 social-support figures show the scale and complexity of the system. Local reports cited by NSO recorded about VND 50.3 trillion in support for national devotees and martyrs' relatives, VND 34.2 trillion for social protection beneficiaries under Decree No. 20/2021/ND-CP, VND 4.7 trillion for poor, near-poor, and difficult households not covered by that decree, and VND 3.2 trillion in sudden and unusual local support. The government also distributed rice support for hunger relief and disaster recovery. These programmes show a state that actively intervenes, but they also reveal fragmentation across beneficiary categories, administrative channels, and emergency mechanisms.

The fourth issue is shock responsiveness. Vietnam is exposed to storms, floods, heat, drought, salinity intrusion, landslides, and industrial pollution. Climate shocks can disrupt water systems, damage schools and clinics, reduce household income, increase food insecurity, and force migration. UNICEF notes that more than 20 million people live in areas affected by climate-driven water shortages. In this context, welfare policy cannot be designed only around stable poverty categories. It must also be able to identify newly vulnerable households quickly, deliver cash or in-kind support, restore WASH systems, protect children, and maintain schooling and health services during emergencies.

The fifth issue is targeting and data integration. Vietnam's digital-government agenda creates an opportunity to improve social-policy administration through population databases, household registries, health-insurance systems, poverty lists, school records, and disaster-response platforms. But data integration also raises risks: exclusion errors, outdated household status, weak local verification, privacy concerns, and uneven digital capacity. The administrative goal should be simple but demanding: the right household should receive the right support at the right time, without excessive paperwork or local discretion that produces unequal treatment.

The sixth issue is the welfare status of migrants and informal workers. Industrialization has moved people from rural areas to cities and industrial zones, but social services often remain tied to residence, local fiscal capacity, or formal employment. Migrant workers may face difficulties accessing public schools, childcare, local health services, affordable housing, unemployment support, or contributory social insurance. This connects 13.3 directly to Chapter 12. Labor-market informality is also a welfare problem because it leaves households outside the most reliable social-insurance channels.

Vietnam's welfare challenge should therefore be framed as a transition from poverty reduction to social risk management. The old agenda was to reduce extreme deprivation. The new agenda is to protect capabilities across the life cycle: prenatal nutrition, early childhood development, safe school WASH, adolescent health, worker protection, elderly care, disability support, disaster response, and climate resilience. This requires more than program spending. It requires administrative coordination across ministries, provinces, communes, schools, health facilities, social-insurance offices, and emergency-response institutions.

Policy Implications

Vietnam should strengthen WASH, nutrition, and welfare as an integrated human-development platform. First, rural and school WASH should be funded as a maintenance and service-quality system, not only as infrastructure construction. Second, nutrition policy should combine ethnic-minority stunting reduction, school meals, adolescent nutrition, food-safety enforcement, and obesity prevention. Third, social assistance should move toward better integrated beneficiary databases, clearer entitlement rules, and faster shock-response mechanisms. Fourth, welfare policy should explicitly include migrants, informal workers, children with disabilities, elderly people, and climate-exposed households. Fifth, poverty reduction indicators should be complemented by vulnerability indicators, because a low poverty rate does not mean that households are protected from illness, disasters, or income shocks.
