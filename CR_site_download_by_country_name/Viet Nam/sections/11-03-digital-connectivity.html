11.3 Digital Connectivity, AI Infrastructure, and the Digital Economy

ASEAN comparative lens (2026). Vietnam should be read in ASEAN comparison as ASEAN's most prominent party-led, export-manufacturing upgrading case. Unlike the Philippines' service-remittance profile, Thailand's older industrial-tourism base, Malaysia's resource-industrial federal structure, or Indonesia's archipelagic domestic-market scale, Vietnam's comparative issue is how a socialist party-state manages very open global production networks. Vietnam’s digital connectivity agenda has moved beyond basic internet access. In the earlier stage, the policy objective was to expand mobile coverage, broadband access, and digital public services. The current challenge is more complex: Vietnam must build the digital infrastructure required for an AI-enabled economy, a modern public administration system, high-value manufacturing, digital finance, e-commerce, cybersecurity, cloud computing, and data-driven industrial upgrading. Digital connectivity should therefore be understood not only as a telecommunications issue, but as a foundation for productivity growth, administrative reform, industrial competitiveness, and national technological autonomy.

Vietnam has made rapid progress in digital infrastructure. The government’s Digital Infrastructure Strategy through 2025, with orientation toward 2030, defines digital infrastructure as the foundation for building a modern and smart digital nation. The strategy targets nationwide household access to fiber-optic services by 2025 and 5G coverage for all localities, high-tech parks, centralized information technology parks, research and innovation centers, industrial parks, train stations, seaports, and international airports. ([en.baochinhphu.vn][1]) This is significant because Vietnam’s digital economy is spatially tied to industrial development. If 5G, cloud, and data infrastructure are concentrated only in major cities, digital transformation may reinforce the Hanoi–Ho Chi Minh City development gap. If digital infrastructure reaches industrial zones, ports, logistics corridors, and secondary cities, it can support more balanced industrial upgrading.

The first policy issue is broadband and 5G connectivity. Vietnam’s manufacturing-led economy increasingly depends on fast and reliable connectivity for logistics, automation, smart factories, customs systems, digital payments, e-commerce, remote services, and industrial monitoring. 5G is especially important for advanced manufacturing, Internet of Things applications, autonomous systems, smart ports, smart cities, and real-time data processing. However, connectivity should not be measured only by coverage. The more important development questions are network quality, affordability, cybersecurity, rural access, industrial use cases, and whether small and medium-sized enterprises can adopt digital tools rather than only large firms.

The second policy issue is data centers, cloud infrastructure, and international connectivity. AI and digital services require much more than mobile internet. They require cloud platforms, domestic data centers, content delivery networks, international submarine cables, cybersecurity systems, and reliable electricity. Vietnam’s digital infrastructure strategy includes the development of green data centers, data centers serving AI, additional international submarine fiber-optic cables, hyperscale data centers, and digital hubs by 2030. ([vietnam-briefing.com][2]) These targets show that Vietnam wants to move from being a user of digital services to becoming a regional digital infrastructure node. Yet this ambition depends on power-system reliability, land-use planning, data governance, capital investment, skilled engineers, and regulatory predictability.

The link between digital infrastructure and energy security is particularly important. Data centers, AI computing, cloud services, and semiconductor-related activities require large and stable electricity supply. If Vietnam’s power system remains vulnerable to shortages, grid bottlenecks, or uncertain renewable integration, digital-sector growth may be constrained. Therefore, digital connectivity must be integrated with the energy policy discussed in Section 11.1. AI infrastructure is not immaterial infrastructure; it is electricity-intensive, land-intensive, cooling-intensive, and dependent on secure networks. Digital transformation and power-system reform must therefore be planned together.

The third policy issue is the digital economy. Vietnam’s digital economy has grown rapidly through e-commerce, digital payments, online travel, food delivery, ride-hailing, digital entertainment, fintech, and platform services. The U.S. International Trade Administration reports that Vietnam’s digital economy was expected to reach approximately USD 45 billion by 2025, with projections ranging from USD 90 billion to USD 200 billion by 2030. It also notes that Vietnam’s digital economy accounted for more than 12 percent of GDP in 2023. ([Trade.gov][3]) Other recent estimates place Vietnam’s digital economy at about USD 39 billion in 2025, with e-commerce as the largest component and digital payments continuing to expand rapidly. ([VnEconomy][4]) These figures indicate that digital activity is no longer peripheral. It is becoming a central component of growth, consumption, finance, and business formation.

However, digital-economy expansion creates regulatory challenges. The suspension of Temu and Shein operations in Vietnam in 2024, after government demands that foreign e-commerce platforms register locally, illustrates the state’s effort to regulate cross-border platforms, protect consumers, address counterfeit goods, and ensure tax compliance. ([Reuters][5]) This case shows that Vietnam’s digital economy is more than a liberalized platform market. It is a regulated digital space in which the state seeks to balance innovation, competition, tax collection, consumer protection, domestic business interests, and sovereignty over digital activity.

The fourth policy issue is artificial intelligence. Vietnam has adopted a national AI strategy with the ambition of becoming a center for AI innovation and application in ASEAN and beyond by 2030. ([CSIRO Research][6]) UNESCO also characterizes Vietnam as demonstrating strong political commitment to AI as a strategic driver of economic growth and digital transformation, while also emphasizing the need for responsible AI governance. ([UNESCO][7]) AI is therefore both an industrial opportunity and a governance challenge. It can improve manufacturing, logistics, health care, education, agriculture, public administration, finance, and urban management. But it also raises risks related to data privacy, algorithmic bias, cybersecurity, surveillance, labor displacement, and unequal access to advanced tools.

Vietnam’s AI ecosystem is beginning to attract major corporate investment. FPT announced a USD 200 million AI factory using Nvidia chips and software, with the goal of supporting AI research and applications such as generative AI and autonomous driving. ([Reuters][8]) Qualcomm also launched an AI research and development center in Vietnam in 2025, focusing on generative AI, agentic AI, smartphones, personal computers, extended reality, automotive systems, and IoT applications. ([Reuters][9]) These developments suggest that Vietnam is trying to position itself not only as a manufacturing base but also as a site for AI application, engineering, and digital talent development.

Nevertheless, Vietnam’s AI ambitions face several constraints. The first is computing capacity. Advanced AI requires GPUs, cloud infrastructure, high-performance data centers, and reliable electricity. The second is data governance. AI systems require high-quality, interoperable, legally usable data, especially in public administration, health care, education, finance, and transport. The third is talent. Vietnam has a large young workforce and strong engineering potential, but advanced AI development requires specialized researchers, data scientists, machine-learning engineers, cybersecurity specialists, and regulatory experts. The fourth is commercialization. Research capacity must be connected to firms, public-sector use cases, and exportable digital products. Without these links, AI strategy may remain aspirational.

The fifth policy issue is digital government. Vietnam’s digital transformation agenda is strongly connected to administrative modernization. Digital public services, interoperable databases, digital identification, electronic payments, online licensing, data-sharing platforms, and AI-enabled decision support can reduce bureaucratic delay, improve service access, and strengthen transparency. A recent AI-centered digital government blueprint aims to establish by 2030 a smart digital government powered by big data and AI, with interconnected national platforms, cybersecurity, personal data protection, digital sovereignty, and AI-supported public administration. ([vietnamnews.vn][10]) This direction is important because digital government is not only about convenience. It can reshape the relationship between citizens, firms, and the administrative state.

Yet digital government also creates institutional risks. Data systems can become fragmented across ministries and provinces. Digital services may exclude elderly, rural, poor, or less digitally literate citizens. AI-enabled administration may increase efficiency but reduce explainability if decisions become opaque. Digital identity systems can improve service delivery but also raise privacy and surveillance concerns. The central governance question is therefore whether Vietnam can build a digital state that is efficient, secure, interoperable, accountable, and inclusive.

The sixth policy issue is the connection between digital infrastructure and industrial upgrading. Vietnam’s current industrial model remains heavily export-oriented and FDI-led. Digital connectivity can help domestic firms move beyond low-value assembly by improving quality control, inventory management, logistics, e-commerce access, digital marketing, supplier certification, financial access, and productivity measurement. AI and cloud services can support small and medium-sized enterprises, but only if adoption costs are manageable and skills are available. Otherwise, digital transformation may widen the gap between large foreign-invested firms and domestic SMEs.

Digital connectivity also affects Vietnam’s role in semiconductor and high-tech supply chains. The elevation of U.S.–Vietnam relations to a Comprehensive Strategic Partnership in 2023 included cooperation in science, technology, innovation, and semiconductor ecosystem development. Vietnam’s digital infrastructure, AI talent, data centers, engineering education, and power reliability will influence whether the country can move into higher-value segments of electronics and semiconductor-related services. Digital infrastructure is therefore part of economic diplomacy and supply-chain strategy, not only domestic ICT policy.

Cybersecurity is the seventh policy issue. As Vietnam digitizes banking, public administration, logistics, energy, ports, health systems, education, and industrial operations, cyber risk increases. Threats include ransomware, online fraud, data theft, critical infrastructure attacks, platform manipulation, and cross-border cybercrime. Cybersecurity must therefore be treated as an enabling condition for digital transformation. Weak cybersecurity can reduce public trust, discourage investment, expose sensitive state and business data, and undermine digital government. Strong cybersecurity, however, must be balanced with privacy, innovation, and legal predictability.

From an administrative perspective, digital connectivity requires coordination across telecommunications regulators, cybersecurity agencies, data-governance authorities, education ministries, industrial-policy agencies, local governments, public-service providers, state-owned enterprises, private technology firms, foreign investors, and international partners. The central challenge lies beyond infrastructure deployment. It is the creation of a digital governance ecosystem: rules for data, standards for interoperability, incentives for investment, protection for consumers, cybersecurity capacity, and mechanisms for public-sector digital adoption.

Vietnam’s digital transition should therefore be interpreted in three layers. The first layer is connectivity infrastructure: broadband, 5G, submarine cables, data centers, cloud, and cybersecurity. The second layer is digital economic activity: e-commerce, fintech, platform services, digital payments, logistics technology, and enterprise digitalization. The third layer is AI-enabled transformation: data-driven public administration, industrial automation, smart logistics, advanced manufacturing, digital health, education technology, and AI-based services. The policy challenge is to connect these layers. Infrastructure without firms and skills produces underused assets. Digital platforms without regulation produce consumer and tax risks. AI without data governance and compute capacity remains rhetorical. Digital government without accountability may increase control without improving service quality.

Policy Implications

Vietnam should treat digital connectivity as strategic infrastructure for the next stage of development. First, broadband and 5G expansion should be linked to industrial zones, ports, logistics corridors, universities, innovation centers, and secondary cities, not only major urban consumer markets. Second, data centers and cloud infrastructure should be developed together with energy security, green standards, cybersecurity, and submarine-cable resilience. Third, AI policy should focus on practical use cases in manufacturing, logistics, health, education, agriculture, and public administration, while also building responsible AI governance. Fourth, digital-economy regulation should balance innovation with tax compliance, consumer protection, competition policy, and domestic SME development. Fifth, digital government reform should prioritize interoperability, data quality, citizen inclusion, privacy, and administrative accountability.

The core issue is not whether Vietnam can digitize rapidly. It already is. The more important question is whether digital transformation will raise productivity, strengthen domestic firms, improve public administration, and support higher-value industrial upgrading. If Vietnam builds only platform consumption and low-level digital services, digital growth may remain shallow. If it builds secure connectivity, cloud and AI infrastructure, skilled human capital, strong domestic technology firms, and accountable digital government, digital transformation can become a major pathway toward higher-income status.
