9.1 GDP Scale, Growth, and Income Level

Nigeria's macroeconomic puzzle is not that the economy is small. It is that continental scale has not produced a comparably high or steadily rising command over resources per person. Three measures that are often collapsed into a single growth narrative therefore need to be kept separate. Nominal GDP measures the money value of production and helps describe the size of markets and some fiscal denominators. Real GDP growth removes domestic price change and shows whether the volume of output is expanding. Real output per person asks the more demanding question: whether production is growing faster than the population that must live from it. None of the three directly measures household income distribution, consumption, public-service quality, or poverty. A credible account of Nigerian growth must move among these measures without allowing one to stand in for the others.

A larger statistical economy is not an overnight increase in welfare

The first complication is measurement. In 2025 the National Bureau of Statistics (NBS) moved the national-accounts base year from 2010 to 2019 and expanded the production boundary and source information to reflect activities whose economic weight had changed. On the new basis, nominal GDP was estimated at ₦205.09 trillion in 2019—41.7 percent above the estimate produced with the previous base—and ₦372.82 trillion in 2024. Real growth in the rebased series was 3.38 percent in 2024. The full-year release for 2025 subsequently reported nominal GDP of approximately ₦441.5 trillion and real growth of 3.87 percent; the first-quarter 2026 release recorded year-on-year real growth of 3.89 percent (National Bureau of Statistics, 2025; National Bureau of Statistics, 2026a).

Rebasing improves the map of the economy; it does not create the newly measured output in the year the revision is published. Digital and financial services, real estate, informal production, and newer data sources may receive more appropriate weights, while historical growth rates and sector shares can change because the benchmark and methods changed. Jerven's study of national-account revisions in Sub-Saharan Africa shows why large revisions can reveal genuine undermeasurement while also reducing comparability across vintages, countries, and policy periods (Jerven, 2013). Nigeria's 2025 revision should thus change ratios whose denominator is GDP—revenue, debt, credit, health spending, or trade openness—without being interpreted as an equivalent improvement in tax administration, debt-service capacity, financial depth, or household welfare. For policy evaluation, the NBS and agencies using its data need published bridge tables that show the old and new series, coverage changes, deflators, and the point at which quarterly estimates become revised annual accounts.

Dollar GDP records exchange-rate translation as well as production

The second complication is currency conversion. World Bank Open Data reports Nigerian GDP of about US$290.79 billion and GDP per capita of US$1,224 in 2025, alongside real GDP growth of about 4.0 percent (World Bank Open Data, 2026). The simultaneous rise in real output and much lower dollar valuation than at the end of the 2010s is not contradictory. Current-dollar GDP converts a nominal naira aggregate at the exchange rate applicable to the series. A large naira depreciation can reduce the dollar value of GDP and GDP per capita even when the domestic volume of production is rising; domestic inflation can push nominal naira GDP in the opposite direction. Changes in population estimates and the national-accounts benchmark add further revisions. Current-dollar GDP is relevant to the foreign-currency scale of imports, external debt, and international market comparisons, but it is a poor stand-alone measure of changes in Nigerian living standards.

This is an exchange-rate-sensitive current-dollar series, not real purchasing power or median household income. Its post-2014 decline reflects the interaction of naira depreciation, nominal output, population growth, and data revisions; it should not be read as a one-for-one change in domestic real production.

The figure makes this distinction visible. It traces the World Development Indicators series for GDP per capita in current US dollars through 2024, not real purchasing power or the median Nigerian's income (World Bank, 2025a). The steep decline after the mid-2010s chiefly records the interaction of exchange-rate translation, nominal output, population growth, and data revisions. It should not be read as a one-for-one fall in real domestic production. Conversely, a future appreciation of the naira could lift current-dollar GDP without an equivalent productivity gain. This is why the government's aspiration for a trillion-dollar economy cannot be evaluated by a dollar threshold alone: the exchange rate and domestic price level can move the headline even when the underlying capacity to produce goods, services, jobs, and public revenue changes much less.

Purchasing-power-parity (PPP) measures answer a different comparative question by valuing output at a common price structure. The May 2025 Nigeria Development Update placed 2023 GDP per capita at US$6,207 in current international dollars, ranking Nigeria 146th among 191 economies with data and at about 30 percent of Botswana's level, the highest in continental Africa on that measure (World Bank, 2025). PPP is more suitable than market-exchange-rate conversion for comparing the volume of locally purchased goods and services, but it is still an average production measure. It neither shows distribution within Nigeria nor converts directly into the foreign currency available for machinery, medicine, debt service, or other imports. The large gap between current-dollar and PPP values is therefore not a choice between a “correct” and an “incorrect” GDP; it identifies two different constraints—domestic purchasing power and external command over resources.

Aggregate recovery remains too slow to transform income per person

The third complication is demographic arithmetic. A growth rate near 4 percent can sound rapid in a slow-growing high-income economy, but Nigeria's population is expanding by roughly 2 to 3 percent a year. The October 2025 Nigeria Development Update estimated real GDP per capita growth at only 1.6 percent in 2025 even though aggregate real GDP was projected to grow by more than 4 percent (World Bank, 2025). The IMF's 2025 baseline similarly put medium-term aggregate growth near 3.5 percent, equivalent to about 1.3 percent per person—too little for rapid poverty reduction (International Monetary Fund, 2025). The latest NBS releases show that the post-2023 economy has avoided recession and gained some momentum; they do not yet establish a break from this low per-capita-growth regime.

This distinction explains why macroeconomic stabilization and household experience can diverge. Removing fuel-price distortions, ending central-bank deficit financing, and improving foreign-exchange price discovery can reduce fiscal and external imbalances, but the transition also changes transport, food, credit, and imported-input costs. The April 2026 Nigeria Development Update judged that stabilization had progressed and growth remained resilient, largely through services, while household incomes had not fully recovered and poverty remained high (World Bank Group, 2026). That assessment is not evidence that reform cannot work. It shows that stabilization is an enabling condition whose distributive and productive transmission must be demonstrated: firms must invest and expand, workers must move into more productive activities, real earnings must rise, and public revenue gains must become reliable services or well-targeted protection.

The mechanism behind Nigeria's weak conversion of scale into income is cumulative. Rapid population growth dilutes modest output gains; high and volatile inflation shortens investment horizons and erodes real earnings; exchange-rate instability raises the naira cost of imported capital and intermediate goods; insecurity and unreliable power reduce productivity; and a large share of employment remains in low-productivity or vulnerable activities. These constraints also interact. For example, a manufacturer facing unreliable electricity and expensive imported inputs cannot respond to a more competitive exchange rate as readily as a firm with stable power, logistics, credit, and access to export markets. The result is not simply “growth without inclusion” as an outcome label, but a weak transmission chain from macroeconomic adjustment to productivity, employment, and household purchasing power. The subsequent sections examine the price, sectoral, and structural links in that chain.

Policy should target the conversion of scale, not the headline alone

The appropriate policy dashboard follows directly from this diagnosis. Nigeria should retain real GDP and nominal GDP for national accounting, but judge economic progress with a compact set of non-interchangeable outcomes: real GDP per capita; labor productivity by sector; employment and real earnings in activities capable of sustained productivity growth; household consumption and poverty measured with transparent price deflators; and the revenue actually available for public investment and social protection. Each measure needs a published vintage, denominator, and revision history. A nominal naira or dollar target can remain a communication device, but it should not determine whether a policy is judged successful.

The same logic changes the design of growth policy. In the 2025 medium-term projections, growth near 3.5 to 4 percent left little margin above population growth (International Monetary Fund, 2025; World Bank, 2025). Policy must therefore raise both the rate and the productivity content of expansion. That means protecting macroeconomic credibility while removing identifiable constraints on production—power reliability, transport and port costs, security, access to finance, skills, and predictable regulation—and testing whether reforms generate additional output per worker rather than only higher asset values or nominal turnover. Social protection is not separate from this strategy when high food and transport costs force households to withdraw children from school, reduce nutrition, or sell productive assets; those responses can lower the human and physical capital on which later growth depends.

Nigeria's economic size remains a major advantage: it supports a large internal market, a diversified service base, fiscal potential, and regional influence. Yet scale becomes development only when national accounting improvements are matched by real per-person gains and when those gains reach households through productive work and public capability. The analytical baseline for the rest of this chapter is therefore deliberately demanding: positive aggregate growth is evidence of recovery, not by itself evidence of transformed living standards.
