10.2 Oil, Gas, Agriculture, Services, and Regional Trade

Nigeria's production base is broader than its export basket. Agriculture, commerce, telecommunications, finance, entertainment, and other services account for much of output and employment, yet hydrocarbons still determine most recorded merchandise earnings. That mismatch is the central analytical problem. Export diversification is not achieved when a new petroleum product is added to the customs schedule or when a few agricultural commodities have a strong quarter. It requires a wider set of firms and products to earn foreign exchange across destinations, retain domestic value added, and survive a fall in oil prices. Regional trade matters because West African markets can provide a first export step for Nigerian firms, but official statistics capture that channel imperfectly and border policy can either formalize or displace it.

Petroleum processing changes the product mix more quickly than the risk structure

The latest merchandise accounts show genuine change inside a still-concentrated basket. In the fourth quarter of 2025, crude oil supplied 51.17 percent of exports and other petroleum products another 32.25 percent. Agricultural exports were valued at ₦1.32 trillion, raw materials at ₦1.19 trillion, and manufactured goods at ₦423.43 billion, compared with total exports of ₦18.96 trillion (National Bureau of Statistics, 2026b). Domestic refining therefore broadened petroleum output and reduced part of the fuel-import burden discussed in Section 10.1, but crude and processed petroleum together still supplied more than four-fifths of quarterly merchandise exports.

This distinction separates vertical upgrading from sectoral diversification. Refining crude domestically can add processing, logistics, engineering, tax, and supplier activity. It can also reduce the external cost of importing fuel. Yet crude, gas, and refined products remain exposed to related hydrocarbon prices, feedstock conditions, pipeline security, and carbon-transition risk. A refinery can improve domestic value added without making the export portfolio resilient to a common oil shock. The appropriate policy question is therefore not whether petroleum processing counts as industrialization—it does—but whether its supplier linkages, skills, and infrastructure lower the cost of entry for other tradable sectors.

UNCTAD's product-concentration index makes the remaining distance visible. The index approaches one when a small number of product groups dominate exports and zero when exports are distributed more evenly. Nigeria's score fell from 0.761 in 2010 to 0.619 in 2025, with most of the decline occurring after 2023. That is meaningful movement, consistent with rising refined-product and some non-oil exports. Nigeria nevertheless remained much more concentrated in 2025 than Ghana (0.487), Kenya (0.186), or South Africa (0.153) under the same definition (UN Trade and Development, 2026a).

A lower score indicates a merchandise basket distributed more evenly across product groups. The index does not measure destination diversity, exporting firms, domestic value added, technological complexity, or services exports, and price changes can move the score without equivalent capability growth.

The comparison should be read as a warning, not a league table. The index measures how merchandise value is distributed across product groups; it does not show the number or survival of exporting firms, domestic content, technological complexity, destination concentration, or services exports. Oil-price changes can also move the index without a comparable change in productive capability. The 2024–2025 decline is thus encouraging but insufficient evidence of structural transformation. A stronger test would ask whether non-petroleum products gain share through sustained volume and firm growth rather than through a temporary change in hydrocarbon prices.

Agricultural potential is constrained between the farm and the border

Agriculture offers a different diversification route because cocoa, sesame, cashew, hibiscus, horticulture, leather, and processed foods can connect rural production to foreign demand. The binding problem is not simply insufficient output. Exportable consistency depends on seed and extension systems, farm security, aggregation, storage, cold chains, testing laboratories, traceability, packaging, inland transport, trade finance, and predictable border rules. A failure in any one link can turn an apparent comparative advantage at the farm gate into an unreliable shipment at the port.

Recent peer-reviewed work using Nigerian quarterly data from 2002 to 2023 associates agricultural-export diversification with greater resilience to external shocks, while also finding that adverse global shocks persist where institutions and value-chain systems are weak (Attah et al., 2025). That finding is best interpreted as mechanism-consistent evidence rather than proof that any single reform will cause export growth: the time-series design cannot observe every firm-level capability or policy reversal. Its useful implication is that commodity promotion without contract enforcement, standards capacity, infrastructure, and policy stability is unlikely to produce durable diversification.

The policy unit should consequently be an export corridor and value chain, not a commodity slogan. For each priority product, government should identify the importing market and its sanitary or technical standard, map the states and firms that can supply it, publish rejection and clearance data, and finance the specific public goods that private producers cannot coordinate alone. Support should be conditional on export-volume growth, quality compliance, new-firm entry, domestic processing, and repayment or sunset rules. Export bans and abrupt licensing changes are especially damaging because they make buyers and processors discount the credibility of future Nigerian supply.

Services are economically large but externally under-realized and under-measured

Nigeria's large domestic services economy does not yet translate into a comparable recorded export position. UNCTAD reports services exports of US$4.565 billion and services imports of US$17.926 billion in 2024; the IMF's balance-of-payments table similarly records a large services deficit in 2025 (UN Trade and Development, 2025a; International Monetary Fund, 2026a). This is not evidence that Nigerian finance, software, film, music, professional services, or platform firms lack regional reach. It shows that the balance of payments records cross-border receipts and payments, not the full social visibility or domestic scale of an industry.

Measurement is particularly difficult for digitally delivered, platform-mediated, and mode-of-supply services. Some transactions are paid through foreign accounts, bundled with intellectual property or advertising, supplied through affiliates, or omitted from detailed bilateral statistics. UNCTAD notes that services trade is underrepresented in many developing-country statistical systems and that balance-of-payments, foreign-affiliate, value-added, and digital-trade data answer different questions (UN Trade and Development, 2025b). Nigeria should therefore improve enterprise surveys and payment classifications before treating a low recorded category as zero activity or designing incentives around an incomplete baseline.

Services also function as inputs to goods exports. Comparative evidence from 36 Sub-Saharan African countries finds a positive association between services trade and participation in global value chains, with effects varying by service category and position in the chain (Efogo, 2020). For Nigeria, reliable logistics, finance, telecommunications, engineering, certification, and business services can raise the competitiveness of agriculture and manufacturing even when those services are not separately visible in the exported good's customs value. The policy objective should be both direct services exports and the “servicification” of other exports.

Regional trade is a capability ladder whose lower rungs are partly invisible

ECOWAS markets can be easier first destinations than Europe, Asia, or North America because distances are shorter, consumer preferences overlap, and Nigerian firms may be competitive in processed food, chemicals, footwear, household goods, finance, entertainment, and business services. Historical firm-level evidence assembled for the World Bank found that more than one-third of surveyed Nigerian exporters identified ECOWAS countries as their primary market, and that regional exporters occupied an intermediate position between domestic-only firms and larger global exporters. The study's data are old and not nationally weighted, so they cannot describe the present share of firms. They nevertheless support a durable mechanism: a regional market can allow firms to learn exporting before meeting the scale and standards of global markets (von Uexkull & Shui, 2014).

Official regional shares should not be read literally. The same World Bank study documented large gaps between recorded and estimated non-oil trade with Cameroon and explained how re-export, smuggling, small consignments, and weak border records obscure food and daily-consumption trade (von Uexkull & Shui, 2014). Informality is not merely a statistical nuisance. It often reflects the cost and uncertainty of certificates of origin, inspections, unofficial payments, foreign-exchange restrictions, road insecurity, and divergent national implementation of nominally common rules. Closing a border can therefore shift routes and prices without eliminating the underlying demand.

This has two policy implications. First, formalization should reduce the fixed cost of legality through interoperable customs records, risk-based inspection, simplified regimes for small traders, transparent origin certification, and monitored border-corridor performance. Second, the success indicator should not be the number of seizures or the decline of imports. It should be lower clearance time and variance, more registered small traders and exporting firms, higher recorded regional trade, fewer unofficial payments, and stable availability of essential inputs and food. Abrupt restrictions that raise the legal cost above the informal premium can worsen both data quality and state credibility.

Nigeria's diversification strategy should therefore link three different ladders. Petroleum processing should be judged by domestic supplier development and whether shared infrastructure benefits non-oil tradables. Agricultural corridors should be judged by verified standards compliance, processing, and repeat export orders. Services and regional trade should be judged by better measurement, firm entry, and progression from domestic to regional and global markets. The evidence does not support a claim that Nigeria has already escaped oil dependence. It supports a more bounded conclusion: the product mix began to widen in 2024–2025, but concentration remains high, and durable diversification will depend on whether institutions convert sectoral potential into repeatable firm-level exporting capability.
