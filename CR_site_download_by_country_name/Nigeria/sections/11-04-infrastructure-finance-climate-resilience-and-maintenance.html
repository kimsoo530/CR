11.4 Infrastructure Finance, Climate Resilience, and Maintenance

Nigeria's infrastructure deficit encourages a search for more capital, but financing is only one stage in the life of an asset. A road, power line, drainage system, port, broadband network, or public building creates value only if the project is selected against alternatives, completed, operated, maintained, adapted to changing hazards, and replaced at the appropriate time. Borrowing or attracting a private partner can move the initial cost off the current capital budget; it cannot eliminate the economic cost, foreign-exchange exposure, user-charge obligation, contingent liability, or maintenance requirement. The central policy question is therefore not simply how Nigeria can finance more projects, but how it can finance reliable services over the whole asset life without transferring hidden risks to future budgets and users.

Fiscal scarcity makes project selection more important, not less

Nigeria's low public revenue and high interest burden constrain capital spending and the recurrent budgets that keep assets operational. The IMF's 2025 Article IV assessment emphasizes that interest spending absorbs fiscal space needed for infrastructure and human development and that credible budgeting and transparent implementation are essential to turn additional revenue into effective investment (International Monetary Fund, 2025). Under this condition, a long list of politically attractive projects is not an investment strategy. Spreading appropriations thinly across many sites increases the probability that contractors mobilize without predictable cash flow, work is suspended, completed components deteriorate, and costs are revised upward before benefits begin.

This failure mechanism is not new. Case studies of abandoned Nigerian water and irrigation projects identified inadequate funding, price variation, contract conditions, corruption, and project-management problems as interacting causes (Sonuga, Aliboh, & Oloke, 2002). The study is more than two decades old and cannot establish the current frequency of abandonment, but the mechanism remains relevant under inflation and exchange-rate volatility: an unrealistic cost estimate combined with annual incremental funding converts macroeconomic shocks into delay, renegotiation, and deteriorating partially built assets.

Project selection should therefore begin with a service gap and a realistic counterfactual. Appraisal must compare rehabilitation, demand management, maintenance, regulatory change, and smaller modular investments with a new megaproject. It should disclose total capital and operating cost, procurement and land schedule, foreign-currency content, climate and security exposure, demand uncertainty, distributional effects, and the public entity that will pay for operation. Independent technical review should occur before political commitment makes cancellation impossible. A project that cannot identify a funded operator and maintenance standard should not receive construction approval.

The table is a governance sequence, not a universal scoring formula. Different assets require different technical metrics, but every project should pass through the same logic: identify the service, compare options, expose risks, assign them to an actor able to manage them, fund operation, and compare delivered results with the business case.

A PPP reallocates risk only when contracts and institutions can enforce the allocation

Public-private partnerships can mobilize expertise and long-term capital and can link payment to asset availability or service quality. Nigeria's Infrastructure Concession Regulatory Commission (ICRC) requires project identification, an Outline Business Case, procurement, a Full Business Case, implementation, monitoring, maturity, and handover. The process also calls for consultation with the finance ministry where a proposal creates a contingent liability (Infrastructure Concession Regulatory Commission, n.d.). This architecture correctly recognizes that government purchases a service rather than merely receives private money.

The difficult issue is credible risk allocation. A private operator can manage construction methods, routine maintenance, and some operating risks, but it cannot efficiently bear every exchange-rate shock, politically determined tariff freeze, land delay, force majeure, or demand collapse. Assigning an uncontrollable risk to the private side may simply raise the bid price, prevent financial close, or generate later renegotiation. Retaining too much risk through guarantees, minimum-revenue commitments, foreign-exchange protection, termination payments, or availability charges can make a PPP fiscally similar to debt while keeping the obligation less visible.

Comparative research on Nigeria and South Africa links PPP performance to financial-market depth, stable policy, legal credibility, transparent procurement, political support, stakeholder engagement, and public-sector capacity rather than to the contractual label alone (Deinde-Adedeji et al., 2024). For Nigeria, that finding implies that the PPP pipeline should not be used as a count of financed infrastructure. Government should publish the project stage, business-case summary, procurement outcome, signed contract, government support, currency and demand assumptions, performance reports, renegotiations, and termination or handback provisions. The finance ministry should report expected annual payments and probability-weighted contingent liabilities with debt and budget documents.

Affordability also determines legitimacy. A toll road, electricity concession, water service, or broadband project may be financially viable at a tariff that excludes poorer users or redirects traffic to unsafe alternatives. Subsidies can be justified where a service creates public or distributional benefits, but they should be explicit, targeted, budgeted, and reviewed. Hidden cross-subsidies and ad hoc tariff freezes weaken the revenue stream on which maintenance and private finance depend. The relevant test is value for money after financing cost, risk transfer, user charges, public support, and contract-management capacity—not whether initial capital appears in the private column.

Climate risk is already a fiscal and service-delivery risk

The 2022 floods demonstrate that resilience cannot remain a future scenario. A World Bank emergency-preparedness review estimates that the event affected about 3.2 million people, displaced 1.4 million, caused more than 600 deaths, and produced direct damages with a median estimate of US$6.7 billion, or about 1.6 percent of GDP. Its risk analysis estimates average annual flood damage to physical assets at US$693 million and a 1 percent annual probability that catastrophic flood damage exceeds US$10 billion—equivalent to 22.3 percent of public expenditure in the study's baseline (World Bank, 2025f). These are model- and assessment-based estimates with uncertainty, but they show why post-disaster reconstruction can crowd out planned development.

Climate exposure varies by place and asset. Coastal cities face sea-level rise, erosion, storm surge, subsidence, drainage blockage, and intense rainfall. Northern infrastructure faces heat, drought, water scarcity, and land degradation. Roads, rail, dams, power systems, telecom sites, schools, and health facilities have different failure thresholds and interdependencies. A flooded substation can interrupt water pumping and digital communications; a failed bridge can isolate markets and emergency services. Resilience therefore means maintaining an essential service under stress and recovering within a specified time, not making every structure equally resistant to every hazard.

Nigeria's Climate Change Act 2021 establishes a national council, action planning, carbon budgeting, and climate duties for public and private entities (Federal Republic of Nigeria, 2021). The legal framework creates authority for mainstreaming, but mainstreaming becomes real only when project appraisal, design standards, procurement, maintenance, and fiscal reporting incorporate hazard information. The 2026 Nigeria Country Climate and Development Report similarly frames resilient and low-emission development as an economy-wide institutional and financing task rather than an environmental-sector project list (World Bank, 2026b). An IMF assessment estimates annual adaptation needs rising toward roughly 3 percent of 2020 GDP by 2050 across agriculture, water, health, and transport and projected annual sea-level-rise costs of 0.1-0.4 percent of GDP (International Monetary Fund African Department, 2025). The estimates are scenario-dependent, but their order of magnitude makes prioritization and external concessional finance essential.

Every major appraisal should therefore include a geospatial hazard screen, asset vulnerability and criticality assessment, design alternatives under multiple climate scenarios, expected service interruption, residual risk, and a funded maintenance and emergency plan. The relevant comparison is lifecycle value: a resilient design may cost more initially but reduce expected damage, downtime, and reconstruction. Conversely, an expensive “climate” label is not justified when basic drainage maintenance, land-use enforcement, early warning, or a less exposed location would produce greater protection.

Maintenance is a budget institution and an information system

Maintenance failures are frequently attributed to insufficient funding, but information and incentives determine how scarce funds are used. Government needs a verified asset register that identifies location, owner, age, condition, replacement value, criticality, maintenance standard, contractor, and next inspection. Without it, ministries cannot calculate the liability embedded in existing infrastructure or distinguish preventive work from politically connected emergency repair. Capital budgets then add assets faster than recurrent systems can absorb them.

The budget should link each new asset to a multi-year operating and maintenance envelope and should protect high-value preventive tasks without creating unaccountable earmarks. Condition-based and performance-based contracts can pay for passability, uptime, water quality, energy availability, drainage capacity, or building safety rather than inputs alone. Their success still depends on independent measurement, auditable baselines, credible sanctions, and competition. Community reporting can identify localized failures, but it cannot replace engineering inspection or transfer statutory responsibility to residents.

Maintenance is also distributional. Wealthier firms and households can buy generators, water storage, private transport, cloud backup, or insurance when public systems fail. Poorer users face longer travel, lost work, spoiled food, interrupted schooling, unsafe water, and uncompensated disaster loss. Prioritization should therefore combine asset criticality with who depends on the service and who has alternatives. A low-cost feeder road, clinic power system, culvert, or drainage channel may have a higher welfare return than a visible expansion of a flagship facility.

Nigeria needs a single lifecycle accountability record for each major asset

The four preceding sections point to the same administrative reform. Electricity, logistics, digital services, and climate-resilient assets all fail at organizational interfaces and are often judged by installed stock rather than delivered service. Nigeria should create a lifecycle record for each major public or PPP asset, linked to procurement and budget systems but readable as a public service contract. It should identify the service objective, business-case baseline, capital and financing terms, public guarantees, construction milestones, climate assumptions, operator, maintenance schedule, condition, uptime or throughput, safety and environmental performance, user charges and subsidies, grievances, and ex-post evaluation.

Such a record would not eliminate political bargaining or uncertainty. It would make the bargaining more disciplined by showing which assumptions changed, who controls the corrective instrument, and what future obligation has already been created. Annual portfolio review could then stop or redesign weak projects before sunk-cost logic dominates, shift funds toward maintenance and completion, and aggregate currency, guarantee, climate, and operating risks across sectors. Climate finance and development-partner support would be most valuable when they strengthen this domestic pipeline—project preparation, hazard data, standards, procurement, monitoring, and maintenance—rather than funding stand-alone assets that cannot be sustained.

The chapter's overall implication is that Nigeria's infrastructure challenge is institutional as well as financial. More capital is necessary, but capital produces development only through reliable electricity, predictable journeys, usable and safe digital services, and assets that continue operating through fiscal and climate shocks. Financing, regulation, federal coordination, data, maintenance, and redress are not supporting details around construction. They are the mechanisms that determine whether an infrastructure promise becomes a public service.
