11.3 Digital Connectivity, Broadband, and Cyber-Institutional Capacity

Nigeria's mobile networks, digital payments, software firms, fintech platforms, and creative industries demonstrate that private actors can scale digital services under difficult infrastructure conditions. Yet a country can have a dynamic digital market without possessing a capable digital state. Public digital capacity requires more than connectivity: people must be able to afford and use a service; agencies must know which record is authoritative; systems must exchange only necessary data under a lawful purpose; users must be able to correct errors and appeal exclusion; and critical services must withstand cyber incidents. The central issue is therefore whether connectivity is being converted into trustworthy, inclusive, and resilient public infrastructure.

Network reach does not equal meaningful use

World Development Indicators show a long expansion in internet use, from 11.5 percent of Nigeria's population in 2010 to 41.2 percent in 2024. Mobile subscriptions rose much faster and were close to one subscription per person in 2022-2023 before the reported series fell to 70.8 per 100 people in 2024. The two indicators are not directly comparable: internet use refers to people, whereas mobile subscriptions count active SIMs, including multiple subscriptions held by one person. The 2024 subscription break should be read with changes in subscriber verification and administrative records, not as proof that almost three in ten Nigerians suddenly lost a handset. Neither measure reveals whether a user has a smartphone, affordable data, reliable electricity, adequate speed, digital skills, or confidence in online services.

Internet use reached 41.2 percent of the population in 2024, while mobile subscriptions are SIM counts rather than unique people. The abrupt change in subscriptions per 100 people in 2024 should be read with subscriber-record and denominator changes, not as a one-year collapse in device access. Neither series measures affordability, skills, service quality, or the safe use of digital public services.

The gap between coverage and meaningful use is socially structured. GSMA survey evidence for 2022 found that women were 38 percent less likely than men to use mobile internet in Nigeria. Although reported mobile ownership was high for both men and women, only 25 percent of men and 13 percent of women used at least three mobile-internet applications daily (GSMA, 2024). This is survey-based evidence from a particular year, not a current census, but it identifies the mechanism that national subscription totals miss: device cost, data affordability, literacy, perceived relevance, safety, and control over household resources shape whether nominal coverage becomes regular use.

The policy implication is to evaluate connectivity as a service ladder. Coverage maps and subscriptions remain useful, but they should be joined by median and lower-decile speed, outage time, data cost relative to income, smartphone access, regular and diverse use, and gender, income, disability, age, rural, and state-level gaps. Infrastructure subsidies should be conditioned on independently verified coverage and quality in underserved locations. Public services should be designed for low bandwidth, small screens, local-language support, assistive technology, and intermittent connectivity, with an assisted or offline channel when denial of access would affect a legal entitlement.

Digital identity creates value only through reliable authentication and redress

Nigeria's National Identification Number (NIN) has expanded rapidly. The National Identity Management Commission reported 121,404,601 unique records at 30 June 2025, of which 52.9 million were recorded as female and 68.5 million as male (National Identity Management Commission, 2025). World Bank reporting traces enrolment from 36.9 million at project launch to 111.4 million by November 2024 and notes efforts to remove documentation barriers for internally displaced people and refugees and to expand child and female enrolment (World Bank, 2025b). These are important gains in foundational coverage. They are not equivalent to universal identity, successful authentication, or access to a particular benefit.

An identity system produces public value when an eligible person can enrol, authenticate, update a record, correct an error, recover credentials, and complete a service without excessive travel, payment, or discretion. Linking the NIN to banking, telecommunications, welfare, tax, health, education, and civil registration can reduce duplicate records and make transactions more convenient. The same linkage can also propagate an error across systems or turn failure to authenticate into simultaneous exclusion from multiple services. Coverage must therefore be paired with authentication success by channel and population group, correction time, rejected or duplicate records, enrolment quality, grievance resolution, and the share of public programs that can verify identity without retaining unnecessary biometric data.

Research conducted before passage of the Nigeria Data Protection Act 2023 remains useful for identifying these design risks. Eke and colleagues (2022) found that privacy, security, human-rights, ethical, and sociocultural concerns were central to public evaluations of the national ID program. Okunoye (2022) distinguishes public trust from system trustworthiness and links acceptance to limits on data sharing, surveillance risk, and meaningful legal safeguards. The 2023 Act addressed an important legislative gap by establishing data-processing principles, data-subject rights, and the Nigeria Data Protection Commission (Federal Republic of Nigeria, 2023). But a statute does not automatically repair legacy databases, specify every inter-agency purpose, or give a person an effective remedy when an automated match fails.

Nigeria should therefore treat NIN as a credential and public trust infrastructure, not as a universal database key that authorizes unrestricted data pooling. Each use needs a defined legal purpose, minimum necessary attributes, retention period, accountable controller, access log, correction route, and independent audit. Authentication can often answer a limited question—such as whether a person is unique, above an age threshold, or enrolled in a program—without transferring the full identity record. Federated or tokenized verification can reduce concentration and breach risk, but technical architecture is not a substitute for legal limits and accessible redress.

Interoperability is an institutional agreement before it is an API

Nigeria already has major functional identifiers and platforms, including NIN, Bank Verification Number, tax, passport, voter, company, payroll, payment, and social-registry systems. Their coexistence is not itself duplication: agencies often need different attributes and statutory purposes. The problem arises when records cannot be reconciled, fields have incompatible definitions, agencies repeatedly collect the same evidence, or data are exchanged without an authoritative source and accountability trail.

NITDA's 2025 draft technical standards for digital public infrastructure specify interoperability, role- and attribute-based access, data classification, immutable audit logs, multi-factor authentication, encryption, accessibility, multilingual capacity, performance targets, recovery, and vulnerability testing (National Information Technology Development Agency, 2025). These provisions identify the right operational domains, but the document was issued as a draft. Its ambitious technical targets should not be reported as achieved national performance, and compliance cannot be inferred from publishing a standard.

The binding issue is institutional ownership. Before connecting systems, agencies need a common data dictionary, an authoritative register for each attribute, lawful-purpose agreements, versioned interfaces, service-level commitments, and rules for notifying downstream systems when a record changes. A national data exchange should log which organization requested which attributes for what purpose and permit audit by both the responsible agency and the data-protection authority. It should also publish non-sensitive performance metrics: transaction volume, uptime, response time, failed matches, correction time, security events, and unresolved grievances. Interoperability without these arrangements may accelerate error and surveillance rather than service delivery.

Cybersecurity must be tested at the service and supply-chain level

Nigeria was classified in Tier 3, “Establishing,” in the ITU Global Cybersecurity Index 2024, while Ghana, Kenya, Rwanda, and Tanzania were in the highest tier (International Telecommunication Union, 2024). The index assesses national commitments across legal, technical, organizational, capacity-development, and cooperation pillars; it does not measure the probability that a specific Nigerian platform will be breached or guarantee service continuity. The peer gap is nevertheless informative: rapid digitization has outpaced the institutional integration of cybersecurity measures.

Digital identity, payments, registries, cloud services, telecom networks, and vendor software form an interdependent attack surface. Chima, Ogbar, and Abayomi-Alli (2021) emphasize that cybersecurity, identity, and data protection cannot be treated separately because compromise at enrolment, authentication, storage, or exchange can undermine the entire ecosystem. Centralization can improve standards and monitoring, but it also raises the consequence of a single credential, privileged account, software dependency, or cloud configuration failing. Outsourcing does not transfer public accountability; agencies remain responsible for vendor access, subcontractors, patching, incident reporting, recovery, and the protection of citizens whose data they process.

The relevant control system begins with an inventory of critical services, data, dependencies, owners, and recovery objectives. Agencies should use multi-factor authentication for privileged access, least-privilege permissions, encryption and key management, immutable logs, tested backups, vulnerability disclosure, patch deadlines, independent penetration tests, supplier-risk clauses, and rehearsed incident response. Metrics should distinguish compliance activity from resilience: the number of audits or staff trained is an input; time to detect and contain an incident, restored service within a tested objective, affected records, repeated vulnerabilities, and notification to users are outcomes. Public disclosure must be calibrated so that accountability improves without revealing exploitable detail.

A trustworthy digital state is measured by completed services, not portals

Nigeria's opportunity is to combine private digital innovation with a public architecture that reduces the cost of proving identity, making a payment, submitting information, and receiving a decision. The failure mode is a collection of agency portals that digitize existing forms while preserving duplicate evidence, office visits, discretion, and exclusion. International digital-government frameworks therefore emphasize user-driven, data-informed, government-as-a-platform, open-by-default, and proactive services rather than isolated websites (OECD, 2020; United Nations Department of Economic and Social Affairs, 2024).

Implementation should start with a limited set of high-volume life events—such as birth registration, school entry, business registration, tax filing, social protection, or pension claims—and map the whole user journey. For each service, government should publish completion rate, completion time, failed authentication, repeat submission, payment and travel costs, assisted-channel use, accessibility failures, grievance resolution, system uptime, and privacy or security incidents. Disaggregation is essential: a rising national completion rate can conceal the exclusion of rural residents, women, displaced people, persons with disabilities, or those whose records do not match.

The resulting policy sequence is conditional rather than technological. Extend connectivity where coverage and quality bind; subsidize devices or access where affordability binds; retain assisted channels where skills or disability bind; repair records and redress where identity failure binds; standardize data and responsibilities before connecting registries; and expand digital services only when data protection and recovery controls are operational. The UN's universal DPI safeguards framework reinforces this logic by treating safety, inclusion, accountability, and effective remedy as design requirements rather than add-ons (Office of the United Nations Secretary-General's Envoy on Technology & United Nations Development Programme, 2024). Nigeria's digital state will be credible when more people complete services safely and can correct government error—not when the number of databases, identifiers, or portals alone increases.
