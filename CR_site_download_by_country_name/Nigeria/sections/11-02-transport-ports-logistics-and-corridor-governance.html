11.2 Transport, Ports, Logistics, and Corridor Governance

Nigeria's logistics problem is not adequately described by the number of kilometres of road, port berths, or railway projects. A shipment succeeds only when it passes predictably through every link between origin and destination: a farm or factory road, consolidation point, trunk corridor, border or port, terminal, customs process, and final distributor. Delay at any interface ties up trucks and inventory, increases spoilage and finance costs, and encourages firms to hold larger stocks. The appropriate unit of analysis is therefore an end-to-end journey and its variability, not an isolated asset.

This distinction also clarifies the role of government. Customs, the Nigerian Ports Authority (NPA), terminal concessionaires, the Nigerian Railway Corporation, federal and state road agencies, police and other security bodies, municipal traffic authorities, and private logistics firms each control only part of a corridor. Individually rational actions can still produce a collectively unreliable chain. Nigeria needs both physical investment and an institutional arrangement capable of measuring time, allocating responsibility, and maintaining performance across organizational boundaries.

The comparative signal is persistent unreliability, not absence of activity

The World Bank's Logistics Performance Index (LPI) combines expert perceptions of customs, infrastructure, shipment arrangements, logistics competence, tracking, and timeliness. In the locally preserved World Development Indicators series, Nigeria's overall score moved from 2.40 in 2007 to 2.81 in 2014, then fell to 2.53 in 2018 and stood at 2.60 in the latest recorded wave. Nigeria remained far below South Africa and did not establish a sustained lead over Ghana; Kenya temporarily moved above 3.3 in 2016 before falling back. These comparisons identify a durable system-level weakness, but they are not annual measures and cannot show that a particular port, road, or reform caused a change (World Bank, 2023).

Nigeria's score fluctuated between 2.4 and 2.81 rather than showing sustained convergence toward South Africa. The perception-based survey index diagnoses the reliability of the logistics chain, not a direct journey time, and sparse waves preclude attributing a change to a single reform.

The absence of sustained improvement is compatible with substantial freight activity. It suggests that scale has grown faster than the reliability of the institutions that coordinate it. A large domestic market can generate high cargo volumes while firms still face uncertain clearance, queueing, damaged roads, security stops, weak tracking, and unpredictable delivery. For industrial policy, reliability matters as much as average cost: an exporter that cannot promise a delivery window may lose an order even when the quoted freight rate is competitive.

A productive port is a node, not a completed corridor

The NPA reports that cargo throughput increased from 71.2 million metric tons in 2023 to 103.3 million in 2024, while container throughput rose 9.7 percent to 1.745 million TEU. Average vessel turnaround improved only slightly, from 4.7 to 4.6 days, although the new Lekki deep-sea port reported 2.5 days (Nigerian Ports Authority, 2025). These administrative figures show expansion and differences across ports. They do not establish that a container cleared customs quickly, found a truck or train slot, exited the port area without delay, and reached an inland destination predictably. Throughput is also composition-sensitive: liquid bulk accounted for 55.6 percent of the reported 2024 cargo, so aggregate tonnage should not be treated as container-service performance.

Section 10.4 documented the clearance bottleneck at Tin Can Island and the importance of the National Single Window. The additional point here is that customs digitization cannot by itself remove congestion beyond the customs gate. Nigeria's landlord-port reforms transferred terminal operations to concessionaires and produced measurable changes in terminal performance, but complaints about services and the distribution of regulatory authority persisted (Onwuegbuchunam, 2018). Port reform therefore requires an interface contract among NPA, customs, terminal operators, shipping lines, truckers, rail operators, and Lagos transport agencies. It should specify appointment systems, document interoperability, empty-container handling, truck and wagon access, gate hours, incident response, and a common clock from vessel arrival to inland delivery.

This measurement architecture prevents a common policy error: moving delay from one administrative boundary to another and declaring success. Median time should be paired with the 90th percentile because a lower average can coexist with a long tail of shipments exposed to repeated inspection, informal payments, documentation problems, or scarce transport capacity. Results should also be disaggregated by port, cargo type, destination corridor, and firm size so that a reform benefiting large compliant traders is not mistaken for a universal gain.

Road dominance makes maintenance and rural access national logistics policy

Roads carry more than 90 percent of passenger and freight movements, according to the World Bank's Nigeria rural-access project appraisal. Yet the 2024 scale-up operation estimated that 80 percent of the road network was in poor condition and tied new financing to State Road Funds and State Rural Access Roads Agencies rather than construction alone (World Bank, 2024b). The institutional design is important. Federal highways, state roads, and local or rural roads form one commercial route, but their budgets, engineering standards, and maintenance responsibilities are fragmented. A repaired trunk road has limited value to farmers if feeder roads become impassable during rains; conversely, a rural road program cannot reduce wholesale prices when the main corridor remains insecure or congested.

Maintenance is therefore not a residual engineering item. Politicians receive visible credit for commissioning a new road, while routine drainage clearing, pavement inspection, bridge maintenance, and axle-load enforcement produce diffuse benefits and are easier to postpone. Deferred maintenance then converts predictable operating expenditure into a much larger rehabilitation bill. Climate exposure intensifies this cycle: higher rainfall, flooding, and heat damage surfaces and culverts, while weak drainage and unregulated development increase failure around cities. State Road Funds can help only if revenues are protected, condition surveys determine priorities, contracts are performance-based, and expenditure and road quality are publicly reported. A dedicated fund without those controls can simply create another off-budget allocation channel.

The distributive stakes are substantial. Rural access connects farms not only to markets but also to storage, processing, health care, schools, and non-farm work. Better roads may increase farm-gate prices and reduce losses, but these effects depend on competition among buyers and transporters; otherwise part of the benefit can be captured through lower purchase prices or unchanged freight margins. Monitoring should therefore pair engineering outputs with travel time, seasonal passability, freight prices, marketed volumes, spoilage, and household access. This is the evidence needed to distinguish a completed contract from an improved economic service.

Rail is valuable where service frequency and interfaces make it usable

Rail is often presented as the natural solution to overloaded roads. The comparison is more conditional. National Bureau of Statistics data record 2.18 million rail passengers in 2023 and a 102 percent annual rise in rail cargo volume, but the absolute freight volume remained only about 318,000 tons (National Bureau of Statistics, 2024). A Lagos-Kano corridor study using 2017-2018 operational data found road freight overwhelmingly larger and faster: trucks completed the route in roughly 2-3 days, compared with 5-6 days by rail. Rail nevertheless performed better in freight ton-kilometres per vehicle-hour and throughput per unit of energy (Daramola, 2022). The evidence explains why lower unit cost in principle does not guarantee modal shift. Shippers purchase frequency, routing flexibility, security, and delivery time as well as transport capacity.

The appropriate rail strategy is thus commodity- and corridor-specific. Regular container or bulk flows can support scheduled rail services where port sidings, inland terminals, rolling stock, loading equipment, and last-mile road connections operate as one system. Rehabilitation that increases track speed but leaves a wagon shortage or an unreliable timetable unresolved will not attract freight. Nor should passenger and freight objectives be mixed without explicit capacity allocation. Contracts should define train frequency, on-time departure and arrival, wagon availability, loss and damage, interchange time, safety, and the road-kilometres displaced—not merely kilometres of track built.

Corridor governance must govern time, territory, and accountability

A corridor is also a political territory. Gambino and Reboredo's (2024) study of the Abidjan-Lagos corridor shows how infrastructure promises generate competing expectations, contested land and labour relations, and periods of suspension as projects and livelihoods wait on uncertain decisions. These effects are not captured by a travel-time model. New highways, port expansions, and logistics hubs can shift land values, displace residents or informal workers, and redirect trade between cities and countries. Unresolved compensation and livelihood claims can delay implementation and undermine the legitimacy needed for enforcement.

The solution is not a single super-agency that replaces every legal mandate. It is a corridor performance compact with a designated convenor, shared data definitions, named owners for each failure point, and escalation rules across federal, state, local, and private actors. The World Bank's data-driven corridor framework recommends combining conventional logistics indicators with spatial and operational data to observe the full chain rather than relying on national averages (World Bank, 2024a). Lagos offers a partial institutional lesson: a metropolitan authority with a statutory transport fund can coordinate modes more effectively than project-by-project administration, although the model still requires accountability for service quality, distribution, and land-use effects (World Bank Independent Evaluation Group, 2016; World Bank, 2025d).

For Nigeria, the practical sequence is to select a small number of economically important journeys—such as port-to-inland industrial and agricultural corridors—and publish a baseline from origin to destination. Investment should then target the binding interface revealed by the data. If the main delay is documentary duplication, interoperability and risk-based inspection precede new concrete. If it is gate congestion, appointments and depot capacity come first. If unreliable rail frequency is binding, operating contracts and rolling stock matter before network extension. If seasonal feeder-road failure drives food losses, maintenance and drainage dominate a flagship expressway.

This approach changes infrastructure policy from a catalogue of projects into a verifiable service strategy. The policy objective is not to maximize cargo tonnage, roads commissioned, or kilometres of rail in isolation. It is to reduce the predictable and tail risks of moving people and goods, while making the fiscal, environmental, labour, and distributional costs of that improvement explicit. A corridor-level public dashboard—covering end-to-end time, variability, cost, safety, emissions, maintenance condition, and grievance resolution—would give firms a planning signal and allow citizens and governments to see which institution is responsible when the chain fails.
