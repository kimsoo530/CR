6.3 Voice, Accountability, Stability, and Security Pressure

Nigeria's governance problem is not a simple choice between democracy and security. Elections, courts, media, unions, professional associations, protest movements, and civil-society organizations give citizens more ways to contest public authority than they possess in a closed regime. Yet insecurity changes who can use those channels, where the state can operate, and how readily officials can justify exceptional measures. The central issue is therefore whether democratic voice can still produce correction when violence disrupts elections, displaces communities, weakens local administration, and expands coercive discretion.

The comparative indicators capture this tension without resolving it. In the World Bank's revised Worldwide Governance Indicators, Nigeria's 2024 estimate for voice and accountability was -0.73, while political stability and absence of violence was -2.00 (World Bank, 2025c). V-Dem's 2026 report, which presents estimates for 2025, placed Nigeria at 0.50 on its Electoral Democracy Index and 0.31 on its Liberal Democracy Index; the liberal component score of 0.54 was marked as a statistically significant decline over the preceding decade (V-Dem Institute, 2026). These are perception-based or expert-coded diagnostics with uncertainty, not administrative counts. Their value is to identify a configuration: electoral competition and participation remain meaningful, but liberal constraints and physical security are substantially weaker.

Democratic demand, declining electoral participation

The demand side of democracy remains stronger than the participation trend might suggest. Afrobarometer's Round 10 survey, fielded in 2024, found that 64 percent of Nigerians preferred democracy to any other form of government (Afrobarometer, 2025). That result does not imply satisfaction with how democracy works. It indicates that low participation should not automatically be interpreted as rejection of elections or a preference for authoritarian rule.

The thinning of the electoral channel is nevertheless pronounced. INEC's retrospective series reports presidential-election turnout, measured against registered voters, falling from 53.7 percent in 2011 to 43.6 percent in 2015, 34.7 percent in 2019, and 26.7 percent in 2023 (Independent National Electoral Commission, 2026). The 2023 register contained 93,469,008 voters, so the denominator had expanded even as the share voting contracted (Independent National Electoral Commission, 2024). Registration, permanent-voter-card collection, accreditation, ballots cast, valid votes, and votes for the winner are different stages and must not be conflated. The series establishes a participation decline, but by itself cannot allocate responsibility among insecurity, administrative failure, distrust, economic costs, registration inflation, or voter choice.

Process evidence identifies several plausible mechanisms. The 2023 election introduced the Bimodal Voter Accreditation System and the INEC Results Viewing portal under the Electoral Act 2022, but the EU observation mission found that late deployment, inconsistent procedures, delayed presidential-result uploads, inadequate communication, violence, and impunity for electoral offences damaged trust. Its 23 recommendations included accountable selection of INEC members, real-time access to results, protection for media practitioners, and enforcement against electoral offences (European Union Election Observation Mission, 2023). Madueke and Enyiazu (2025), using interviews and documentary evidence, similarly locate integrity problems in INEC's organizational independence, adherence to rules, and political interference. Their qualitative design identifies mechanisms rather than estimating how much each mechanism reduced turnout.

Security and election administration also interact unevenly across territory. Oni et al. (2025) argue from documentary and secondary evidence that kidnapping, banditry, terrorism, attacks on election facilities, and weak security coordination can disrupt logistics and deter participation. Yet a large security deployment is not automatically corrective. Ezeibe et al. (2026) find in qualitative research on off-cycle governorship elections that militarized election environments can intimidate voters and advantage incumbents even when deployment is justified as protection. The relevant distinction is therefore between security that keeps the participation chain open and coercion that narrows political competition.

One country, several security systems

Treating insecurity as one national phenomenon produces the wrong administrative response. In the North East, the long insurgency involving Boko Haram factions and the Islamic State West Africa Province has combined territorial contestation, attacks on civilians, restricted humanitarian access, and prolonged displacement. In the North West, armed bandit networks use kidnapping, illegal levies, livestock theft, and control of rural routes. North Central violence more often combines farmer-herder conflict, communal mobilization, land and local-authority disputes, and reprisals. The South East faces separatist coercion and attacks affecting public offices and mobility, while the Niger Delta's oil theft and armed networks create a different relationship among illicit markets, environmental harm, state revenues, and security agencies.

Displacement data make the territorial distinction visible. IOM's September 2024 assessment identified 2,255,595 internally displaced people in the North East, while its November 2024 assessment recorded 1,322,766 across ten North Central and North West states (International Organization for Migration, 2024). The rounds use different geographic frames and dates and should not be summed as if they were a synchronized census. By the end of 2025, UNHCR reported a national stock of 3.57 million internally displaced people, alongside more than 408,000 Nigerian refugees in neighboring countries (United Nations High Commissioner for Refugees, 2026). These figures measure people displaced, not government territorial control or the number of violent events. They show, however, that insecurity is a service-delivery and representation problem as well as a military one: displacement changes access to registration, schooling, health care, land, livelihoods, local political voice, and legal remedy.

Federalism complicates response capacity. The armed forces, police, intelligence services, and much of the legal authority for coercion are federal, while governors and local officials receive immediate demands for protection and relief. Communities therefore turn to vigilantes, hunters, neighborhood guards, and the Civilian Joint Task Force when centrally controlled forces lack local information or response time. The arrangement can improve intelligence and access, but it distributes coercive power without necessarily distributing clear command, finance, discipline, or liability.

When protection is delegated

Research on the Civilian Joint Task Force in the North East illustrates the trade-off. Agbiboa (2021) shows how civilian defense groups emerged from community efforts to survive Boko Haram violence and became useful intermediaries between residents and state forces. Their local knowledge can reveal armed-group movement and make protection more responsive. The same proximity creates risks of retaliation, recruitment abuse, factional capture, and unresolved status after active conflict. Hybrid security is thus neither evidence of state absence nor proof of successful participation; it is a form of delegated authority whose accountability depends on registration, command, rules on force, complaints, and an exit pathway.

The civic-space problem appears even where armed conflict is not the immediate setting. The National Human Rights Commission recorded more than two million complaints in 2024 and highlighted mass killings, abductions, restrictions on expression, assembly and association, and law-enforcement-related violations (National Human Rights Commission, 2025). Complaint totals are shaped by awareness, access, duplicate or unresolved submissions, and reporting practices; they cannot be read as verified incidence. Amnesty International (2024) separately documented alleged attacks, arrests, and intimidation surrounding the August 2024 #EndBadGovernance protests. Together, the sources identify a correction-system risk: when agencies responsible for order are also the subject of complaints, credible oversight requires independent investigation and visible case disposition rather than institutional self-reporting alone.

The accountability test is traceable protection

Reform should begin by making both participation and coercion traceable. For elections, INEC should publish polling-unit readiness, opening time, BVAS accreditation status, result-form upload time, cancellations, supplementary polls, and final disposition using stable identifiers. Election-security plans should specify which agency commands each location, record disruptions and use-of-force incidents, and permit comparison of turnout and operational failure across polling units without treating correlation as proof of intimidation. Publicly accountable appointment procedures, enforceable deadlines for results access, and documented prosecution or reasoned closure of electoral offences would address the process failures identified by observers more directly than another technology purchase.

For everyday security, the Office of the National Security Adviser, armed forces, Nigeria Police Force, state governments, emergency agencies, and humanitarian partners need a shared but access-controlled incident and displacement architecture. It should distinguish attacks, abductions, communal clashes, security-force operations, civilian casualties, displacement, return, and service restoration by location and date. Success should not be inferred from enemy body counts or announced camp closures. More defensible measures include reductions in repeat attacks and abductions, safe access to markets and schools, voluntary and durable return, case resolution, and verified civilian-harm investigations.

Oversight must follow delegated force. Community security groups should have public legal status, vetted rosters, identifiable command, weapons and payment records, training obligations, complaint channels, and demobilization or integration rules. The National Human Rights Commission, Police Service Commission, courts, and relevant legislative committees need access to incident files and deadlines for reporting outcomes. This design does not assume that all complaints are substantiated or that all informal protection is abusive. It creates a route by which citizens can challenge coercion without dismantling the local knowledge on which protection sometimes depends.

Nigeria's voice-and-stability dilemma is therefore best understood as an accountability-chain problem. Citizens continue to demand democracy, but participation falls when insecurity, administrative failure, or coercive discretion makes voice costly and correction uncertain. Security policy strengthens democratic stability only when it preserves access to elections and services, differentiates regional threats, and makes every exercise of public or delegated force reviewable. This is why the governance indicators considered in the next section must be read through specific institutions and territorial processes rather than as a single national verdict.
