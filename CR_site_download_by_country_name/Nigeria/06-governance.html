6.1 Government Effectiveness and Regulatory Quality

Nigeria's central governance problem is not an absence of reform activity. It is the difficulty of converting a presidential decision, statute, budget authorization, or regulatory rule into a predictable result across ministries, agencies, states, and frontline organizations. The distinction matters because *government effectiveness* concerns the quality and credibility of policy formulation and public-service delivery, whereas *regulatory quality* concerns the state's capacity to design and implement rules that enable private activity. Both are produced through implementation chains rather than by a single central institution.

The 2025 revision of the Worldwide Governance Indicators (WGI) places Nigeria's 2024 government-effectiveness estimate at -1.01 and its regulatory-quality estimate at -0.63 on an approximately -2.5 to +2.5 scale (World Bank, 2025c). These values indicate persistent weakness, but they should not be treated as precise administrative measurements. WGI combines perceptions from household, firm, and expert sources, and the World Bank recalculated the historical series under a revised methodology. Its 90 percent confidence intervals are wide and overlap across adjacent years. The apparent improvement in government effectiveness in 2023, for example, was not sustained in the 2024 point estimate and is too small relative to sampling uncertainty to establish a real one-year change. Regulatory quality improved in point estimates between 2022 and 2024, but the overlapping intervals again counsel caution.

The defensible conclusion is therefore not that Nigeria's administration suddenly improved or deteriorated in a particular year. It is that the country remains in a zone where policy credibility and implementation are recurrent constraints, while regulatory performance may vary materially by sector and reform episode. The aggregate indicators are useful warning signals, but they are too coarse to identify whether a failure originates in policy design, coordination, financing, procurement, staffing, enforcement, or subnational execution. Those mechanisms require institution- and program-level evidence.

A national score over a federal implementation system

Nigeria's federal structure makes a single national effectiveness score especially incomplete. Federal institutions control macroeconomic policy, national regulation, and major transfers, but states and local systems carry much of the operational responsibility for basic education, primary health care, water, roads, land administration, and business-facing permits. The same federal rule can consequently produce different results where political commitment, inherited bureaucratic capability, revenue, security, and personnel continuity differ. Variation is not statistical noise around one national bureaucracy; it is part of the governing system.

The State Fiscal Transparency, Accountability and Sustainability Program (SFTAS) illustrates both the possibility and the limits of reform in that system. Between 2018 and 2022, the program tied performance-based grants to fiscal transparency, domestic-revenue administration, expenditure efficiency, and debt management. All 36 states published budgets and audited financial statements, and 33 regularly published quarterly budget-implementation reports (Isser & Zovighian, 2025). These are substantial institutional outputs. Yet state performance varied, and sustainability depended on political stability, retained bureaucratic competence, ownership of the reform, and whether new routines survived leadership transitions. Legal adoption and document publication were easier than changes that threatened patronage or required continuous organizational learning. SFTAS thus shows that fiscal incentives can alter administrative behavior, but also that compliance outputs should not be confused with better services unless budgets, procurement records, staffing, and facility-level results can be traced through the same chain.

This perspective also changes how central coordination should be assessed. Nigeria's 2023 macroeconomic reforms demonstrated that the executive can take consequential decisions, including fuel-subsidy removal and exchange-rate unification. The World Bank's country diagnostic nevertheless finds that compensatory social-protection delivery was slow and uneven and identifies weak policy coordination, budget transparency, and policy-reversal risk as continuing constraints (World Bank, 2026). Decisiveness at the point of adoption and effectiveness at the point of delivery are analytically distinct. A reform loses credibility when households and firms experience its costs immediately but receive mitigation, regulatory clarity, or public services late and unevenly.

Management practices and the credibility of rules

Evidence from the federal civil service further cautions against reducing effectiveness to tighter central control. Rasul and Rogger (2018) matched management-practice surveys to independent engineering assessments of roughly 4,700 federal projects. Greater managerial autonomy was positively associated with project completion, while management practices centered on incentives and monitoring were negatively associated with completion. Because the study is observational, these associations should not be read as universal causal effects. They nevertheless expose an important mechanism: where public tasks are complex and implementation requires adaptation, additional reporting and target systems can crowd out problem solving, encourage defensive compliance, or reward what is easiest to count. Effective control must therefore vary with task clarity and verifiability. Standardized procurement, payment, and audit controls may be appropriate for routine transactions, while technically complex delivery requires bounded discretion combined with ex post verification of quality and integrity.

Regulatory quality is similarly sector-specific. Usman (2020) explains Nigeria's uneven post-military reform record through its political settlement: external pressures and formal institutions interact with the distribution of power among political and economic elites, producing selective and episodic reform. Telecommunications liberalization could generate a coalition around entry, investment, and expansion, whereas petroleum-sector reform repeatedly confronted concentrated rents and stronger veto interests. The implication is not that a single national regulatory score is irrelevant, but that it averages across markets with different coalitions, enforcement capacities, and credibility problems. A well-drafted rule will not improve investment if licenses remain discretionary, contracts cannot be enforced, foreign-exchange access changes unpredictably, or regulated firms expect reversal after a political transition.

Policy design: diagnose and repair the delivery chain

The evidence supports three linked priorities. First, government-effectiveness reform should begin with a sector- and tier-specific delivery diagnosis. For each priority program, the government should publish the responsible organization, budget release, procurement milestone, staffing commitment, output, service-quality measure, and correction route. This would reveal whether the binding constraint is authority, cash release, coordination, capability, or enforcement rather than assuming that another coordinating body is required.

Second, intergovernmental incentives should reward verified continuation and service results, not only rule adoption. The SFTAS experience supports transparent, independently verified conditions, but future transfers should connect fiscal routines to comparable operational outcomes and test whether gains persist after grants and governors change. Transition protocols, protected technical teams, interoperable administrative records, and public reporting of unresolved exceptions would make institutional continuity an explicit performance dimension.

Third, regulatory reform should be organized around credible commitment. Independent regulators need clear mandates, published decision criteria, reasoned and reviewable rulings, predictable transition rules, and outcome measures such as processing time, entry, investment, price, reliability, and complaint resolution. These arrangements constrain discretion without eliminating the bounded autonomy needed for complex decisions. Taken together, the goal is not to raise an aggregate governance score directly. It is to make policy commitments traceable, rules predictable, and implementation failures observable and correctable. Sustained improvement in those underlying systems would then provide a substantive basis for better governance assessments.

6.2 Rule of Law, Corruption Control, and Judicial Capacity

Nigeria's rule-of-law problem is not adequately described as a shortage of courts or anti-corruption bodies. The country has a constitutionally differentiated court system, a National Judicial Council (NJC), federal and state ministries of justice, police and prosecutorial organizations, the Economic and Financial Crimes Commission (EFCC) established under its 2004 Act, the Independent Corrupt Practices and Other Related Offences Commission (ICPC), public auditors, and specialized accountability bodies (Federal Republic of Nigeria, 2004). The harder problem is whether a complaint or arrest can move through investigation, charging, adjudication, judgment, enforcement, and remedy without delay, informal payment, political interruption, or loss of the case record. Rule of law is therefore produced by the continuity of a justice chain, not by judicial independence or prosecution statistics in isolation.

International indicators establish the breadth of the problem but not its institutional cause. In the World Justice Project's 2025 index, Nigeria scored 0.41 and ranked 120th of 143 jurisdictions overall. Its factor scores were 0.33 for absence of corruption, 0.45 for civil justice, and 0.39 for criminal justice; order and security was especially weak at 0.36. Nigeria's overall score increased by less than one percent, while the index also recorded deterioration in measures of improper government influence over civil and criminal justice (World Justice Project, 2025). These household- and expert-survey measures should be treated as comparative signals rather than objective court-performance rates. They show that formal judicial institutions coexist with serious problems of integrity, accessibility, security, and enforcement, but they do not identify which agency or procedural stage produces a particular failure.

Constitutional independence and operational dependence

The 1999 Constitution vests judicial power in federal and state courts, protects fair hearing, and establishes the NJC to participate in appointments, discipline, policy, and the administration of judicial funds (Federal Republic of Nigeria, 1999/2023). This architecture is intended to buffer judges from direct executive control. Formal insulation, however, does not by itself secure timely or impartial adjudication. State judiciaries depend on functioning budget releases, registries, courtrooms, digital and paper records, bailiffs, legal aid, police case files, prosecutors, witnesses, and the production of detained defendants. Weakness in any one of these links can convert a legal entitlement into delay.

Judicial independence also has more than one dimension. It requires protection from executive or partisan retaliation, but it also requires judges to apply law consistently and to resist being drawn beyond their institutional competence by powerful litigants. Sulayman (2025) argues that the Supreme Court's growing involvement in high-stakes political disputes can allow powerful actors to use the Court's legitimacy to secure outcomes that stretch the boundary between adjudication and political decision. This interpretation is contestable: intervention may also be necessary when political institutions fail to resolve constitutional disputes. Its value is to show that independence should not be equated with unchecked judicial discretion. Transparent appointment criteria, reasoned judgments, enforceable ethical rules, and consistent jurisdictional doctrine are complementary safeguards.

The criminal-justice system makes the operational constraint visible. The Administration of Criminal Justice Act 2015 sought faster case management, protection of suspects and victims, and stronger coordination among justice institutions (Federal Republic of Nigeria, 2015). Yet the Federal Ministry of Justice acknowledged in 2024 that implementation across federal and state jurisdictions continued to face court congestion, unavailable data, delayed trials, and inconsistent procedures (Federal Ministry of Justice, 2024). On 29 June 2026, the Nigerian Correctional Service recorded 53,670 people awaiting trial out of a total custodial population of 82,195, or 65 percent (Nigerian Correctional Service, 2026). This proportion cannot be attributed to judges alone. It is a system-level stock produced by arrest and investigation quality, charging decisions, access to counsel and bail, adjournments, court scheduling, transfer of case files, transport from custody, and the capacity to conclude or discontinue weak cases.

Bribery as a repeated transaction and a failed correction route

The 2023 corruption survey conducted by the National Bureau of Statistics and UNODC provides a more direct measure of citizens' encounters with public authority. Based on 33,035 households, it found that 56 percent of adults had contacted at least one public official during the preceding year. Among those with contact, 27 percent paid a bribe; when requests that citizens refused are included, exposure reached 34 percent. The payment-only rate fell modestly from 29 percent in 2019, but bribe-payers still made an estimated 87 million payments in 2023, an average of 5.1 each (National Bureau of Statistics & United Nations Office on Drugs and Crime, 2024). The repetition is analytically important: bribery is often not a one-off moral lapse but an administrative equilibrium in which officials control recurring access to police assistance, utilities, documents, permits, or case movement.

The survey also identifies a possible opening for institutional reform. Seventy percent of people asked for a bribe refused on at least one occasion, and the share of bribe-payers who reported an incident to an official authority rose from 3.6 percent in 2019 to 8.6 percent in 2023. Among reported incidents, the share that initiated a formal procedure increased from 16 to 45 percent. But 8.6 percent remains a very low reporting rate, and the national improvement conceals a sharp territorial divergence: reporting rose to 13.4 percent in the northern zones but fell to 1.7 percent in the southern zones. These figures do not reveal whether a procedure ended in a substantiated finding or sanction. They show that willingness to resist and report is growing, while the correction route remains weak, geographically uneven, and poorly observable.

This distinction matters for evaluating anti-corruption agencies. Convictions and asset recoveries demonstrate enforcement activity, but totals alone cannot show whether cases are selected impartially, survive appeal, reach politically connected defendants, return assets transparently, or deter corruption in the service transaction where it occurs. Nwozor et al. (2020), drawing on ten expert interviews and documentary evidence, identify presidential interference, selective prosecution, and weak institutionalization as constraints on Nigeria's anti-corruption campaign. The small qualitative sample cannot estimate how prevalent selective enforcement is, but it supplies a plausible mechanism for why high enforcement activity may coexist with low public confidence. The appropriate empirical test is case continuity: whether similarly situated defendants face comparable investigation, charging, trial, and disposition regardless of party or office.

Public audit reveals a related problem before prosecution begins. Abdul-Baki et al. (2023) document cases in which Auditors-General disclosed accounting irregularities despite personal and institutional risks, sometimes using less visible strategies to sustain resistance and reduce retaliation. This evidence complicates the claim that all accountability institutions are simply captured. Officials can resist corruption from within, but disclosure has limited effect when audit findings do not trigger documented legislative review, management response, recovery, investigation, or prosecution. Protection of individual integrity therefore needs an institutional pathway that carries a finding to correction.

Making cases and remedies traceable

The policy implication is to reorganize reform around traceable case progression. Courts, prosecutors, police, corrections, legal-aid institutions, EFCC, and ICPC should use interoperable case identifiers that record arrest or complaint, charge, custody status, counsel, scheduled hearings, adjournment reasons, disposition, appeal, enforcement, and asset recovery. Public reporting should present median time at each stage, the age distribution of pending cases, adjournments by cause, pretrial detention beyond statutory thresholds, and compliance with judgments. Aggregate filing or conviction counts are insufficient because they reward initiation without revealing attrition, delay, or unequal treatment.

For pretrial detention, the responsible institutions should jointly review old and legally weak cases, enforce regular remand review, expand functioning legal aid and non-custodial measures, and require an auditable reason whenever a detainee is not produced or a case is adjourned. Success should be measured by shorter detention conditional on case type, fewer avoidable adjournments, and completed dispositions without increasing arbitrary release or unsafe case dismissal.

Anti-corruption credibility requires a parallel chain. Appointment and removal rules should protect investigative and prosecutorial leadership from case-specific political direction, while published selection criteria and disposition data should make discretion reviewable. Audit findings and citizen reports need acknowledgement, status tracking, confidentiality, protection from retaliation, and a reasoned closure notice. Recovered assets should be reconciled from court order to receipt and final public use. These measures do not promise to eliminate corruption through technology or another special body. They make selective inaction, procedural delay, and lost remedies more visible, allowing judicial independence and anti-corruption enforcement to be judged by consistent treatment and completed correction rather than institutional form alone.

6.3 Voice, Accountability, Stability, and Security Pressure

Nigeria's governance problem is not a simple choice between democracy and security. Elections, courts, media, unions, professional associations, protest movements, and civil-society organizations give citizens more ways to contest public authority than they possess in a closed regime. Yet insecurity changes who can use those channels, where the state can operate, and how readily officials can justify exceptional measures. The central issue is therefore whether democratic voice can still produce correction when violence disrupts elections, displaces communities, weakens local administration, and expands coercive discretion.

The comparative indicators capture this tension without resolving it. In the World Bank's revised Worldwide Governance Indicators, Nigeria's 2024 estimate for voice and accountability was -0.73, while political stability and absence of violence was -2.00 (World Bank, 2025c). V-Dem's 2026 report, which presents estimates for 2025, placed Nigeria at 0.50 on its Electoral Democracy Index and 0.31 on its Liberal Democracy Index; the liberal component score of 0.54 was marked as a statistically significant decline over the preceding decade (V-Dem Institute, 2026). These are perception-based or expert-coded diagnostics with uncertainty, not administrative counts. Their value is to identify a configuration: electoral competition and participation remain meaningful, but liberal constraints and physical security are substantially weaker.

Democratic demand, declining electoral participation

The demand side of democracy remains stronger than the participation trend might suggest. Afrobarometer's Round 10 survey, fielded in 2024, found that 64 percent of Nigerians preferred democracy to any other form of government (Afrobarometer, 2025). That result does not imply satisfaction with how democracy works. It indicates that low participation should not automatically be interpreted as rejection of elections or a preference for authoritarian rule.

The thinning of the electoral channel is nevertheless pronounced. INEC's retrospective series reports presidential-election turnout, measured against registered voters, falling from 53.7 percent in 2011 to 43.6 percent in 2015, 34.7 percent in 2019, and 26.7 percent in 2023 (Independent National Electoral Commission, 2026). The 2023 register contained 93,469,008 voters, so the denominator had expanded even as the share voting contracted (Independent National Electoral Commission, 2024). Registration, permanent-voter-card collection, accreditation, ballots cast, valid votes, and votes for the winner are different stages and must not be conflated. The series establishes a participation decline, but by itself cannot allocate responsibility among insecurity, administrative failure, distrust, economic costs, registration inflation, or voter choice.

Process evidence identifies several plausible mechanisms. The 2023 election introduced the Bimodal Voter Accreditation System and the INEC Results Viewing portal under the Electoral Act 2022, but the EU observation mission found that late deployment, inconsistent procedures, delayed presidential-result uploads, inadequate communication, violence, and impunity for electoral offences damaged trust. Its 23 recommendations included accountable selection of INEC members, real-time access to results, protection for media practitioners, and enforcement against electoral offences (European Union Election Observation Mission, 2023). Madueke and Enyiazu (2025), using interviews and documentary evidence, similarly locate integrity problems in INEC's organizational independence, adherence to rules, and political interference. Their qualitative design identifies mechanisms rather than estimating how much each mechanism reduced turnout.

Security and election administration also interact unevenly across territory. Oni et al. (2025) argue from documentary and secondary evidence that kidnapping, banditry, terrorism, attacks on election facilities, and weak security coordination can disrupt logistics and deter participation. Yet a large security deployment is not automatically corrective. Ezeibe et al. (2026) find in qualitative research on off-cycle governorship elections that militarized election environments can intimidate voters and advantage incumbents even when deployment is justified as protection. The relevant distinction is therefore between security that keeps the participation chain open and coercion that narrows political competition.

One country, several security systems

Treating insecurity as one national phenomenon produces the wrong administrative response. In the North East, the long insurgency involving Boko Haram factions and the Islamic State West Africa Province has combined territorial contestation, attacks on civilians, restricted humanitarian access, and prolonged displacement. In the North West, armed bandit networks use kidnapping, illegal levies, livestock theft, and control of rural routes. North Central violence more often combines farmer-herder conflict, communal mobilization, land and local-authority disputes, and reprisals. The South East faces separatist coercion and attacks affecting public offices and mobility, while the Niger Delta's oil theft and armed networks create a different relationship among illicit markets, environmental harm, state revenues, and security agencies.

Displacement data make the territorial distinction visible. IOM's September 2024 assessment identified 2,255,595 internally displaced people in the North East, while its November 2024 assessment recorded 1,322,766 across ten North Central and North West states (International Organization for Migration, 2024). The rounds use different geographic frames and dates and should not be summed as if they were a synchronized census. By the end of 2025, UNHCR reported a national stock of 3.57 million internally displaced people, alongside more than 408,000 Nigerian refugees in neighboring countries (United Nations High Commissioner for Refugees, 2026). These figures measure people displaced, not government territorial control or the number of violent events. They show, however, that insecurity is a service-delivery and representation problem as well as a military one: displacement changes access to registration, schooling, health care, land, livelihoods, local political voice, and legal remedy.

Federalism complicates response capacity. The armed forces, police, intelligence services, and much of the legal authority for coercion are federal, while governors and local officials receive immediate demands for protection and relief. Communities therefore turn to vigilantes, hunters, neighborhood guards, and the Civilian Joint Task Force when centrally controlled forces lack local information or response time. The arrangement can improve intelligence and access, but it distributes coercive power without necessarily distributing clear command, finance, discipline, or liability.

When protection is delegated

Research on the Civilian Joint Task Force in the North East illustrates the trade-off. Agbiboa (2021) shows how civilian defense groups emerged from community efforts to survive Boko Haram violence and became useful intermediaries between residents and state forces. Their local knowledge can reveal armed-group movement and make protection more responsive. The same proximity creates risks of retaliation, recruitment abuse, factional capture, and unresolved status after active conflict. Hybrid security is thus neither evidence of state absence nor proof of successful participation; it is a form of delegated authority whose accountability depends on registration, command, rules on force, complaints, and an exit pathway.

The civic-space problem appears even where armed conflict is not the immediate setting. The National Human Rights Commission recorded more than two million complaints in 2024 and highlighted mass killings, abductions, restrictions on expression, assembly and association, and law-enforcement-related violations (National Human Rights Commission, 2025). Complaint totals are shaped by awareness, access, duplicate or unresolved submissions, and reporting practices; they cannot be read as verified incidence. Amnesty International (2024) separately documented alleged attacks, arrests, and intimidation surrounding the August 2024 #EndBadGovernance protests. Together, the sources identify a correction-system risk: when agencies responsible for order are also the subject of complaints, credible oversight requires independent investigation and visible case disposition rather than institutional self-reporting alone.

The accountability test is traceable protection

Reform should begin by making both participation and coercion traceable. For elections, INEC should publish polling-unit readiness, opening time, BVAS accreditation status, result-form upload time, cancellations, supplementary polls, and final disposition using stable identifiers. Election-security plans should specify which agency commands each location, record disruptions and use-of-force incidents, and permit comparison of turnout and operational failure across polling units without treating correlation as proof of intimidation. Publicly accountable appointment procedures, enforceable deadlines for results access, and documented prosecution or reasoned closure of electoral offences would address the process failures identified by observers more directly than another technology purchase.

For everyday security, the Office of the National Security Adviser, armed forces, Nigeria Police Force, state governments, emergency agencies, and humanitarian partners need a shared but access-controlled incident and displacement architecture. It should distinguish attacks, abductions, communal clashes, security-force operations, civilian casualties, displacement, return, and service restoration by location and date. Success should not be inferred from enemy body counts or announced camp closures. More defensible measures include reductions in repeat attacks and abductions, safe access to markets and schools, voluntary and durable return, case resolution, and verified civilian-harm investigations.

Oversight must follow delegated force. Community security groups should have public legal status, vetted rosters, identifiable command, weapons and payment records, training obligations, complaint channels, and demobilization or integration rules. The National Human Rights Commission, Police Service Commission, courts, and relevant legislative committees need access to incident files and deadlines for reporting outcomes. This design does not assume that all complaints are substantiated or that all informal protection is abusive. It creates a route by which citizens can challenge coercion without dismantling the local knowledge on which protection sometimes depends.

Nigeria's voice-and-stability dilemma is therefore best understood as an accountability-chain problem. Citizens continue to demand democracy, but participation falls when insecurity, administrative failure, or coercive discretion makes voice costly and correction uncertain. Security policy strengthens democratic stability only when it preserves access to elections and services, differentiates regional threats, and makes every exercise of public or delegated force reviewable. This is why the governance indicators considered in the next section must be read through specific institutions and territorial processes rather than as a single national verdict.

6.4 Reading Governance Indicators with Nigerian Institutions

The indicators should not be read as a single scorecard. They are diagnostic signals that point toward specific administrative arenas: regulatory quality to business licensing and market governance; rule of law to courts and policing; corruption control to procurement and revenue; political stability to security coordination; government effectiveness to budget execution and service delivery.

The six series are perception-based composites with estimation uncertainty, not direct measures of a single national bureaucracy. Read levels and multi-year patterns alongside confidence intervals and institution-specific evidence; do not infer annual reform effects from small movements in point estimates.
