4.1 Historical Formation of the Political Order

Nigeria's Fourth Republic is best understood not as a clean democratic beginning in 1999, but as a civilian political order built from institutions created under colonialism and repeatedly reorganized by military governments. Colonial rule joined diverse political communities within one state while governing them through territorially differentiated systems of indirect rule and, later, powerful regions. The early post-independence federation consequently combined competitive national politics with parties rooted in regional and ethnopolitical constituencies. Military rule altered that balance. Successive regimes created states, centralized coercive authority and oil revenue, and governed through a hierarchical chain of command. By the time elected government returned, Nigeria had more subnational units but a far more centralized fiscal and security system than the federation inherited at independence (Osaghae, 2018; Suberu, 2022).

This history produced a dual institutional inheritance. The 1999 Constitution restored an elected presidency, bicameral legislature, courts, 36 elected state governments, and regular elections, while retaining important features of military-era centralization. Political authority thus became horizontally and territorially contestable without becoming administratively decentralized to the same degree. One interpretation identifies this as a central contradiction of the Fourth Republic: electoral institutions operate within a state whose constitutional and organizational structure still bears the imprint of prolonged military government. Yet that inheritance is not mechanically deterministic. Civilian actors have repeatedly used legislatures, courts, state governments, parties, and elections in ways that military rulers did not control (Onuoha, 2023).

The first phase of the Fourth Republic was dominated by the People's Democratic Party (PDP), which held the presidency from 1999 to 2015 and assembled a broad coalition across regions and elite networks. Its dominance supplied a measure of national integration, but it relied heavily on incumbency, access to state resources, defections, and distributive bargaining rather than stable programmatic commitments. The defeat of the proposed constitutional changes associated with President Olusegun Obasanjo's third-term project in the National Assembly in May 2006 was therefore an important institutional test. It showed that executive influence was substantial but not unlimited: factional conflict inside the governing coalition, legislative vetoes, civil-society mobilization, and constitutional rules could combine to block presidential tenure extension (Ibrahim & Egwu, 2010).

The 2015 alternation revealed another consequence of Nigeria's federal design. The All Progressives Congress did not defeat the PDP only because voters were dissatisfied with insecurity, corruption, or economic management. Federalism had allowed opposition parties and governors to retain offices, revenue, organization, and bargaining leverage below the national level. LeVan's field research shows how state and local political struggles could be linked to national opposition coordination, giving the emerging APC a territorial platform from which to challenge the federal incumbent (LeVan, 2018). This helps explain why opposition survived long enough to merge and win—an outcome less likely where losing national office eliminates access to all meaningful political organization. Carbone and Cassani (2016) appropriately describe the result as “democratic progress by elections”: peaceful opposition victory changed actors' expectations about the possibility of alternation, but it did not by itself consolidate liberal democracy.

Participation data reinforce this qualified interpretation. International IDEA reports registered-voter turnout of 40.69 percent in the 1999 House election, 43.65 percent in 2015, and 32.14 percent in 2019. For 2023, the EU Election Observation Mission reported turnout of approximately 27 percent of registered voters in the presidential election. The series is not perfectly uniform—the first three observations refer to parliamentary elections held alongside national contests, while the 2023 figure refers to the presidential contest—but it shows that uninterrupted elections have not generated steadily increasing participation (International IDEA Voter Turnout Database; European Union Election Observation Mission, 2023).

Turnout alone cannot identify why citizens abstained or establish whether an election was free and fair. It may reflect distrust, insecurity, registration problems, the perceived quality of candidates, or different mobilization incentives. Still, the decline matters because the social authorization delivered by elections has narrowed even while electoral succession has become routine. The EU mission found that the 2023 contests occurred on schedule but that failures in planning, communication, and result transparency reduced public confidence. The election also weakened the appearance of a closed two-party contest: the Labour Party and New Nigeria Peoples Party demonstrated that regional and urban constituencies could be mobilized outside the APC-PDP axis. This was pluralization, but not necessarily party institutionalization (European Union Election Observation Mission, 2023).

Recent scholarship explains the distinction. Weak ideological differentiation makes Nigerian parties vehicles for elite access and patronage more than durable policy alternatives. Weak internal democracy, elite capture, and limited grassroots organization also reduce the capacity of parties to aggregate interests and channel grievances, so competition can coexist with voter disengagement, defections, violence, and extra-institutional mobilization. Research on state elections adds an important qualification: personalistic and patronage-based parties weaken representation, yet valuable gubernatorial offices also keep subnational competition meaningful and provide opposition parties with bases from which to survive a national defeat (Angerbrandt, 2020; Babalola, 2024; Madueke, 2026). Federalism therefore supports contestation and reproduces localized elite control at the same time.

Comparative democracy measures should be read in this light. V-Dem's 2026 Democracy Report places Nigeria on the democratic side of its electoral-democracy “grey zone” for 2025 and in the lower half of countries on the Liberal Democracy Index. These are expert-coded diagnostic classifications, not direct observations of electoral administration. Their value lies in confirming that Nigeria combines competitive electoral institutions with uncertain liberal constraints; they do not establish which agency or event caused the classification (V-Dem Institute, 2026). Nigeria also differs from several West African states that experienced military coups after 2020 because constitutional civilian succession continued. At the same time, Liberia's opposition turnover in 2023 demonstrates why succession within the same governing party and opposition alternation should not be treated as equivalent tests of competition.

The resulting political order is resilient against outright closure but weakly institutionalized. Nigeria has replaced military succession with regular civilian elections, blocked at least one tenure-extension effort, and demonstrated that an incumbent party can lose. It has not yet converted those achievements into consistently transparent election administration, socially rooted parties, or strong programmatic accountability. Policy design should therefore protect the mechanisms that made alternation possible rather than concentrate only on new election technology. INEC should maintain a public implementation record linking observer and court findings to responsible units, deadlines, and tested corrections. Party regulation should require verifiable membership registers, transparent primary results, audited campaign and party finance, and public records of mergers and defections. Federal and state electoral reforms should preserve subnational arenas in which opposition can organize while constraining governors' control over party nominations and local electoral bodies. These measures follow directly from the historical diagnosis: democratic durability in Nigeria depends not simply on holding the next election, but on turning recurrent competition into organizations and procedures that survive particular elites.

4.2 Executive Power and Government Formation

Nigeria's presidency is constitutionally concentrated but politically bargained and administratively distributed. Section 5 of the 1999 Constitution vests federal executive power in the president, who is also head of state, chief executive, and commander-in-chief. Yet the office is not won or exercised as a purely personal mandate. A presidential candidate must combine the highest national vote with a constitutionally prescribed territorial spread; a running mate is elected on the same ticket; ministers require Senate confirmation; and the cabinet must include at least one indigene of every state. Government formation is therefore a continuing exercise in assembling territorial legitimacy, political support, and administrative competence rather than a single appointment event (Federal Republic of Nigeria, 1999).

These rules constitutionalize inclusion, but inclusion and coordination are not equivalent. A 36-state minimum creates a large cabinet before the president adds positions needed for coalition management or policy specialization. It can reassure regions that they have a voice at the centre, yet it also creates incentives to treat a ministerial post as a state's distributive entitlement. The relevant trade-off is not simply “politicians versus technocrats.” Semenova and Orji's minister-level data for 1999–2017 show that approximately half of federal ministers had no prior political-office experience, often including party-affiliated technocrats; about one-third had held party leadership positions, 34 percent of all ministers had previously been active only at state level, and only 5 percent of politically experienced ministers came solely from federal-level positions. These descriptive results do not measure ministerial performance, and the observation period ends in 2017. They do show that Nigerian cabinets join expertise, party affiliation, state-level careers, and territorial claims rather than following a single recruitment ladder (Semenova & Orji, 2025).

The Senate's role could convert that diversity requirement into a capability check, but only if confirmation examines suitability for a defined portfolio. The 2023 constitutional alteration addressed one bottleneck by requiring presidents and governors to transmit nominees within 60 days and by requiring evidence of asset declaration before confirmation. The Policy and Legal Advocacy Centre nevertheless identifies a recurrent weakness in practice: deferential “bow and go” screening can bypass serious questions about qualifications, conflicts, and fit. The amendment therefore improves the timing of nomination, not necessarily the quality of selection. Speed without portfolio-specific scrutiny can produce a formally constituted cabinet whose responsibilities remain unclear; scrutiny without a deadline can leave ministries politically unattended. Both dimensions must be evaluated (Policy and Legal Advocacy Centre, 2023).

Government formation also extends beyond Abuja's cabinet. Governors control substantial subnational administrations, influence party and candidate networks, and possess their own electoral mandates. Their collective constitutional venue is the National Economic Council (NEC), chaired by the vice-president and comprising all 36 governors and the Central Bank governor, which advises the president on coordinating the federation's economic programmes. This design recognizes that many national outcomes depend on state action. It also exposes a structural accountability problem. The World Bank's 2026 diagnostic finds that the federal system has helped hold together a politically fractious union, while fragmented responsibilities across tiers have impeded policy alignment and obscured responsibility for services such as basic education and primary health care. A presidential announcement is consequently not evidence of delivery when finance, personnel, or implementation authority lies partly with states (World Bank, 2026; Suberu, 2022).

Nor does control of a broad-based governing party remove the need for bargaining. Research covering the 2015–2019 8th and 2019–2023 9th National Assemblies, based on a survey of 119 federal legislators, 23 interviews with legislators, and additional interviews and archival work, finds that Nigeria's federation-wide parties played a limited role in organizing intergroup cooperation inside the National Assembly. Ethno-regional balancing of leadership and committee positions was more important to legislative accommodation (Demarest & Langer, 2023). The finding concerns parliament rather than cabinet performance, but it clarifies the executive's coordination environment: a shared party label does not automatically yield a coherent policy majority. Budgets, bills, appointments, and distributive demands must still be negotiated with legislators and regional blocs. This bargaining can stabilize a diverse federation, but it can also blur whether an appropriation serves an agreed national programme or the maintenance of a political coalition.

The practical consequence is selective rather than uniformly weak executive capacity. Usman's political-settlements analysis shows that the same post-military order enabled successful telecommunications liberalization while obstructing reform in the oil sector because the distribution of power and external constraints differed across sectors. More recent evidence is consistent with that mechanism rather than with a simple “strong” or “weak” presidency label: the World Bank records rapid macroeconomic reforms after May 2023, but also slow and uneven social-protection rollout, limited policy coordination, and weak budget transparency. Formal presidential authority can initiate a decision; implementation depends on whether affected ministers, agencies, legislators, governors, and organized interests can be aligned around it (Usman, 2020; World Bank, 2026).

Reform should therefore make the coalition-to-delivery chain observable. Before inauguration, a statutory transition process should produce a public inventory of pending decisions, fiscal commitments, and intergovernmental obligations. Nominees should be transmitted with proposed portfolios, qualification and conflict-of-interest records, and a short statement connecting each appointment to published government priorities; the Senate should publish portfolio-specific questions, documents reviewed, and reasons for confirmation or rejection. Once constituted, the Federal Executive Council should assign each cross-ministerial outcome one lead minister, named contributing agencies, a baseline, deadline, and financing source. NEC agreements that require state implementation should record which tier funds, executes, reports, and corrects failure, while respecting the Council's advisory status. These measures would preserve territorial representation and legitimate political bargaining. Their purpose is to prevent inclusion from becoming a substitute for capability and to prevent presidential initiative from being mistaken for implemented policy.

4.3 Parliament, Parties, and Elections

Nigeria's electoral system does more than choose officeholders: it determines which social interests can enter the legislature and whether citizens can trace votes into public decisions. The 1999 Constitution assigns federal legislative power to a bicameral National Assembly. The Senate has 109 prescribed seats—three for each state and one for the Federal Capital Territory—while the House of Representatives has 360 single-member constituencies. The arrangement deliberately combines territorial equality in the Senate with population-sensitive representation in the House (Federal Republic of Nigeria, 1999). Yet formal apportionment is only the first link in a longer representation chain. Party nomination determines who can compete; election administration determines whether votes are credibly recorded; electoral rules convert votes into seats; and parliamentary organization determines whether members can legislate and scrutinize the executive.

The first important bottleneck therefore occurs before election day. Section 84 of the Electoral Act 2022 permits political parties to select candidates through direct, indirect, or consensus procedures (Federal Republic of Nigeria, 2022). In practice, the value of each procedure depends on verifiable membership or delegate registers, transparent voting and enforceable appeals. The European Union Election Observation Mission (EU EOM) found that the 2023 party primaries were frequently costly and opaque; more than 1,200 pre-election court cases concerned candidacy disputes. This is not a marginal procedural problem. Where a party is dominant in a constituency, control over its nomination can be more decisive than the general election. Angerbrandt's study of the 2019 state elections similarly shows that national party labels conceal substantial subnational variation in competition and opposition organization (Angerbrandt, 2020). Candidate selection thus links local patronage networks to the composition of the federal legislature.

Political finance amplifies this gatekeeping effect. A 2024 Policy and Legal Advocacy Centre (PLAC) study, based partly on 22 interviews with politicians and party actors, found that personal resources were commonly the principal source of campaign finance and that respondents often regarded nomination contests as especially expensive. The small, non-representative interview sample cannot establish the prevalence or average cost of these practices. It nevertheless identifies a plausible mechanism consistent with the observed outcome: high entry costs and discretionary party support can screen out aspirants before voters encounter them (Policy and Legal Advocacy Centre, 2024). The mechanism is particularly consequential for women because parties that nominated more women were often electorally weaker, whereas the four leading parties each fielded no more than 10 percent women across the contests assessed by the EU EOM.

The 2023 figures locate attrition at successive stages rather than treating underrepresentation as a single election-day result (European Union Election Observation Mission, 2023; Inter-Parliamentary Union, 2023).

This funnel changes the diagnosis. Low numbers of women elected cannot be attributed only to voter preferences because the candidate pool had already narrowed sharply. Nor does a higher number of parties necessarily imply meaningful choice: eighteen parties contested the 2023 general elections, but organizational reach, access to finance and local competitiveness were highly unequal (European Union Election Observation Mission, 2023). Measures confined to campaign training after nomination therefore arrive too late. The relevant policy target is access to viable party nominations, especially in constituencies where a party label is strongly advantaged.

The second bottleneck lies between votes cast and results accepted. The Electoral Act requires voter verification and accreditation using a smart card reader or another technological device prescribed by the Independent National Electoral Commission (INEC), providing the legal basis for the Biometric Voter Accreditation System (BVAS). BVAS was intended to constrain multiple accreditation, while the INEC Result Viewing portal (IReV) was intended to let parties and citizens compare polling-unit records. These tools perform different functions, and neither replaces the signed paper result and statutory collation process. Technology can reduce one form of discretion while creating new dependencies on configuration, staff training, connectivity, upload protocols, exception handling and public communication.

The 2023 experience demonstrates why legal design and implementation must be evaluated separately. The EU EOM reported that more than one quarter of presidential polling-unit result forms were still absent from IReV when the presidential result was declared; by the end of March, more than 5 percent remained missing. These are presidential-process figures and should not be misreported as legislative turnout or National Assembly result completeness. Their relevance here is institutional: a portal presented as a transparency mechanism cannot sustain confidence unless users can observe completeness, timing, corrections and the reconciliation of digital images with official collation records (European Union Election Observation Mission, 2023). The evidence documents a transparency failure, but it does not by itself establish that a different candidate would have won.

The final link is what elected legislators do with their mandate. The Fourth Republic National Assembly should not be reduced either to an executive rubber stamp or to a fully institutionalized counterweight. Using member-level data from the Eighth National Assembly (2015–2019), Demarest found that membership in committees offering access to resource-rich ministries and agencies was positively associated with sponsorship of bills and motions. Some initiatives were self-serving or created new distributive opportunities, while others strengthened accountability or the rule of law (Demarest, 2021). This apparently contradictory result matters: legislative activity and prebendal incentives can coexist. Bill counts alone are therefore an incomplete performance measure; analysis also requires the subject, disposition, implementation and public value of legislative initiatives.

Reform should follow the representation chain. Parties should publish machine-readable membership or delegate registers before primaries, timestamped polling-station or delegate results afterward, candidate-selection expenditures and reasons for substitutions; internal appeals should have deadlines that conclude before ballot preparation. Gender-inclusion measures should operate at nomination, with transparent placement or nomination rules for viable constituencies and finance support subject to disclosure, rather than relying only on post-nomination appeals to voters. INEC should publish a polling-unit completeness dashboard, upload timestamps, correction histories, cancellation reasons and accreditation-result reconciliation files in reusable formats. Parliament, in turn, should disclose committee membership, attendance, recorded votes, amendments, bill disposition and follow-up on enacted laws so that voters can assess representation after election day. These measures do not eliminate contestation. They make each stage auditable and prepare the question taken up in the next section: whether courts, audit bodies and anti-corruption institutions can convert documented violations into timely and credible accountability.

4.4 Judiciary, Rule of Law, Corruption, and Accountability

Nigeria possesses a dense formal accountability system. Courts review official acts and election disputes; the National Judicial Council participates in judicial appointments and discipline; the Auditor-General examines public accounts; legislative public-accounts committees conduct oversight; the Economic and Financial Crimes Commission and Independent Corrupt Practices and Other Related Offences Commission investigate corruption; and the Bureau of Public Procurement regulates federal procurement. The existence of many bodies is institutionally significant, but it should not be confused with an effective accountability chain.

The chain can fail at several points. Investigators may lack timely access to financial records; prosecutors may face weak case preparation or delay; courts may carry heavy dockets; audit findings may reach parliament late; sanctions may be inconsistent; and recovered assets may not be transparently traced. Overlapping mandates can encourage cooperation, but they can also allow agencies to shift responsibility or compete over politically salient cases. Formal independence is similarly incomplete if appointments, operating budgets, or enforcement assistance remain vulnerable to executive control.

Public attitudes help identify legitimacy pressures but do not directly measure legal performance. Afrobarometer's 2024 Nigeria survey found that 80 percent of respondents said corruption had increased “somewhat” or “a lot” during the preceding year. That is evidence of public perception, not an audited estimate of the number or monetary value of corrupt transactions. It should be read alongside administrative measures such as audit-query resolution, procurement competition, case duration, conviction quality, asset recovery, and compliance with court orders (Afrobarometer, 2025).

The same caution applies to international governance indicators. The World Bank's Worldwide Governance Indicators aggregate perceptions from multiple sources and report uncertainty around estimates. They are useful for broad comparison and trend screening, but they cannot identify which Nigerian agency failed or whether a particular reform caused a change. A processed series without a documented edition, raw source, plausible scale, and reproducible transformation should not be reported as a fact. For this reason, this report does not reproduce the earlier local WGI values until their provenance is rebuilt against the World Bank's documented methodology (World Bank WGI methodology).

Two interpretations of persistent corruption deserve separation. A capacity account emphasizes weak records, salaries, procurement systems, investigation, and court administration. A political-accountability account emphasizes selective enforcement, patronage, and the protection of powerful networks. Capacity-building alone will disappoint if enforcement remains selective; institutional independence alone will disappoint if agencies cannot assemble admissible evidence or process cases promptly. Reform must address both mechanisms.

Nigeria should publish an end-to-end accountability dashboard using case cohorts rather than publicity counts. It should trace audit findings through legislative review, referral, investigation, prosecution, judgment, appeal, recovery, and administrative correction, while protecting due process and personal data. Procurement reporting should disclose tender participation, beneficial ownership where lawful, contract modification, completion, and payment. Judicial statistics should include age of pending cases and time to disposition, disaggregated by court and case type. Finally, agency heads and judicial bodies need transparent appointment criteria, protected operating appropriations, reasoned disciplinary decisions, and enforceable conflict-of-interest rules. These measures would make accountability observable without treating allegation, arrest, charge, conviction, and recovery as equivalent outcomes.

4.5 Local Politics and Real Power Relations

Local government is constitutionally recognized as Nigeria's third tier, but real local power is produced through the interaction of councils, governors, state assemblies, State Independent Electoral Commissions (SIECs), parties, traditional authorities, security actors, and federal transfers. This makes local politics an institutional test: it shows whether formal decentralization creates accountable proximity or simply relocates patronage.

State governments have decisive leverage. State law structures councils, SIECs conduct local elections, and State Joint Local Government Accounts historically mediated fiscal flows. Governors and state assemblies have also used caretaker or transition committees where elected councils were absent or dissolved. Olaniyi's study describes SIECs as frequently “independent in name,” noting delayed elections, caretaker arrangements, and overwhelming victories for the governing party in many state-administered contests (Olaniyi, 2017). This pattern does not prove that every local result is fraudulent; it identifies structural conditions under which the organization of competition is controlled by actors with a direct stake in its outcome.

Fiscal dependence reinforces political dependence. Councils charged with primary services often possess weak own-source revenue, uncertain personnel systems, and limited procurement capacity. Amah argues that constitutional ambiguity and state control have constrained local autonomy despite formal recognition of local government (Amah, 2018). The July 2024 Supreme Court judgment altered this relationship by supporting direct federal allocations to democratically elected councils and rejecting practices that diverted or withheld their funds. The legal change is consequential, but financial autonomy is not equivalent to democratic or administrative autonomy. A directly funded unelected council would still lack electoral legitimacy; an elected council without audit and delivery capacity could misuse funds independently.

Traditional rulers and community institutions complicate the picture. They may provide dispute resolution, local knowledge, and social legitimacy where formal administration is thin. They can also reproduce exclusion or become incorporated into partisan competition. Uniform abolition or uncritical incorporation would miss this variation. The appropriate question is which functions they perform, whose rights are affected, how decisions can be appealed, and how their public role is financed.

Local reform should therefore be evaluated as a bundle. First, electoral autonomy requires fixed election calendars, public polling-unit results, professional SIEC appointments, judicial review, and sanctions for unlawful caretaker government. Second, fiscal autonomy requires direct and disclosed transfers, local budgets, competitive procurement, payroll controls, and independent audit. Third, administrative autonomy requires a competent local service, clear assignments, interoperable financial systems, and authority over appropriate personnel. Fourth, social accountability requires open council meetings, ward-level project disclosure, complaint channels, and inclusion of women, youth, migrants, and minority communities.

The best political diagnostic is not whether money bypasses the state treasury, but whether residents can answer four questions: Was the council elected? How much did it receive and raise? What was it legally responsible for? What services and assets resulted? Publishing those answers for every council would expose variation that national averages conceal. It would also make the 2024 judgment a platform for accountable local government rather than merely another redistribution of control (Supreme Court judgment, 2024). The focus here is political control—elections, parties, governors and informal authority. Section 5.5 separately tests the administrative chain through which a lawful council converts transfers, staff and procurement into a verifiable service and remedy.

4.6 Political System Diagnostics and Democratic Trajectory

Nigeria's democratic trajectory cannot be represented responsibly by one score. Elections, representation, public attitudes, rule of law, administrative effectiveness, and political violence measure different objects with different error structures. A sound diagnostic therefore triangulates them and preserves disagreement rather than averaging them into an apparently precise index.

The table identifies a mixed regime pattern. Nigeria has sustained civilian rule since 1999 and achieved opposition alternation at the presidential level in 2015, demonstrating that incumbent defeat is possible. At the same time, very low 2023 turnout and severe underrepresentation of women indicate shallow participation and unequal access to office. Afrobarometer's finding that 64 percent prefer democracy shows that majority normative support persists, but it should not be read as satisfaction with democratic performance. The simultaneous perception that corruption increased helps explain why attachment to democracy can coexist with distrust of governing institutions.

Expert-coded measures such as V-Dem can track concepts including electoral democracy and liberal constraints across long periods. Perception composites such as the World Bank's WGI can compare broad governance dimensions across countries. Citizen surveys reveal experienced attitudes; election returns document administrative participation; conflict-event datasets record reported incidents. None is a substitute for the others. Expert scores may embed coding uncertainty, international composites are not agency diagnostics, surveys have sampling and wording effects, registers can be inaccurate, and event data depend on reporting coverage. The earlier processed WGI file in this project is therefore quarantined from the chapter: its edition and raw lineage were not adequately archived and some values were outside the expected scale. Reproducibility is a substantive condition for evidence, not a technical afterthought.

The competing interpretations are consequential. One view describes Nigeria as resilient because elections recur, opposition can win, federal institutions diffuse power, and courts remain politically relevant. Another describes a democracy constrained by administrative failures, money and violence, weakly programmatic parties, selective accountability, and insecurity. These accounts test different thresholds. The first asks whether authoritarian closure has been avoided; the second asks whether citizens can exercise equal, informed, and consequential political choice. The evidence supports resilience against closure, but not complacency about democratic quality.

Policy should use a multidimensional annual scorecard rather than a synthetic ranking. Electoral integrity should track polling-unit completeness, result reconciliation, litigation, and turnout by sex, age, disability, and territory. Representation should track nominations and elected office. Accountability should follow audit findings and cases through final disposition. Rule of law should report case delay and judgment compliance. Security should separate civilian harm, displacement, and election-related violence. Survey attitudes should include confidence intervals and questionnaire wording. Each series must carry a source, edition, denominator, update date, and transformation script. Improvement should be claimed only when several indicators linked to the same mechanism move consistently. This approach turns democratic diagnosis from a contest over labels into a reviewable account of where institutions are becoming more competitive, inclusive, lawful, and capable—and where they are not. Chapter 5 then moves from these political conditions to the administrative mechanisms that convert authoritative choices into staffed, financed, delivered and correctable transactions.
