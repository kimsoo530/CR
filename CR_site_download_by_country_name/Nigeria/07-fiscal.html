7.1 Government Revenue, Taxation, and the Federation Account

Nigeria's fiscal problem is not simply that government spends more than it collects. It is that the state must finance extensive federal, state, and local responsibilities from a revenue base that remains small, volatile, and unevenly administered. Oil and gas receipts, federally collected taxes, state internally generated revenue (IGR), and transfers through the Federation Account follow different rules and respond to different shocks. The central question is therefore whether recent revenue gains represent a durable expansion of fiscal capacity or a temporary improvement produced by exchange-rate valuation, oil-sector receipts, and exceptional transfers.

A revenue recovery that remains structurally narrow

The most recent IMF accounts show a clear recovery from the low point of the early 2020s, but also warn against treating that recovery as complete. Consolidated-government revenue and grants increased from 6.6 percent of GDP in 2022 to 10.8 percent in 2024. Non-oil revenue accounted for most of the increase, rising from 3.5 to 6.8 percent of GDP. Expenditure nevertheless remained above revenue, and the IMF estimates that consolidated revenue slipped to 10.2 percent of GDP in 2025 while expenditure remained at 12.0 percent. More importantly, the financing-based deficit reached 4.4 percent of GDP in 2025, well above the 1.8 percent above-the-line deficit, because the accounts contained an estimated statistical discrepancy of 2.7 percent of GDP. The discrepancy may capture spending outside the reporting perimeter and incomplete reconciliation across fiscal institutions; it is therefore a problem of fiscal information and control, not merely an accounting footnote (International Monetary Fund, 2026).

This improvement should also be distinguished from tax deepening. On the internationally comparable definition used in *Revenue Statistics in Africa 2025*, Nigeria's tax-to-GDP ratio was 8.2 percent in 2023, compared with an unweighted average of 16.1 percent across the 38 African countries covered. Nigeria's ratio was almost unchanged from 8.3 percent in 2013, whereas the African average rose by 1.4 percentage points over the decade (OECD/AUC/ATAF, 2025). The OECD tax measure is not interchangeable with the IMF's broader revenue-and-grants series: the former counts compulsory unrequited payments and has its own subnational coverage, while the latter also includes oil and other non-tax revenue. Read together, however, they show that recent aggregate revenue gains have not yet erased Nigeria's long-standing dependence on a narrow set of sources.

The composition of the 2024 increase reinforces that interpretation. World Bank calculations attribute the rise to the removal of the implicit foreign-exchange subsidy, improved tax administration, and stronger remittance of revenue by government-owned entities. Gross receipts collected by the principal Federation revenue agencies rose from ₦16.5 trillion in 2023 to ₦29.5 trillion in 2024. Yet gross collection is not the amount available for budgets: 43.5 percent of the 2024 total, or ₦12.8 trillion, was deducted before distribution, including collection costs, refunds, transfers, and other deductions. The same diagnostic found that NNPCL remitted only ₦0.6 trillion to FAAC in 2024, down from ₦1.1 trillion in 2023, while fuel-subsidy and arrears claims complicated the passage from oil receipts to usable public revenue (World Bank, 2025d). This is why claims based on gross agency collections, net Federation revenue, consolidated government revenue, and tax revenue can all be numerically different without one necessarily being false. Transparent reconciliation among them is itself a measure of fiscal capacity.

The Federation Account transmits both resources and risk

Section 162 of the Constitution places most federally collected revenue in the Federation Account for distribution among the three tiers of government. Under the subsisting vertical formula for net distributable Federation Account revenue excluding VAT, the federal government receives 52.68 percent, states 26.72 percent, and local governments 20.60 percent; oil-producing states also receive the constitutionally required 13 percent derivation from natural-resource revenue. VAT follows a separate sharing rule (National Bureau of Statistics, 2020). This system performs a necessary equalization function in a federation where taxable economic activity and administrative capacity are concentrated geographically. At the same time, it separates many subnational spending responsibilities from the taxes paid directly to the government delivering the service. The political and developmental consequences of centrally collected petroleum revenue and negotiated allocation rules have been identified for decades: the core problem is not transfer sharing by itself, but the combination of vertical fiscal imbalance, unstable resource revenue, and allocation rules whose implementation and development effects are politically contested (Ekpo, 1994; Phillips, 1991).

Recent state data show both sides of this arrangement. For the 34 states with complete quarterly budget implementation reports, total revenue increased from ₦7.2 trillion in 2023 to ₦13.9 trillion in 2024. FAAC transfers rose from ₦4.8 trillion to ₦9.5 trillion, while IGR reached ₦2.9 trillion. Oil-producing states captured a disproportionate share of the transfer windfall through derivation and oil-related refunds (World Bank, 2025d). The national IGR distribution was already highly concentrated before that windfall: the NBS recorded ₦2.43 trillion for the 36 states and the FCT in 2023, of which Lagos alone generated ₦815.86 billion, roughly one-third (National Bureau of Statistics, 2024). The contrast does not imply that every state should reproduce Lagos. Lagos combines a large formal payroll, dense property and consumption bases, and sustained investment in revenue administration that most states do not possess. It instead shows why a uniform demand for “more IGR” is analytically weak: the feasible tax base, administrative cost, and risk of coercive multiple taxation vary greatly across states.

Transfer dependence can weaken the incentive to build a taxpayer register and a visible fiscal contract, but the relationship is not automatic. Lagos research finds that linking direct taxation to recognizable service delivery can strengthen the perceived exchange between citizens and government, although the effect varies across social groups (Gatt & Owen, 2018). A recent field experiment in 112 Lagos markets further found that appeals delivered by state revenue officials sometimes increased registration and payment, whereas messages delivered by market associations did not, despite the associations' embedded local position (Gottlieb et al., 2025). Research in informal settlements adds an important qualification: trust was associated with tax morale but not necessarily with actual payment; ability to pay, legal liability, and community organization also mattered (Rosenzweig & Wilson, 2023). These studies are not nationally representative templates, but they establish a mechanism that aggregate revenue ratios cannot reveal. Administrative legibility, credible enforcement, service experience, and taxpayer treatment jointly shape compliance.

From legal consolidation to measurable collection capacity

The four Tax Acts signed in June 2025 and implemented from 2026 attempt to address fragmentation at the national level. The Nigeria Tax Act consolidates substantive tax rules; the Nigeria Tax Administration Act creates a more uniform administrative framework; the Nigeria Revenue Service (Establishment) Act replaces FIRS with the Nigeria Revenue Service; and the Joint Revenue Board (Establishment) Act formalizes coordination across federal, state, and local revenue authorities (State House, 2025). Transition guidelines apply the Nigeria Tax Act from 1 January 2026 and preserve pre-2026 liabilities under the former rules, thereby limiting retrospective application (Federal Ministry of Finance, 2026). A February 2026 executive order also directed specified oil and gas payments under production-sharing contracts to be remitted directly to the Federation Account, bypassing NNPCL (State House, 2026).

These changes improve the legal architecture, but enacted coordination is not yet demonstrated administrative integration. The IMF reports that the new package contains both revenue-raising measures and deliberate relief: the authorities estimated gains of 3.9 percent of GDP from policy changes and 3.1 percent from administration over three years, offset by 2.4 percent of GDP from expanded input credits, zero rating, and thresholds, for a net projected gain of 4.6 percent. These are ex ante estimates, not collected revenue. The IMF accordingly treats the yield from fiscalization, e-invoicing, Tax ID consolidation, and data cross-checking conservatively, while warning that additional rate or base changes should be sequenced with a funded and functioning cash-transfer system so that adjustment does not fall disproportionately on poor households (International Monetary Fund, 2026).

The appropriate policy test is therefore narrower and more demanding than a headline tax-to-GDP target. First, petroleum receipts, statutory deductions, refunds, and final distributions should be published in a reconciled chain so that Parliament, states, and citizens can trace gross collection into spendable revenue. Second, the new revenue institutions should be judged by operational measures—unique and validated Tax IDs, filing-to-payment gaps, e-invoice matching, audit yield net of reversals, dispute-resolution time, and the incidence of duplicate or unauthorized levies—rather than registrations or assessments alone. Third, subnational reform should combine national interoperability with tax instruments suited to local bases, especially well-administered personal-income and property taxation, while protecting informal and low-income taxpayers from repeated collection by overlapping authorities. In Nigeria's institutional setting, fiscal capacity will be strengthened not when every tier maximizes extraction independently, but when revenue assignment, transfer predictability, taxpayer rights, and visible service obligations reinforce one another.

7.2 Fiscal Balance, Debt, and Interest Burden

Nigeria's debt problem is easily misdiagnosed. Its public-debt ratio is not exceptionally high by the standards of many emerging economies, yet interest payments absorb an unusually large share of federal revenue. The apparent contradiction disappears once the numerator, denominator, and institutional pathway are separated. Debt sustainability depends not only on the stock of liabilities relative to GDP, but also on the revenue actually controlled by the Federal Government of Nigeria (FGN), the currency and maturity composition of borrowing, the credibility of the budget, and whether borrowed funds become productive assets or recurrent and off-budget obligations. Nigeria's binding constraint is therefore better described as a revenue-and-execution problem expressed through debt service than as a debt-stock problem alone.

A moderate debt ratio can coexist with a severe interest burden

The IMF's latest internally consistent fiscal accounts illustrate this distinction. Consolidated-government financing needs rose from 2.4 percent of GDP in 2024 to an estimated 4.4 percent in 2025, even though the above-the-line deficit was only 1.8 percent. The difference was an estimated statistical discrepancy of 2.7 percent of GDP, which the IMF considers consistent with expenditure not yet captured by the Office of the Accountant General of the Federation. At the same time, public gross debt fell from 39.3 to 36.1 percent of GDP. That decline did not represent a comparable reduction in the nominal debt stock or in annual budget pressure: it reflected a favorable interest-growth differential, naira appreciation, and a 34 percent upward revision to 2024 nominal GDP following rebasing. In contrast, FGN interest payments rose from 40.8 to 53.2 percent of FGN revenue (International Monetary Fund, 2026).

This is why a debt-to-GDP threshold alone is an unreliable policy guide. The denominator contains economic activity that the federal tax system captures only weakly, whereas interest must be paid from much narrower cash revenue. Nigeria-specific time-series studies also do not support a simple proposition that all borrowing is growth-reducing. Yusuf and Mohd (2021) find different short- and long-run effects for domestic and external debt, while Onuoha (2025) finds that public spending partly mediates the relationship between domestic debt and growth. Oyadeyi et al. (2024) identify nonlinear relationships whose estimated thresholds vary substantially when debt is divided by GDP, revenue, or exports. These studies use historical aggregate data and cannot identify the effect of a particular recent loan or reform; their value here is more limited but still important. They show that the fiscal denominator, financing source, and use of proceeds matter. More generally, a negative debt-growth correlation can disappear after addressing endogeneity, warning against treating weak growth as proof that debt caused it (Panizza & Presbitero, 2014).

The relevant Nigerian mechanism is thus fiscal compression. When more than half of FGN revenue is pre-empted by interest, personnel and other statutory obligations become difficult to adjust quickly, leaving capital expenditure and non-salary service inputs as residual buffers. In 2025, the oil-revenue shortfall was partly accommodated through under-execution of reported federal capital spending. The World Bank similarly finds a marked federal-subnational asymmetry: interest and wages dominate the federal expenditure structure, while states devote a larger reported share to capital expenditure, although state execution and project quality remain heterogeneous (World Bank, 2026). Debt service therefore affects development less through an abstract national debt ceiling than through the annual cash sequence that determines which authorized services and projects are actually funded.

The stock conceals currency, refinancing, and accounting risks

The Debt Management Office (DMO) supplies the nominal portfolio view that the GDP ratio cannot. At 31 March 2026, total public debt reported by the DMO was ₦159.35 trillion (US$114.95 billion): domestic debt accounted for 54.85 percent and external debt for 45.15 percent. FGN domestic obligations represented 52.01 percent of the total portfolio, while states and the Federal Capital Territory represented 2.84 percent (Debt Management Office, 2026a). These are point-in-time debt-stock shares, not measures of the consolidated deficit or interest burden. They nevertheless reveal that debt strategy must manage two distinct exposures. Domestic issuance reduces direct foreign-currency risk but can be expensive when monetary policy is tight and refinancing needs are concentrated. External borrowing can offer longer maturities or concessional terms, but commercial foreign-currency debt transmits exchange-rate depreciation and global risk premia into naira-denominated fiscal costs.

Actual 2025 service payments show the price of that composition. DMO records ₦8.24 trillion in interest on FGN domestic debt and ₦8.61 trillion in total domestic debt service after including promissory-note principal. Treasury bills alone generated ₦2.55 trillion in interest, and FGN bonds generated ₦5.49 trillion. External public debt service totaled US$5.15 billion, comprising US$3.06 billion in principal, US$2.03 billion in interest, and US$59 million in other charges; commercial creditors accounted for almost half of the total, mainly Eurobonds (Debt Management Office, 2026b, 2026c). The domestic and external totals cannot be added without a dated exchange rate, and neither equals the IMF's accrual-based interest ratio. Read separately, however, they show simultaneous exposure to high local-currency carrying costs and foreign-currency refinancing.

Portfolio risk also extends beyond conventional bonds and loans. The 2025 deficit was financed through domestic securities, a Eurobond, syndicated infrastructure loans, and a CBN deposit drawdown equal to 1.1 percent of GDP. The IMF judges the liquidity effect of that drawdown to be similar to the earlier *Ways and Means* overdraft financing. It also warns that collateralized or otherwise complex financing instruments can create margin-call and contingent-liability risks that ordinary debt-stock tables may not reveal (International Monetary Fund, 2026). The fiscal risk register must therefore cover CBN-related financing, guarantees, state-owned enterprises, oil-backed or collateralized facilities, electricity-sector arrears, and public-private partnerships—not merely securities formally issued under the DMO mandate.

Exchange-rate valuation illustrates why transparency about composition is indispensable. A naira depreciation increases the domestic-currency value and service cost of foreign-currency debt even if no new dollar is borrowed; appreciation produces the reverse. Yet a favorable valuation movement or GDP rebasing can lower the reported debt-to-GDP ratio without releasing cash for hospitals, schools, security, or infrastructure. Conversely, domestic borrowing avoids that mechanical currency translation but may raise interest expense and deepen the sovereign-bank nexus when banks and institutional investors hold more government paper. The policy objective should not be to maximize either domestic or external borrowing. It should be to minimize cost subject to refinancing, currency, market-access, and contingent-liability risks while preserving credit and fiscal space for productive activity.

Budget credibility determines whether borrowing creates assets or arrears

Nigeria's debt dynamics begin before a bond is issued. Khalid's review of federal Budget Implementation Reports for 2011–2023 finds persistent optimism in oil-production and revenue forecasts and systematic under-execution of capital budgets. Forecast errors weaken the budget as a binding statement of policy because approved projects exceed the cash envelope that realistic revenue can support (Khalid, 2025). The World Bank's public finance review reaches the same institutional diagnosis for an earlier period: recurrent expenditure was protected more consistently than capital expenditure, while revenue shortfalls and delays made capital spending the de facto adjustment item. It estimates that the FGN deficit exceeded its budgeted level by an average of 1.4 percent of GDP from 2016 to 2021 (World Bank, 2022). These historical findings remain relevant because the 2025 accounts again combine an oil-revenue shortfall, capital under-execution, and a large financing-to-operations discrepancy.

The mechanism is cumulative. An optimistic revenue baseline permits an expansive appropriation; cash shortfalls then delay or ration releases; projects are extended, repriced, or moved outside the ordinary budget; and unrecorded commitments or exceptional financing enlarge future obligations. The January 2026 repeal-and-re-enactment legislation incorporated some previously executed expenditures into the 2024 and 2025 budgets after the fact. That may improve legal coverage, but ex post regularization is not equivalent to ex ante authorization, commitment control, and timely reconciliation. A 2.7 percent-of-GDP statistical discrepancy is consequently not just a measurement nuisance. It weakens the government's ability to know its true financing requirement, compare projects, enforce borrowing limits, and explain why approved services were not delivered (International Monetary Fund, 2026).

Policy should follow this causal sequence. First, fiscal reports should reconcile appropriation, commitment, cash release, payment, and financing on the same institutional perimeter, with a bridge between above-the-line operations and below-the-line financing. Second, the macro-fiscal unit should publish forecast-error histories and use conservative oil-production, price, exchange-rate, and tax-yield assumptions; major revisions should trigger a transparent supplementary budget rather than parallel execution. Third, each new borrowing proposal should disclose currency, effective interest cost, maturity and refinancing profile, collateral, guarantees, and the project or liability financed. Fourth, debt strategy should lengthen and smooth maturities where cost permits, favor concessional external finance for projects able to meet appraisal and procurement standards, and avoid instruments whose collateral or margin calls constrain later fiscal and exchange-rate choices. Finally, capital and social spending should be protected by selecting a smaller set of appraised projects and essential service commitments that can be financed through the medium-term cash envelope. The test is not the size of the approved capital budget but the share completed on time, within cost, and with a verified service outcome.

For understanding Nigeria, the implication is precise: the headline debt ratio can improve while fiscal capacity deteriorates. A credible assessment must jointly track the financing-based deficit, the revenue denominator, actual debt service, portfolio risks, and the conversion of borrowing into assets and services. Fiscal consolidation that simply cuts capital releases after optimistic budgeting would lower reported expenditure without repairing this mechanism. Sustainable adjustment instead requires realistic revenue forecasts, complete fiscal reporting, disciplined debt composition, and protected high-value spending—so that lower financing pressure results from better institutions and project choices rather than the repeated postponement of development.

7.3 Public Financial Management Reform

Public financial management reforms include TSA, GIFMIS, IPPIS, procurement rules, debt management, audit, and performance monitoring. These tools can reduce leakages and improve budget credibility when properly integrated. But reforms often face institutional resistance because they change who controls cash, payroll, contracts, and information.

7.4 Defense and Public Security Spending

Defense spending belongs in fiscal analysis because security organizations compete for labor, capital, procurement capacity, and budget space. The relevant question is not only whether military expenditure is high, but whether security commitments create opportunity costs for development spending and whether personnel levels indicate a large uniformed claim on the labor force.

Defense and public-security spending in Nigeria should be read as a fiscal commitment as well as a security indicator. Armed forces personnel account for 0.2% of the total labor force in the latest World Bank series. Military expenditure is 0.6% of GDP and 3.1% of government expenditure. A country can have modest military expenditure as a share of GDP but still face high opportunity costs if the overall budget is small, revenue is weak, or internal-security obligations compete with education, health, maintenance, and local services.

Nigeria's public-sector constraint is not the size of government but the weakness and volatility of revenue. Oil proceeds and the Federation Account finance a large intergovernmental system, yet non-oil tax capacity remains too narrow for infrastructure, security, health, and education demands.

Debt ratios must be interpreted together with revenue. The IMF's 2025 Article IV notes improved fiscal performance after reforms, but interest costs and oil-price risks keep debt service at the center of Nigeria's administrative reform agenda.
