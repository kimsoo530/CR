1.1 National Profile

Nigeria's scale is not background information; it is a central condition of governing. World Bank series place the population at 232.7 million in 2024, up from 126.4 million in 2000—an increase of about 84 percent in twenty-four years. Over the same period, the urban share rose from 43.3 to 63.0 percent (World Bank, 2025a). A federal ministry is therefore not administering a stable population distributed across a few comparable jurisdictions. It is coordinating policies for a rapidly growing and urbanizing federation whose states differ sharply in settlement patterns, revenue bases, security conditions, and service capacity.

National scale, however, should not be confused with a nationally uniform social condition. The 2022 Nigeria Multidimensional Poverty Index estimated that 63 percent of residents—about 133 million people—were multidimensionally poor, using a survey of more than 56,000 households and indicators spanning health, education, living standards, work, and shocks (National Bureau of Statistics, 2022). The World Bank's Streamlined Country Diagnostic further reports that roughly three quarters of poor Nigerians live in the North and emphasizes large subnational differences in income, human capital, and conflict exposure (World Bank, 2026). These measures are not interchangeable: the first is a household-level multidimensional index, while the latter synthesizes monetary poverty and regional diagnostics. Read together, they show why one national mean cannot serve as a sufficient policy baseline.

Nigeria is formally a federal presidential republic under the 1999 Constitution, as amended. It comprises 36 states, the Federal Capital Territory (FCT), and 774 constitutionally listed local government areas. The bicameral National Assembly contains a Senate that gives equal state representation and a House of Representatives organized primarily by population. Abuja is the political capital, while Lagos remains the largest commercial and financial center. English is the official language, but public authority is exercised in a highly multilingual society through federal, state, local, customary, religious, and civic institutions (Federal Republic of Nigeria, 1999/2023).

This profile changes how policy problems should be formulated. A national education target, for example, must be translated into state-specific teacher, school-security, language, and attendance strategies. Urban policy must distinguish Lagos's megacity coordination problems from fast-growing secondary cities and conflict-affected displacement centers. Social protection must be designed for large informal labor markets and must reach people whose identification, banking access, or local administrative records may be incomplete. The appropriate unit of diagnosis is consequently often the state, local government area, or urban region rather than the country alone.

For comparative administration, Nigeria demonstrates that demographic size can enlarge markets and political influence while simultaneously increasing coordination costs. Population growth is not itself evidence of administrative failure, and regional inequality does not by itself identify its cause. The defensible claim is narrower: rapid growth and territorial heterogeneity raise the value of reliable subnational data, equalization rules, interoperable administrative systems, and differentiated implementation. Policies should therefore specify which level of government has authority, finance, personnel, and information—and should report results by territory instead of treating a national average as the outcome experienced by every Nigerian.

1.2 Geography, Federal Territory, and Administrative Divisions

Nigeria links the Gulf of Guinea to the Sahel and the Lake Chad basin. It borders Benin, Niger, Chad, and Cameroon; its ecological zones run from coastal wetlands and the Niger Delta through forest and savannah to the semi-arid north. This geography makes several policy systems inseparable from territory: ports and petroleum infrastructure concentrate national revenue risks in the south; cross-border trade and pastoral mobility require regional coordination; desertification and water stress affect northern livelihoods; and insurgency, banditry, and displacement impose highly unequal security and service burdens. Geography does not determine institutional performance, but it changes the costs and feasible instruments of administration.

The federation's territorial map is the result of repeated political reorganization. Nigeria moved from large regions to 12 states in 1967 and, through later rounds of state creation, to 36 states by 1996. The FCT and Abuja were established to provide a federal capital less identified with the dominance of Lagos. The Constitution now recognizes 36 states and lists 774 local government areas (Federal Republic of Nigeria, 1999/2023). State creation helped give minorities access to subnational offices and reduced the dominance of the former regions, but a larger number of units is not equivalent to deeper decentralization. Authority, revenue, personnel, and accountability may still remain centralized even when the number of governments increases (Suberu, 2022).

That distinction is visible in Nigeria's intergovernmental finances. States receive statutory transfers through the Federation Account, yet their capacity to generate revenue independently differs enormously. The National Bureau of Statistics recorded total state-level internally generated revenue of ₦2.43 trillion in 2023. Lagos generated ₦815.86 billion, the FCT ₦211.10 billion, and Rivers ₦195.41 billion; at the other end, Taraba generated ₦10.87 billion, Yobe ₦11.19 billion, and Kebbi ₦11.74 billion (National Bureau of Statistics, 2024). These are total nominal receipts, not per-capita tax effort or service-quality measures, so they cannot rank administrative performance by themselves. They do, however, demonstrate that formally similar territorial units enter federal bargaining with radically different own-source fiscal bases.

The administrative consequence is vertical and horizontal interdependence. Federal transfers can equalize resources and sustain national cohesion, but heavy transfer dependence may weaken the link between local taxation, citizen monitoring, and expenditure choices. Conversely, demanding uniform own-source revenue targets from poor or conflict-affected states would ignore structural differences in taxable activity. The World Bank's recent diagnostic characterizes the federation as politically stabilizing but fiscally fragmented, with overlapping responsibilities and uneven subnational capability (World Bank, 2026). This supports a policy approach that separates three questions: who is legally responsible, who pays, and who can actually deliver.

Metropolitan governance creates a second mismatch between administrative boundaries and functional problems. Lagos's housing, transport, drainage, ports, and pollution spill across local jurisdictions. Kano's commercial region, the Niger Delta's environmental systems, and displacement corridors in the northeast also exceed single-agency or single-state boundaries. A map of states is therefore necessary but insufficient; policy design also needs functional regions, travel-to-work areas, watersheds, trade corridors, and conflict-displacement networks.

The practical implication is not a generic call for “more decentralization.” Nigeria needs differentiated intergovernmental compacts. Minimum national standards and equalization finance are important for rights and territorial solidarity, while states and metropolitan institutions need discretion matched by transparent budgets, administrative data, and audit capacity. Transfer formulas should acknowledge need and fiscal effort without punishing jurisdictions with weak tax bases. For every major reform, analysts should identify the territorial scale of the problem, the institution with implementation capacity, and the mechanism for coordinating spillovers. That is the difference between drawing federal units on a map and making federalism operational.

1.3 The Core Institutional Puzzle

Nigeria's central institutional puzzle is a tension between successful political accommodation and uneven administrative performance. Federalism, state creation, the federal character principle, revenue sharing, and informal geographic rotation of high office have helped a deeply diverse polity remain within one competitive constitutional order. Yet the same system has not consistently converted national resources and formal mandates into secure, equitable, and reliable services. The puzzle is therefore not the absence of institutions. It is why institutions that help contain territorial conflict can coexist with weak implementation incentives, fragmented responsibility, and large differences in state capacity.

Two facts sharpen the puzzle. First, the World Bank estimates overall government revenue at only about 8.4 percent of GDP in 2024, among the lowest ratios internationally. Second, oil and gas generated only about 4 percent of GDP but typically accounted for more than 80 percent of exports (World Bank, 2026). These figures describe different denominators and should not be combined as if they were a single dependency ratio. Together, however, they reveal a state with a narrow revenue envelope and continuing external exposure to a sector that provides foreign exchange and distributable rents. Fiscal federalism consequently operates under scarcity and volatility: federal, state, and local governments negotiate shares of revenue while citizens often cannot clearly attribute a service failure to the level that authorized, financed, or implemented it.

Representative bureaucracy adds a second trade-off. The constitutional federal character principle seeks to prevent domination by a few states or groups in federal institutions (Federal Republic of Nigeria, 1999/2023). Recent public-administration research treats it as an equity institution whose effects depend on implementation: territorial representation may increase symbolic inclusion and legitimacy, but quota compliance alone does not guarantee substantive responsiveness, merit protection, or equal service outcomes (Ikpebe et al., 2025). This is not a reason to discard representation. It is a reason to evaluate recruitment through both inclusion and competence, and to examine whether representative presence changes decisions and citizen experience.

The competing explanations should remain distinct. A resource-rent account emphasizes weak taxation-based accountability and volatile allocation. A federal bargaining account emphasizes overlapping authority and veto points. A historical-institutional account emphasizes colonial regionalism, military centralization, and durable territorial inequalities. A capacity account focuses on personnel, information, procurement, security, and organizational routines. The observed outcome—uneven service delivery—is compatible with all four. Cross-sectional statistics cannot establish which mechanism dominates, and the relative weight is likely to vary by sector and state.

This yields a more useful research strategy. If transfer dependence weakens fiscal accountability, states with similar structural conditions but different own-revenue institutions should show different budget transparency or service results. If fragmented authority is central, reforms with an explicit assignment of functions and joint monitoring should outperform reforms with overlapping mandates. If historical inequality matters, gaps should persist most strongly in federally mediated services and in areas shaped by different state–local authority arrangements, as Archibong (2018) finds for several welfare indicators. These are testable implications, not settled conclusions.

Policy should address the coordination problem rather than merely add another plan or agency. Intergovernmental transfers can be tied to transparent service benchmarks without eliminating equalization. Federal character can be paired with competitive entry, professional development, and outcome audits. National programs can publish state- and local-level delivery data so citizens can identify responsibility. Reform sequencing should also reflect uneven capability: the same legal mandate may require different implementation support across states. Nigeria's experience thus offers a broader lesson for comparative administration: institutions that hold a diverse federation together and institutions that make services work are related, but they are not identical. Durable reform must protect political inclusion while strengthening the fiscal, informational, and organizational mechanisms that turn inclusion into performance.

1.4 Evidence Base

The report uses an evidence hierarchy because no single source can describe both Nigeria's formal state and its actual performance. Constitutional and statutory texts establish legal authority; federal and state publications document official policy and administrative outputs; international datasets provide comparable time series; household surveys measure lived conditions; and peer-reviewed historical and public-administration research supplies mechanisms and competing interpretations. These sources answer different questions and are not treated as substitutes.

The quantitative baseline follows reproducible indicator codes. Chapter 1 uses World Development Indicators SP.POP.TOTL and SP.URB.TOTL.IN.ZS for population and urbanization (World Bank, 2025a). Poverty evidence comes from the Nigeria Multidimensional Poverty Index, whose dimensions and survey design differ from monetary poverty measures (National Bureau of Statistics, 2022). State internally generated revenue is taken from the NBS 2023 compilation (National Bureau of Statistics, 2024). A value is reported with its year, unit, denominator, and source; missing observations are not converted to zero, and estimates with different definitions are not silently spliced into one series.

Administrative claims require similar discipline. The Constitution shows how responsibilities and representation are formally arranged, but it cannot demonstrate that a service is delivered. Budget allocations indicate authorization rather than execution or impact. Total state revenue reflects economic scale as well as administrative effort. National indicators obscure within-country variation. Insecurity may also degrade survey access and administrative reporting precisely where conditions are worst. For these reasons, the report labels evidence as descriptive, associational, diagnostic, or causal and avoids causal language when the design does not identify a counterfactual.

Historical claims are triangulated across specialized scholarship rather than inferred from present-day correlations alone. Research on colonial federalism, indirect rule, state creation, fiscal centralization, and spatial inequality is used to specify possible persistence mechanisms (Afigbo, 1991; Archibong, 2018; Cappelen & Sorens, 2018; Stilwell, 2011; Suberu, 2022). The presence of a historical institution and a contemporary regional gap is not sufficient proof of legacy. A persuasive legacy claim must identify an intermediate institution—such as native authority, transfer dependence, recruitment rules, or inherited service geography—and show evidence that it persists.

The research workflow also used a ChatGPT web-search conversation to formulate eight section-specific questions and generate candidate explanations and source leads. That output was treated as an unverified research memo, not as citable evidence. Every statistic and publication retained in the chapter was checked against an official data page, publisher record, or the archived project data; unsupported details were excluded. This separation preserves the useful exploratory role of generative AI while keeping the evidentiary burden on identifiable sources.

Readers should therefore interpret the chapter as a structured diagnosis rather than a causal evaluation of one reform. Its strongest claims concern legal design, demographic scale, measured territorial disparities, and documented historical sequences. Claims about why these patterns persist are mechanism-based interpretations with explicit limitations. Future updates should add subnational panel data, per-capita fiscal measures, comparable service outcomes, and policy discontinuities that permit stronger inference. The policy implication is itself methodological: a federation cannot govern territorial inequality well if it cannot measure comparable inputs, implementation, and outcomes at the level where services are delivered.
