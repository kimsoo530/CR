13. Education, Health, and Welfare

13.1 Education System, Schooling Outcomes, and Public Spending

Education is formally shared across federal, state, and local responsibilities. Federal institutions, state ministries, universal basic education financing, local schools, and examination bodies interact in a large and uneven system. Out-of-school children, security disruptions, teacher quality, gender gaps, and regional inequality make education a central state-capacity challenge.

13.2 Health Outcomes and Service Capacity

Health governance is similarly intergovernmental. Primary healthcare depends heavily on state and local systems, while federal institutions set policy, manage tertiary facilities, regulate, and support programs. Life expectancy and infant mortality trends show improvement, but Nigeria remains constrained by underfunding, workforce distribution, out-of-pocket spending, immunization gaps, maternal health risks, and conflict-affected service delivery.

13.3 Water, Sanitation, Nutrition, and Social Protection

Water, sanitation, nutrition, and social protection connect public health to infrastructure and poverty policy. Food inflation after macro reforms made nutrition and cash transfers politically urgent. The World Bank's 2025 Nigeria Development Update emphasizes the need to bring reform gains to households through food-price reduction, better public spending, and stronger safety nets (World Bank, 2025).

13.4 Human Development, Territorial Equity, and State Capacity

Human development in Nigeria is territorially uneven. National averages can hide large differences between states, between urban and rural areas, and between secure and conflict-affected zones. A professional country report must therefore treat human development as a governance issue, not only a social-sector issue.

Education indicators reveal whether Nigeria can convert demographic scale into human capital. State-level variation in school attendance, teacher quality, and security means national averages understate territorial inequality.

Health outcomes have improved over the long run but remain constrained by primary-health funding, state implementation, workforce distribution, out-of-pocket spending, and insecurity in vulnerable zones.

Spending and input indicators help distinguish formal policy promises from service capacity. Nigeria's health system is strongly affected by state and local financing, facility management, and household out-of-pocket exposure.
