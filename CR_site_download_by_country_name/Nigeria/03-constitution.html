3.1 Constitutional Order and Amendments

Nigeria's 1999 Constitution restored elected civilian government, but it did not begin as the product of an elected constituent assembly. It was promulgated by the departing military government and presented as the constitutional basis for the Fourth Republic. That origin matters because constitutional endurance and constitutional legitimacy are not the same thing. Arowosegbe argues that the unresolved authorship question continues to shape demands for restructuring and a more genuinely popular constitution, even though repeated elections and alterations have given the document substantial institutional continuity (Arowosegbe, 2022). The analytical issue is therefore not whether Nigeria has a constitution, but how a formally durable order acquires social authority while its founding procedure remains contested.

The amendment record shows an incremental rather than foundational response. The First, Second, and Third Alteration Acts of 2010 revised electoral timetables, election adjudication, and the financial position of national electoral and judicial bodies. The Fourth Alteration cycle, signed in 2017, addressed matters including legislative procedure and the age threshold for candidacy. In March 2023 the president assented to sixteen Fifth Alteration Acts. Among other changes, these acts strengthened financial arrangements for state legislatures and judiciaries and moved railways, electricity generation and transmission, airports, and correctional services toward concurrent federal-state competence. These were enacted alterations, not merely reform proposals; distinguishing assented acts from pending amendment bills is essential when evaluating constitutional change (PLAC, 2023).

The pattern suggests a mechanism of negotiated adaptation. Nigeria's high regional, ethnic, religious, and fiscal diversity makes a one-time comprehensive bargain difficult. Political actors therefore use discrete amendments to correct specific veto points without reopening the whole settlement. This lowers the immediate cost of reform and can preserve stability. Yet it also fragments constitutional change: a new competence or financial rule may be enacted without the administrative capacity, regulations, budgets, intergovernmental agreements, or judicial enforcement needed to make it operational. Devolution of electricity authority, for example, expands the legal space for state action but does not by itself create bankable utilities, credible regulators, or transmission coordination.

There are two competing readings. An optimistic interpretation treats amendment through supermajoritarian bargaining as evidence that the 1999 order is becoming domesticated through use. A more skeptical interpretation sees repeated piecemeal amendment as avoiding deeper disputes over constitutional authorship, police centralization, resource control, indigeneity, and local-government status. Both may be correct: incremental repair can improve performance while leaving the legitimacy debate open. The key empirical test is whether each alteration changes observable institutional behavior rather than only constitutional text.

For policy, Nigeria needs an implementation register for constitutional alterations. For every assented act, the register should identify the responsible institution, necessary secondary legislation, budget provision, implementation deadline, litigation status, and measurable outcome. Parliamentary committees and civil-society monitors could publish an annual scorecard separating enactment from compliance. A longer-term constitutional dialogue should also state clearly which grievances can be solved through ordinary legislation, which require alteration, and which concern political practice rather than text. This distinction would make reform more credible and reduce the tendency to treat constitutional replacement as a substitute for administrative execution (Federal Republic of Nigeria, 1999/2023; Suberu, 2019).

3.2 Sovereignty, Federalism, Citizenship, and Fundamental Rights

The Constitution locates sovereignty in the people while distributing public authority across the federation, 36 states, and 768 local government areas plus six area councils in the Federal Capital Territory. This architecture must govern a society in which national citizenship coexists with strong subnational identities. Federalism is consequently more than territorial decentralization: it is an inclusion system intended to prevent permanent domination by a few regions or groups. Section 14(3) expresses that purpose through the federal-character principle, which requires the composition of federal institutions to reflect Nigeria's diversity and avoid predominance by persons from a few states or ethnic groups.

Representative bureaucracy can widen access and signal membership, but its effects depend on implementation. Demarest, Langer, and Ukiwo's study of the Federal Character Commission found substantial symbolic value but little reduction in several public-service imbalances between 2003 and 2013. They attribute the gap to legal and administrative weaknesses, underfunding, and political dependence rather than to the irrelevance of inclusion itself (Demarest et al., 2020). Recent research likewise shows that representative staffing can improve perceived equity while raising a second question: whether recruitment and promotion rules also protect professional competence and transparent selection (Ikpebe et al., 2025). The appropriate contrast is therefore not diversity versus merit, but opaque patronage versus auditable rules that pursue both diversity and capability.

Citizenship reveals a sharper contradiction. The Constitution supplies a national status, yet access to scholarships, public employment, university quotas, and political opportunities may depend in practice on certificates of state or local-government "indigeneity." The Federal Character Commission defines an indigene largely through ancestral attachment to a locality, which can leave long-term residents classified as settlers (Federal Character Commission). This mechanism converts a policy designed to protect communities into a source of unequal treatment among Nigerian citizens. It can also reward political entrepreneurs who mobilize boundaries between indigenes and settlers.

The rights framework is similarly layered. Chapter IV contains judicially enforceable rights, including life, dignity, personal liberty, fair hearing, privacy, expression, assembly, religion, movement, and freedom from discrimination. Chapter II's economic and social objectives—education, health, welfare, and equitable development—are generally non-justiciable under section 6(6)(c). This creates a recurring gap between constitutional aspiration and legally enforceable entitlement. Legal pluralism adds another layer: federal and state courts operate alongside customary courts and Sharia courts of appeal within constitutionally defined jurisdictions. Such pluralism can make justice socially accessible, but inconsistent jurisdiction, gender inequality, or limited appellate access can undermine equal citizenship if safeguards are weak.

The policy implication is a residence-based pathway to equal civic status. Nigeria could preserve federal-character monitoring while requiring states and local governments to extend specified benefits to residents after a transparent period, regardless of ancestry. The Commission should publish machine-readable recruitment and promotion data by institution, grade, state, sex, and recruitment route, with privacy safeguards and an explanation of merit criteria. Courts and legal-aid bodies need accessible routes for challenging discriminatory administrative decisions. Finally, debates over making selected Chapter II obligations enforceable should specify fiscal duties, responsible tiers, and minimum standards; declaring broad rights without funding or assignment of responsibility risks symbolic constitutionalism. These measures would recast diversity management as equal citizenship with accountable representation rather than as a permanent allocation of ancestral shares.

3.3 Separation of Powers, Courts, and Emergency Authority

Nigeria's presidential constitution separates legislative, executive, and judicial authority, but the practical balance is asymmetrical. The president combines head-of-state, head-of-government, appointment, budgetary, and security powers. The National Assembly legislates, authorizes expenditure, confirms specified appointments, investigates administration, and can impeach. Courts interpret the Constitution and review official action, while the National Judicial Council (NJC) plays a central role in judicial appointment and discipline. These formal checks are substantial; the recurring problem is that institutional dependence over finance, information, enforcement, and security can narrow their practical reach.

Judicial autonomy illustrates the distinction between a rule and its operating conditions. The 2023 Fifth Alteration (No. 6) Act changed section 121 to reinforce direct financial arrangements for state legislatures and judiciaries. This addresses a mechanism of executive leverage: when another branch depends on a governor-controlled release process, constitutional authority can be weakened through delay even without overt interference. Yet direct constitutional provision does not ensure timely releases, transparent internal budgeting, or effective court administration. The relevant evidence is therefore not only the text of the alteration, but budget execution, case-processing time, appointment vacancies, disciplinary consistency, and compliance with judgments (Fifth Alteration No. 6 Act, 2023).

Legislative oversight faces a parallel implementation problem. Committees can summon officials and investigate expenditure, but they depend on reliable accounts, professional staff, timely audit reports, and follow-up sanctions. Oversight hearings without document production or remedial tracking may generate visibility rather than correction. Conversely, conflict between the executive and legislature is not automatically evidence of effective checking; it can also reflect bargaining over appointments, projects, or distributive benefits. Evaluation should ask whether oversight produces disclosed findings, recovered funds, corrected procedures, or improved services.

Security governance places the greatest pressure on separation of powers. Policing is highly centralized at the federal level even though insecurity varies dramatically across states and communities. Governors are designated chief security officers politically but do not command the Nigeria Police Force in the same way that they control state agencies. This mismatch encourages informal security financing and hybrid arrangements whose accountability is often unclear. Section 305 permits a state of emergency through a procedure involving presidential proclamation and legislative approval. Emergency authority may be necessary where violence overwhelms ordinary administration, but its legitimate use requires necessity, proportionality, time limitation, legislative scrutiny, judicial access, and public reporting. Emergency power should not become a substitute for routine police, justice, and local conflict-management capacity.

Reform should concentrate on operational checks. Judicial and legislative appropriations should be accompanied by published quarterly releases and audited expenditure. National Assembly committees need standardized recommendation trackers showing the executive response and implementation status. Any reform of policing should assign functions, finance, complaints mechanisms, data standards, and intergovernmental coordination before creating new armed bodies. Emergency proclamations should publish the factual basis, geographic scope, measures authorized, rights affected, duration, and review schedule. These steps do not eliminate political conflict. They make conflict governable by converting abstract separation of powers into auditable flows of money, information, coercive authority, and remedies.

3.4 Fiscal Federalism and the Constitutional Architecture of Distribution

Nigeria's fiscal constitution combines political decentralization with strong revenue centralization. Section 162 establishes the Federation Account, into which most federally collected revenue is paid and from which allocations are distributed among the federal, state, and local tiers. It also guarantees producing states a derivation share of at least 13 percent of revenue from natural resources. The arrangement finances national equalization and keeps every tier connected to a common fiscal pool, but it also creates incentives that shape taxation, accountability, and intergovernmental bargaining.

The central mechanism is vertical fiscal dependence. Oil and federally collected taxes flow into the common account, while many states and local governments raise only a limited portion of their spending from residents. Transfers can equalize capacity across territories with very different economic bases. However, when officials obtain marginal revenue through allocation bargaining rather than local taxation, citizens may find it harder to connect taxes, budgets, and service performance. Phillips identified revenue allocation as a persistent distributive problem early in the Fourth Republic's institutional ancestry; later decentralization research shows that changes in the number and authority of subnational units did not remove competition over centrally pooled resources (Phillips, 1991; Suberu, 2022).

Oil derivation exposes the trade-off. The 13 percent rule compensates producing areas for extraction and environmental costs, yet disagreements remain over the distributable base, offshore production, ecological damage, and whether state-level receipts reach affected communities. At the same time, non-producing states defend population, equality, social-development, and land-mass criteria as necessary for national cohesion. A formula can therefore be legally valid and still produce weak development if transfers are volatile, budget disclosure is poor, or subnational spending capacity is limited. The World Bank's recent country diagnostic accordingly treats low public revenue, service-delivery gaps, and uneven subnational capability as mutually reinforcing constraints rather than isolated fiscal variables (World Bank, 2026).

Local government is the sharpest constitutional fault line. Section 162 provides for State Joint Local Government Accounts, while states have often controlled the timing and composition of local councils and the flow of funds. In *Attorney-General of the Federation v. Attorney-General of Abia State & 35 Others* (SC/CV/343/2024), decided on 11 July 2024, the Supreme Court rejected state practices that obstruct allocations to democratically elected local councils and supported direct payment of their share. The judgment changes the formal channel of authority, but it does not by itself establish local autonomy. Direct transfers can still be absorbed by weak procurement, poor payroll control, limited own-source revenue, or councils lacking an electoral mandate (Supreme Court judgment, 2024).

A better fiscal compact requires measurement at each link in the chain. The Federation Account Allocation Committee should publish timely, machine-readable gross revenue, deductions, derivation, formula components, and transfers by tier. States and local councils should disclose budgeted and actual receipts, procurement awards, payroll, and sector outputs using comparable classifications. Direct local allocations should go only to democratically elected councils, as required by the 2024 judgment; separate public-finance rules should require lawful budgets, transparent procurement, timely accounts and independent audit without restoring gubernatorial discretion over the transfer. Formula reform should model distributional effects and protect minimum service standards while expanding incentives for non-oil own-source revenue. The policy goal is not maximal decentralization. It is a transparent assignment of revenue, functions, and accountability so that citizens can identify which tier received resources and which institution failed to deliver.
