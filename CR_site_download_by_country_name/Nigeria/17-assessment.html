17.1 National Operating Model

Nigeria's national operating model is a federal, distributive, oil-influenced democracy with strong private entrepreneurship and uneven public capacity. The center controls key revenue, security, monetary, and regulatory levers; states control much everyday service delivery; local governments are constitutionally present but often administratively weak; and private actors fill many gaps in power, transport, education, health, finance, and security.

17.2 Institutional Strengths, Risks, and Bottlenecks

The country's strengths are scale, social energy, electoral durability, regional influence, private-sector dynamism, cultural industries, fintech capacity, and a large policy community. Its risks are insecurity, low revenue, inflation, corruption, administrative fragmentation, oil dependence, debt-service pressure, education deficits, and distrust in public institutions. The most important bottleneck is not the absence of plans but weak conversion of plans into coordinated, financed, monitored implementation.

17.3 Implications for Korea and International Cooperation

For Korea and international cooperation, Nigeria is not only a market but an institutional partner. Useful cooperation should link infrastructure finance with maintenance and regulation, digital government with data protection and inclusion, vocational training with industrial demand, health and education support with state-level delivery, and security cooperation with civilian accountability.

17.4 Nigeria and Comparative Administration Theory

Nigeria contributes to comparative administration theory by challenging simple resource-curse and neopatrimonial explanations. Oil rents and patronage matter, but Nigeria also has competitive elections, courts, federal bargaining, sophisticated markets, active civil society, and subnational variation. The case suggests that state capacity in large federations is not a single national attribute; it is produced through negotiated intergovernmental systems, fiscal incentives, territorial security, data infrastructure, and coalition politics. Nigeria resembles existing hypotheses about rentier constraints and patron-client administration, yet differs because electoral competition and private innovation continually create counter-pressures for reform.

17.5 Strategic Outlook

The strategic outlook depends on whether macro stabilization becomes administratively delivered growth. If reforms improve revenue, reduce inflation, stabilize the exchange rate, expand power, improve food supply, and finance human capital, Nigeria's scale can become a major advantage. If reforms remain painful without visible delivery, the federal system may absorb stress but at the cost of deeper distrust and slower transformation.
