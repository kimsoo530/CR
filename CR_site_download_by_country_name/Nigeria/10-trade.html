10. Trade, Investment, and External Economy

10.1 Exports, Imports, and the Current Account

Nigeria's external accounts can look stronger without the economy becoming more competitive. A merchandise-trade surplus records that exported goods exceed imported goods in value. The current account is broader: it adds services, primary income, and transfers such as remittances. Neither measure by itself shows whether exporters are becoming more productive, whether imports consist of consumption goods or machinery, or whether households can afford previously imported essentials. The central question is therefore not simply whether Nigeria has a surplus, but what produces it and whether that mechanism expands domestic capability.

A stronger balance emerged from both export recovery and import adjustment

The balance-of-payments recovery since 2023 is substantial. On provisional official data compiled in the IMF's 2026 Article IV report, the current-account surplus rose from US$6.4 billion in 2023 to US$19.0 billion in 2024 before moderating to US$14.0 billion, or 4.8 percent of GDP, in 2025. The goods-trade surplus increased from US$8.1 billion to US$13.2 billion and then US$14.5 billion over the same period. Exports recovered to US$57.5 billion in 2025, while imports rose to US$43.0 billion but remained well below their 2022 value. These values indicate a real rebuilding of the external buffer, but they also reflect exchange-rate translation, changing oil prices, and weaker import demand after the sharp naira depreciation (International Monetary Fund, 2026a, Table 2).

The widening goods surplus reflects both export recovery and a lower import base. It does not distinguish prices from volumes or productive inputs from consumption imports, and therefore cannot by itself establish improved competitiveness.

The figure uses the IMF's consistent goods series rather than combining merchandise data with services or GDP ratios. It shows that imports fell more sharply than exports between 2022 and 2024, after which both recovered in 2025. The widening gap therefore arose from export improvement and a lower import base, not from uninterrupted export expansion. Because the values are in current US dollars, they still combine quantities and prices and cannot identify domestic value added or the welfare and investment function of imports without the product-level accounts (International Monetary Fund, 2026a, Table 2).

Three mechanisms produced the 2025 goods surplus. Oil and gas exports remained the main source of foreign exchange; domestic refining began converting part of Nigeria's crude into exportable refined products; and the higher cost of foreign currency compressed some non-oil imports. The IMF estimates crude-oil exports of US$31.5 billion, gas exports of US$10.5 billion, and refined-oil exports of US$6.1 billion in 2025. Refined-fuel imports fell from US$13.2 billion in 2024 to US$10.0 billion, while imports classified as “other” increased from US$25.7 billion to US$29.2 billion. The refinery effect is therefore economically important but incomplete: it reduced a long-standing fuel-import drain while capital goods, intermediate inputs, food, medicines, and other imports continued to reflect domestic production and infrastructure gaps (International Monetary Fund, 2026a, Table 2).

Quarterly merchandise data reveal the concentration hidden inside the aggregate balance. In the fourth quarter of 2025, total goods trade was ₦36.21 trillion, consisting of ₦18.96 trillion in exports and ₦17.25 trillion in imports, for a surplus of ₦1.71 trillion. Crude oil still provided 51.17 percent of exports and other petroleum products another 32.25 percent. Manufactured exports were only ₦423.43 billion, whereas manufactured imports reached ₦8.80 trillion. The result was a positive balance dominated by hydrocarbons alongside a large revealed demand for foreign manufactured goods (National Bureau of Statistics, 2026b).

This distinction matters for vulnerability. A fall in the oil price or production volume can reduce export receipts rapidly, while many imports are difficult to cut without damaging welfare or future output. Firms need machinery, spare parts, raw materials, and some specialized services; households need food, medicines, and transport equipment that domestic producers do not yet supply reliably. Import compression can improve the balance in the short run, but if it restricts productive inputs it can lower later export capacity. Conversely, a temporary current-account deficit caused by well-selected machinery and infrastructure imports can be consistent with structural transformation if those investments generate future foreign-exchange earnings.

The current account is not the merchandise balance

The broader current account qualifies the goods-surplus narrative. Nigeria recorded a net services deficit of US$14.6 billion in 2025, reflecting payments for transport, travel, finance, professional services, and other cross-border activities. Net primary income was negative US$9.1 billion, influenced by returns and repatriations from oil and other foreign-owned investments. Net transfers of US$23.2 billion—dominated by remittances—offset much of these outflows. The current-account surplus was therefore supported simultaneously by hydrocarbons, lower fuel imports, and household-to-household transfers, while services and investment-income payments remained structural drains (International Monetary Fund, 2026a, Table 2).

Current-account shifts reflect oil prices, import compression, exchange-rate policy, and services flows. They are therefore an external test of domestic macroeconomic and industrial reform.

The historical current-account figure shows why a one-year surplus is a poor measure of diversification. Nigeria has moved repeatedly between surplus and deficit as oil prices, import demand, exchange-rate policy, and domestic cycles changed. The 2025 surplus is stronger than many earlier outcomes, but the IMF's external-sector assessment estimates that temporary non-oil and gas import compression accounted for about 2.3 percent of GDP. It also reports net errors and omissions of US$15.7 billion—about 3.9 percent of GDP—which is large enough to limit confidence in precise balance-of-payments interpretation. The 2024 data were revised and the 2025 data remain provisional (International Monetary Fund, 2026a, Annex V).

The balance should consequently be read as a flow identity, not a national score. A surplus may reflect competitive exports and high saving, but it may also reflect recession, import rationing, underinvestment, or an undervalued currency. A deficit may finance unsustainable consumption, but it can also accompany productive investment. Nigeria's case contains elements of both interpretations: improved oil and gas production and domestic refining strengthened the supply side, while depreciation and weak purchasing power suppressed part of import demand. Policy evaluation must decompose these channels rather than reward the headline surplus.

Depreciation changes prices faster than productive capacity

Exchange-rate unification improved price discovery and reduced the subsidy embedded in access to official foreign exchange, but a cheaper naira cannot by itself diversify exports. Nigerian non-oil exporters often import machinery, packaging, chemicals, fuel-related inputs, and business services. Depreciation can lower their foreign-currency wage and local-input costs while simultaneously raising the naira cost of imported inputs and finance. Gold and Yusuf's time-series analysis finds that exchange rates and trade openness are associated with non-oil exports, but its results for fuel prices, credit, and other production costs vary by estimator. The appropriate inference is conditional: a competitive and predictable exchange rate can support exporters only where power, logistics, standards, finance, and imported-input access allow firms to respond (Gold & Yusuf, 2025).

Comparative research reinforces that diversification is not captured by the number of nominal export categories. Ross finds that oil exporters became substantially more specialized than non-oil economies over the late twentieth century and that African oil producers had especially weak diversification outcomes. The mechanism is not merely commodity abundance; petroleum production occupies an isolated part of the product space and can appreciate the real exchange rate, concentrate rents, and reduce incentives to build broad tradable capabilities (Ross, 2019). Nigeria's growing refined-petroleum exports may improve the trade balance and add domestic processing, but they do not by themselves eliminate hydrocarbon-price exposure. Diversification requires new products, destinations, firms, technologies, and domestic supplier linkages whose earnings survive an oil downturn.

External strength should be judged by its productive quality

A more useful policy dashboard separates resilience from transformation. For resilience, authorities should track the current account, net rather than only gross reserves, the oil price and production volume, external debt service relative to exports, and the share of short-term portfolio financing. For transformation, they should track non-oil export value and volume, product and destination concentration, services exports, domestic value added, the survival and growth of exporting firms, customs-release time, rejection rates under foreign standards, and the import content of exports. For welfare and investment, the composition of imports should distinguish food and consumer goods from capital equipment and intermediate inputs.

The policy implication is not to minimize imports or maximize the current-account surplus. It is to make foreign exchange less dependent on crude oil while ensuring that essential and productivity-enhancing imports remain financeable. Domestic refining can reduce fuel-import exposure, but competition, feedstock allocation, pricing transparency, and environmental regulation determine whether the gain becomes lower system cost rather than a protected rent. Non-oil exporters need a predictable exchange market, reliable power and ports, trade finance, standards laboratories, and rapid duty-drawback or input-import procedures. Remittance formalization can strengthen recorded transfers, but remittances should not be treated as a substitute for export capability.

Nigeria entered 2026 with a materially stronger external position than it had two years earlier (International Monetary Fund, 2026a). The evidence nevertheless supports a bounded conclusion. The surplus reflects genuine improvements in oil and gas supply and domestic refining, but also import compression and continued reliance on transfers; large statistical discrepancies further limit precision. The test of durable adjustment is whether the next investment cycle can increase machinery and intermediate imports without recreating an external crisis because non-oil goods and services exports, domestic value added, and stable long-term financing expand at the same time.

10.2 Oil, Gas, Agriculture, Services, and Regional Trade

Nigeria's production base is broader than its export basket. Agriculture, commerce, telecommunications, finance, entertainment, and other services account for much of output and employment, yet hydrocarbons still determine most recorded merchandise earnings. That mismatch is the central analytical problem. Export diversification is not achieved when a new petroleum product is added to the customs schedule or when a few agricultural commodities have a strong quarter. It requires a wider set of firms and products to earn foreign exchange across destinations, retain domestic value added, and survive a fall in oil prices. Regional trade matters because West African markets can provide a first export step for Nigerian firms, but official statistics capture that channel imperfectly and border policy can either formalize or displace it.

Petroleum processing changes the product mix more quickly than the risk structure

The latest merchandise accounts show genuine change inside a still-concentrated basket. In the fourth quarter of 2025, crude oil supplied 51.17 percent of exports and other petroleum products another 32.25 percent. Agricultural exports were valued at ₦1.32 trillion, raw materials at ₦1.19 trillion, and manufactured goods at ₦423.43 billion, compared with total exports of ₦18.96 trillion (National Bureau of Statistics, 2026b). Domestic refining therefore broadened petroleum output and reduced part of the fuel-import burden discussed in Section 10.1, but crude and processed petroleum together still supplied more than four-fifths of quarterly merchandise exports.

This distinction separates vertical upgrading from sectoral diversification. Refining crude domestically can add processing, logistics, engineering, tax, and supplier activity. It can also reduce the external cost of importing fuel. Yet crude, gas, and refined products remain exposed to related hydrocarbon prices, feedstock conditions, pipeline security, and carbon-transition risk. A refinery can improve domestic value added without making the export portfolio resilient to a common oil shock. The appropriate policy question is therefore not whether petroleum processing counts as industrialization—it does—but whether its supplier linkages, skills, and infrastructure lower the cost of entry for other tradable sectors.

UNCTAD's product-concentration index makes the remaining distance visible. The index approaches one when a small number of product groups dominate exports and zero when exports are distributed more evenly. Nigeria's score fell from 0.761 in 2010 to 0.619 in 2025, with most of the decline occurring after 2023. That is meaningful movement, consistent with rising refined-product and some non-oil exports. Nigeria nevertheless remained much more concentrated in 2025 than Ghana (0.487), Kenya (0.186), or South Africa (0.153) under the same definition (UN Trade and Development, 2026a).

A lower score indicates a merchandise basket distributed more evenly across product groups. The index does not measure destination diversity, exporting firms, domestic value added, technological complexity, or services exports, and price changes can move the score without equivalent capability growth.

The comparison should be read as a warning, not a league table. The index measures how merchandise value is distributed across product groups; it does not show the number or survival of exporting firms, domestic content, technological complexity, destination concentration, or services exports. Oil-price changes can also move the index without a comparable change in productive capability. The 2024–2025 decline is thus encouraging but insufficient evidence of structural transformation. A stronger test would ask whether non-petroleum products gain share through sustained volume and firm growth rather than through a temporary change in hydrocarbon prices.

Agricultural potential is constrained between the farm and the border

Agriculture offers a different diversification route because cocoa, sesame, cashew, hibiscus, horticulture, leather, and processed foods can connect rural production to foreign demand. The binding problem is not simply insufficient output. Exportable consistency depends on seed and extension systems, farm security, aggregation, storage, cold chains, testing laboratories, traceability, packaging, inland transport, trade finance, and predictable border rules. A failure in any one link can turn an apparent comparative advantage at the farm gate into an unreliable shipment at the port.

Recent peer-reviewed work using Nigerian quarterly data from 2002 to 2023 associates agricultural-export diversification with greater resilience to external shocks, while also finding that adverse global shocks persist where institutions and value-chain systems are weak (Attah et al., 2025). That finding is best interpreted as mechanism-consistent evidence rather than proof that any single reform will cause export growth: the time-series design cannot observe every firm-level capability or policy reversal. Its useful implication is that commodity promotion without contract enforcement, standards capacity, infrastructure, and policy stability is unlikely to produce durable diversification.

The policy unit should consequently be an export corridor and value chain, not a commodity slogan. For each priority product, government should identify the importing market and its sanitary or technical standard, map the states and firms that can supply it, publish rejection and clearance data, and finance the specific public goods that private producers cannot coordinate alone. Support should be conditional on export-volume growth, quality compliance, new-firm entry, domestic processing, and repayment or sunset rules. Export bans and abrupt licensing changes are especially damaging because they make buyers and processors discount the credibility of future Nigerian supply.

Services are economically large but externally under-realized and under-measured

Nigeria's large domestic services economy does not yet translate into a comparable recorded export position. UNCTAD reports services exports of US$4.565 billion and services imports of US$17.926 billion in 2024; the IMF's balance-of-payments table similarly records a large services deficit in 2025 (UN Trade and Development, 2025a; International Monetary Fund, 2026a). This is not evidence that Nigerian finance, software, film, music, professional services, or platform firms lack regional reach. It shows that the balance of payments records cross-border receipts and payments, not the full social visibility or domestic scale of an industry.

Measurement is particularly difficult for digitally delivered, platform-mediated, and mode-of-supply services. Some transactions are paid through foreign accounts, bundled with intellectual property or advertising, supplied through affiliates, or omitted from detailed bilateral statistics. UNCTAD notes that services trade is underrepresented in many developing-country statistical systems and that balance-of-payments, foreign-affiliate, value-added, and digital-trade data answer different questions (UN Trade and Development, 2025b). Nigeria should therefore improve enterprise surveys and payment classifications before treating a low recorded category as zero activity or designing incentives around an incomplete baseline.

Services also function as inputs to goods exports. Comparative evidence from 36 Sub-Saharan African countries finds a positive association between services trade and participation in global value chains, with effects varying by service category and position in the chain (Efogo, 2020). For Nigeria, reliable logistics, finance, telecommunications, engineering, certification, and business services can raise the competitiveness of agriculture and manufacturing even when those services are not separately visible in the exported good's customs value. The policy objective should be both direct services exports and the “servicification” of other exports.

Regional trade is a capability ladder whose lower rungs are partly invisible

ECOWAS markets can be easier first destinations than Europe, Asia, or North America because distances are shorter, consumer preferences overlap, and Nigerian firms may be competitive in processed food, chemicals, footwear, household goods, finance, entertainment, and business services. Historical firm-level evidence assembled for the World Bank found that more than one-third of surveyed Nigerian exporters identified ECOWAS countries as their primary market, and that regional exporters occupied an intermediate position between domestic-only firms and larger global exporters. The study's data are old and not nationally weighted, so they cannot describe the present share of firms. They nevertheless support a durable mechanism: a regional market can allow firms to learn exporting before meeting the scale and standards of global markets (von Uexkull & Shui, 2014).

Official regional shares should not be read literally. The same World Bank study documented large gaps between recorded and estimated non-oil trade with Cameroon and explained how re-export, smuggling, small consignments, and weak border records obscure food and daily-consumption trade (von Uexkull & Shui, 2014). Informality is not merely a statistical nuisance. It often reflects the cost and uncertainty of certificates of origin, inspections, unofficial payments, foreign-exchange restrictions, road insecurity, and divergent national implementation of nominally common rules. Closing a border can therefore shift routes and prices without eliminating the underlying demand.

This has two policy implications. First, formalization should reduce the fixed cost of legality through interoperable customs records, risk-based inspection, simplified regimes for small traders, transparent origin certification, and monitored border-corridor performance. Second, the success indicator should not be the number of seizures or the decline of imports. It should be lower clearance time and variance, more registered small traders and exporting firms, higher recorded regional trade, fewer unofficial payments, and stable availability of essential inputs and food. Abrupt restrictions that raise the legal cost above the informal premium can worsen both data quality and state credibility.

Nigeria's diversification strategy should therefore link three different ladders. Petroleum processing should be judged by domestic supplier development and whether shared infrastructure benefits non-oil tradables. Agricultural corridors should be judged by verified standards compliance, processing, and repeat export orders. Services and regional trade should be judged by better measurement, firm entry, and progression from domestic to regional and global markets. The evidence does not support a claim that Nigeria has already escaped oil dependence. It supports a more bounded conclusion: the product mix began to widen in 2024–2025, but concentration remains high, and durable diversification will depend on whether institutions convert sectoral potential into repeatable firm-level exporting capability.

10.3 FDI, Portfolio Flows, and Investment Climate

Nigeria does not face a generic shortage of “foreign capital.” It faces a composition and conversion problem. Foreign direct investment (FDI) gives an investor a lasting interest and some control in an enterprise; portfolio investment purchases tradable debt or equity without the same managerial commitment; “other investment” includes loans, deposits, trade credit, and related claims. These flows can all finance useful activity, but their maturity, currency exposure, reversibility, and contribution to productive capacity differ. An investment announcement is different again: it records an intention, not a completed balance-of-payments transaction. The relevant question is therefore whether Nigeria is attracting capital that remains through shocks, expands non-oil production, transfers capabilities, and creates competitive supplier and employment linkages.

Market scale has not translated into sustained direct investment

Nigeria offers a large consumer market, abundant energy and agricultural resources, strong entrepreneurial networks, and access to ECOWAS and AfCFTA markets. Those advantages have not prevented a long decline in realized FDI. The World Bank Group reports that net inflows averaged only 0.5 percent of GDP from 2013 to 2023 and that extractives, information-technology services, and manufacturing were the main recipient sectors. Announced projects rose in 2023, especially in services and manufacturing, but the report correctly treats their realization as conditional rather than completed investment (World Bank Group, 2025).

The GDP-relative measure prevents Nigeria's economic size from overstating investment intensity. Net FDI can be negative when disinvestment or intercompany repayments exceed new inflows, and the aggregate does not identify sector, greenfield capacity, ownership, jobs, or domestic spillovers.

The historical series shows why a large-market narrative is insufficient. FDI inflows exceeded 2 percent of GDP in several years during the 2000s, then declined sharply after the early 2010s and remained below 1 percent from 2014 through 2024. The 2022 value was slightly negative because net FDI is an accounting balance that can fall below zero when disinvestment, intercompany debt repayment, or profit-related transactions exceed new inflows. The figure uses a ratio to GDP to avoid confusing Nigeria's size with investment intensity, but it does not show project quality, sector, ownership, greenfield versus merger activity, or domestic spillovers (World Bank, 2025a).

This trajectory reflects several reinforcing mechanisms. Exchange-rate segmentation and foreign-currency shortages made it difficult to price assets, import equipment, service foreign-currency obligations, and repatriate earnings. High inflation and policy reversals raised the option value of waiting. Unreliable power, port and road costs, insecurity, and weak contract enforcement increased operating and risk-insurance costs after entry. Trade restrictions raised the price of imported intermediate and capital goods needed by domestic producers. These are not separate items on a generic “ease of doing business” list: together they weaken a project's expected cash flow and raise the discount rate applied to it.

The foreign-exchange reforms since 2023 reduced one important distortion by restoring more market-based price discovery and improving access to foreign currency. Yet depreciation also raised the naira cost of imported machinery and reduced the dollar value of domestic revenues until prices and productivity adjusted. A credible regime must therefore do more than produce a flexible rate on a particular day. Investors need to know that conversion, remittance, hedging, tax, customs, and dispute-resolution rules will remain intelligible across the life of a project.

The 2025 financing recovery remained portfolio-led

The composition of the recent recovery illustrates the difference between liquidity and commitment. On provisional balance-of-payments data, the financial account recorded a net inflow of US$5.9 billion in 2025. Net FDI improved to US$2.8 billion but remained below 1 percent of GDP. Net portfolio inflows were US$7.1 billion, while other investment registered a US$4.0 billion net outflow. Foreign participation in Central Bank open-market-operation (OMO) instruments accounted for about US$5.8 billion of net inflows. The same flows helped gross reserves rise, but they also created short-term liabilities and rollover risk (International Monetary Fund, 2026a, Annex VI).

Portfolio flows reacted most strongly to the new macro-financial setting, while direct investment recovered more gradually and other investment remained volatile. The categories differ in maturity and reversibility; they should not be added into an undifferentiated measure of investor confidence.

The figure shows a four-year pattern that a total-capital headline would conceal. Portfolio inflows rose from US$3.9 billion in 2022 to US$15.2 billion in 2024 before moderating in 2025. Direct investment recovered more slowly, from a net outflow in 2022 to US$2.8 billion in 2025. Other investment moved from a US$7.2 billion inflow in 2023 to a US$19.1 billion outflow in 2024 and a US$4.0 billion outflow in 2025. These categories are provisional balance-of-payments aggregates, not project counts, and the large errors and omissions discussed in Section 10.1 limit false precision. The defensible conclusion is that external financing improved, but its most responsive component was still the more reversible one (International Monetary Fund, 2026a, Table 2).

Portfolio investment is not inherently undesirable. A deeper government-bond and equity market can broaden financing sources, improve price discovery, and create an exit route that long-term investors value. The vulnerability arises when high-yield, short-maturity instruments are needed to attract non-residents and reserve accumulation depends on their continued rollover. A fall in the interest differential, a rise in global risk aversion, or doubts about currency adjustment can reverse the flow quickly. The authorities and IMF therefore share an interest in shifting foreign participation from short-term OMO instruments toward longer-dated government securities and equities while reducing the concentration of rollover risk (International Monetary Fund, 2026a).

The binding constraint is credible coordination, not another promotion campaign

Nigeria has investment-promotion institutions, sector plans, tax incentives, free zones, public-private-partnership frameworks, and a large pipeline of proposed projects. The harder problem is that a project crosses agencies and levels of government. A manufacturing investor may need federal customs and tax decisions, a state land title and right-of-way, regulated power or self-generation, environmental approval, port access, standards certification, security, and a reliable court or arbitration pathway. A fast company-registration portal cannot compensate for an uncertain sequence across all these veto points.

The 2025 Country Private Sector Diagnostic identifies power, finance, transport, security, trade barriers, and policy uncertainty as central constraints. It reports that domestic credit to the private sector was 12.9 percent of GDP in 2024, below the Sub-Saharan African average of 20.1 percent and the lower-middle-income average of 34.0 percent. It also estimates that net FDI averaged only 0.5 percent of GDP over 2014–2023, while announcements were concentrated in oil and gas, communications, renewable energy, and chemicals (World Bank Group, 2025). The mechanism is cumulative: firms facing unreliable infrastructure self-provide power and security, but high domestic interest rates and shallow long-term finance make that self-provision more expensive; foreign investors then demand still higher returns or choose sectors with enclave revenues and foreign-currency earnings.

Investment incentives can worsen this coordination problem when they are negotiated firm by firm. Tax holidays or customs exemptions may offset a private cost without fixing the public bottleneck, while discretion creates unequal treatment and fiscal opacity. A stronger regime would publish eligibility, duration, fiscal cost, performance conditions, and exit rules. It would use time-bound facilitation teams for strategic projects, but record approvals and delays in a common case-management system so that exceptional coordination becomes administrative learning rather than permanent privilege.

FDI quality depends on sector, linkages, and additionality

The value of FDI cannot be inferred from its gross amount. Resource-seeking investment can expand exports and fiscal receipts while operating as an enclave with limited local sourcing. Market-seeking investment can serve Nigerian consumers but raise import demand if production relies on foreign inputs. Efficiency-seeking investment is more likely to join regional or global value chains, but it requires reliable power, logistics, skills, standards, and policy access to imported inputs. Greenfield projects may create capacity and jobs, whereas acquisitions can improve management and finance without adding the same immediate physical capacity. These categories overlap, so project evaluation needs evidence rather than labels.

Peer-reviewed Nigerian evidence reinforces the need for disaggregation. Okechukwu, De Vita, and Luo find a positive long-run relationship between aggregate FDI and total exports, but the relationship survives disaggregation only for oil exports; primary and manufacturing FDI were linked to total and oil exports, while services FDI showed no significant long-run relationship with exports in their 1980–2015 data (Okechukwu et al., 2018). The study uses aggregate time-series relationships and cannot establish firm-level spillovers or describe the post-2015 digital economy. Its important contribution is narrower: attracting FDI did not automatically diversify Nigerian exports.

Policy should therefore test additionality and linkage formation. For a proposed incentive, officials should ask whether the investment would occur without support, whether it introduces technology or capacity not otherwise available, how many local suppliers can meet standards, what training is verifiable, how much foreign exchange it earns or saves after imported inputs and profit repatriation, and whether competition remains open. Incentives should be released against audited milestones such as installed and operating capacity, formal employment and training, supplier certification, export orders, research collaboration, and environmental compliance—not only committed capital.

A capital-quality dashboard makes policy trade-offs visible

A useful dashboard would publish five layers. The first separates announced, approved, committed, disbursed, and operating projects. The second separates FDI, portfolio equity, portfolio debt, OMO holdings, external loans, and trade credit by maturity and currency. The third records sector, state, greenfield or acquisition status, ownership, and ultimate investor economy. The fourth measures outcomes: jobs and wages, local procurement, training, exports, domestic value added, tax paid after incentives, and environmental performance. The fifth records administrative performance: time and variance for land, customs, standards, permits, grid connection, and dispute resolution.

This framework changes the policy objective. Nigeria should not maximize FDI at any cost or reject portfolio investment because it is liquid. It should lower economy-wide uncertainty, deepen longer-term domestic markets, and reserve selective support for investments with measurable spillovers that markets cannot coordinate alone. Short-term portfolio flows can support liquidity during stabilization, but monetary authorities should avoid making high-yield OMO financing a substitute for durable reserves and productive investment. FDI policy should favor transparent rules, competition, and capability transfer over bespoke exemptions.

The 2025 improvement is consequently real but incomplete. Direct investment recovered, foreign-exchange functioning improved, and proposed non-oil projects signal renewed interest. Yet the financial account remained portfolio-led and the long-run FDI ratio remained far below its earlier level and peer benchmarks. The test of a better investment climate is not the size of the next announcement ceremony. It is whether projects reach operation, remain through exchange-rate and political cycles, widen the non-oil export base, and leave Nigerian workers and suppliers with capabilities that persist even if the original foreign investor later exits.

10.4 AfCFTA, Ports, Refineries, and Industrial Policy

AfCFTA membership, port investment, refinery capacity, and sector incentives are often presented as four separate policy achievements. Economically they form one implementation chain. A preferential tariff matters only if a firm can prove origin, meet standards, move inputs and output through a predictable border, finance the transaction, and deliver at a competitive cost. A refinery or special economic zone matters only if infrastructure, customs, suppliers, competition, and environmental rules allow productive capacity to operate rather than remain an enclave. The central question is therefore not whether Nigeria has adopted the right instruments, but whether public agencies can coordinate them and withdraw support when outcomes do not justify its cost.

AfCFTA converts a large home market into a test of regional competitiveness

Nigeria's AfCFTA implementation moved beyond signature during 2025–2026. It formally adopted a tariff-liberalization schedule in April 2025 covering 80 percent of traded goods, ratified the Digital Trade Protocol in November 2025, and submitted a services schedule to ECOWAS in October 2025. In April 2026 the authorities also adopted measures to remove selected import bans and phase out some Import Adjustment Taxes (Stojanov et al., 2026). These are consequential steps because they make preferences and rules more operational, but adoption does not establish utilization.

Preferential access is useful only when firms claim it. Utilization depends on whether exporters know the eligible tariff line, can document rules of origin, obtain sanitary and technical certification, and complete customs procedures at a cost lower than the tariff preference. Smaller firms face a larger fixed compliance burden per shipment. Digital-trade rules similarly require domestic legal recognition of electronic documents, interoperable identity and payment systems, data protection, cybersecurity, and avenues for cross-border redress. A protocol can reduce policy uncertainty, but it cannot supply these administrative complements by itself.

The agreement also exposes a tension between regional integration and discretionary import substitution. Domestic producers may need time to acquire capabilities, yet open-ended bans and high input tariffs can protect inefficient incumbents, raise consumer prices, and make exported manufactures more expensive. A 2014 World Bank simulation found that replacing Nigeria's bans and levies with a more coherent ECOWAS tariff regime could lower input costs for many firms and reduce incentives for informal trade, while also creating concentrated adjustment losses in particular industries (von Uexkull & Shui, 2014). The exact estimates are historical and should not be projected onto today's tariff schedule. The mechanism remains relevant: the costs of protection are diffuse among consumers and input-using firms, whereas the losses from reform are concentrated and politically visible.

Nigeria should therefore publish a product-level AfCFTA implementation dashboard. It should record the tariff schedule and effective date, preference claims and rejection reasons, origin-certificate time and cost, standards and sanitary failures, firm size, destination, and repeat exporting. A rising number of registered exporters is not enough if few complete a second shipment. The decisive indicators are utilization, survival, delivery reliability, domestic value added, and progression into more demanding products and markets.

Port performance is a multi-agency waiting-time problem

Physical port capacity matters, but the binding delay often lies in the sequence among shipping lines, terminal operators, Customs, the Nigerian Ports Authority, standards and food-safety agencies, banks, truck systems, and importers. Nigeria's first Time Release Study at Tincan Island Port examined the interval from booking a consignment for examination to physical gate-out. It reported an average of 3 days and 10 hours and attributed more than 84 percent of elapsed time to waiting rather than active processing. The study also acknowledged that missing integrated timestamps prevented full measurement from vessel arrival through earlier clearance stages (Nigeria Customs Service, 2026a). The number is therefore a measured segment, not total end-to-end port dwell time.

That limitation is analytically useful: a port cannot manage what its agencies do not jointly timestamp. Sequential inspection, manual delivery orders and truck dispatch, limited use of scanning, system downtime, and poor visibility create queues even when each agency reports acceptable internal processing time. The failure is relational. Optimizing Customs alone may move the bottleneck to a regulator, terminal, bank, or gate.

The Authorized Economic Operator pilot points to a possible incentive-compatible approach. The Nigeria Customs Service reported an average release time of 43 hours for certified operators, compared with five days before certification and seven days for regular operators (Nigeria Customs Service, 2025). These administrative comparisons are not a randomized evaluation: selected firms are more compliant and capable at baseline. Still, they suggest that risk-based treatment and trusted-trader status can reduce physical inspection without abandoning enforcement.

The National Single Window is valuable if it produces one submission, one shipment identifier, shared status visibility, and binding handoffs across agencies. A portal that merely places separate forms behind one webpage does not remove sequential approvals. Implementation should therefore be assessed through end-to-end timestamps, the share of declarations receiving coordinated rather than repeated inspection, scanner utilization, variance as well as average release time, unofficial payment reports, demurrage and storage cost, and the proportion of cases released without manual intervention (Nigeria Customs Service, 2026b).

Refining is a strategic gain whose development effect depends on market design

New refining capacity changes Nigeria's external economy by replacing some fuel imports and creating exportable petroleum products. Sections 10.1 and 10.2 established both the balance-of-payments improvement and the continued common exposure to hydrocarbons. The industrial-policy issue is how the gain is governed. Feedstock access, pipeline and depot infrastructure, product standards, pricing, foreign-exchange settlement, import rules, and third-party logistics determine whether refining lowers system costs and builds suppliers or creates a protected bottleneck.

Scale economies make concentration unsurprising in refining, but they do not eliminate the need for competition policy. Authorities should publish feedstock-allocation rules, benchmark domestic prices against relevant import- and export-parity measures, ensure non-discriminatory access to shared infrastructure where feasible, and monitor product quality and environmental performance. Import restrictions should not be triggered solely by nominal installed capacity; they should depend on reliable operating output, geographic distribution, inventories, and the ability to meet demand without creating scarcity rents.

The same logic applies to backward linkages. Local-content targets can support engineering, maintenance, fabrication, logistics, and professional services when domestic firms can learn and compete. If targets are detached from capability and cost, they can instead create paper compliance or privileged intermediaries. Supplier-development support should fund certification, training, and technology adoption and then test whether local firms win repeat contracts, improve productivity, and enter other markets. The desired outcome is transferable capability, not permanent dependence on one anchor firm.

Industrial policy needs performance discipline and a political strategy for adjustment

Nigeria has long used tariffs, import bans, credit, tax relief, local-content rules, procurement, free zones, and infrastructure projects to promote production. The case for intervention is real where firms face coordination failures, learning spillovers, missing standards infrastructure, or first-mover risk. The danger is that support becomes a distributive bargain that outlives learning, blocks entry, or shifts costs to consumers and input users.

Evidence from African special economic zones illustrates the implementation problem. Farole and Moberg document that Nigerian zone authorities, Customs, finance agencies, local governments, and infrastructure providers have often held misaligned mandates. In Calabar, institutional competition contributed to a flagship zone being disconnected from the port, and one-stop arrangements lacked authority over the participating agencies (Farole & Moberg, 2017). The lesson is not that zones cannot work. It is that fenced infrastructure does not substitute for governance, connectivity, and credible agency commitments.

Recent Nigerian evidence also shows why “losers” cannot be ignored. Odijie's study of the Nigeria Sugar Master Plan finds that land disputes, displaced livelihoods, and smuggling responses undermined targets despite incentives for domestic growing and refining. Groups with limited formal political power could still obstruct implementation through conflict or informal adaptation (Odijie, 2024). Adjustment assistance is therefore not merely compensation after reform. Consultation, land and livelihood safeguards, worker transition, consumer-price monitoring, and lawful market access are part of an implementable industrial policy.

Performance discipline requires an explicit contract between support and learning. Before intervention, government should state the market failure, baseline, beneficiary, fiscal and consumer cost, measurable target, review date, and exit rule. During implementation, an independent unit should publish disbursement, tax expenditure, output, productivity, employment, wages, local sourcing, exports, prices, and environmental compliance. Support should expand when verified learning and spillovers exceed cost, change when the mechanism fails, and end when firms can compete or when targets are repeatedly missed.

This is not a choice between laissez-faire and permanent protection. Nigeria needs active coordination because power, ports, standards, finance, skills, and regional rules must work together. It also needs rivalry and evaluation because connected firms can convert coordination into exclusion. AfCFTA can strengthen discipline by enlarging the market and exposing firms to regional competition, while temporary and transparent support helps capable firms cross initial thresholds.

Chapter 10's evidence converges on a single conclusion. Nigeria's external position improved, export categories widened, and direct investment began to recover. Yet merchandise earnings remain hydrocarbon-concentrated, services exports and regional transactions are incompletely measured, recent financing is portfolio-led, and trade costs arise from multi-agency implementation failures. Success should therefore be judged by whether firms use preferences, clear borders predictably, learn from investment, add domestic value, and remain competitive without indefinite protection. The external economy becomes transformative only when foreign exchange earnings, productive imports, investment, and industrial policy reinforce the same process of capability accumulation.
