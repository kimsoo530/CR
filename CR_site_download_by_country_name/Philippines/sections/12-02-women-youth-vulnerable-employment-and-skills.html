12.2 Women, Youth, Vulnerable Employment, and Skills

ASEAN comparative lens (2026). The Philippines should be read in ASEAN comparison as a populous archipelagic democracy with strong local political mediation, labor migration, and maritime exposure. Compared with Indonesia, it has a smaller domestic market and weaker industrial depth; compared with Vietnam and Thailand, it is less manufacturing-centered; compared with Singapore and Malaysia, it has a more dispersed territorial-service problem and heavier reliance on external labor and services. For this section, the regional comparison should compare labor-force participation, informality, gender, youth employment, skills, and industrial upgrading with ASEAN peers at different income levels. Local comparable indicators show: population 115.84 million (2024), rank 2 among the nine ASEAN reports in this workspace; GDP US$461.62 billion (2024), rank 5 among the nine ASEAN reports in this workspace; GDP per capita US$3,985 (2024), rank 6 among the nine ASEAN reports in this workspace; real GDP growth 5.69% (2024), rank 3 among the nine ASEAN reports in this workspace; government effectiveness 0.11 (2023), rank 5 among the nine ASEAN reports in this workspace; internet use 67.26% (2024), rank 7 among the nine ASEAN reports in this workspace. Use `sources/documents/regional/asean_comparative_lens_2026.md` together with ASEANstats, ASEAN Secretariat materials, and the country processed-indicator files before making cross-ASEAN claims.

Academic and statistical strengthening note (2026). World Bank WDI/WGI data in the local evidence file put the 2024 population at 115.84 million and GDP at about US$461.62 billion, while the Philippine Statistics Authority's 2024 Census of Population count declared official in 2025 gives 112.73 million as of 1 July 2024; the WDI file also records 2024 real GDP growth of 5.69 percent, GDP per capita of US$3,985, revenue excluding grants of 16.70 percent of GDP, tax revenue of 14.37 percent of GDP, internet use of 67.26 percent, and WGI 2023 scores of 0.11 for government effectiveness, 0.11 for regulatory quality, -0.29 for rule of law, and -0.38 for control of corruption. In this section, the analysis should connect labor-force participation, informality, gender, youth employment, skills, and productivity to the administrative problem of converting population into human capital. The statistics should be read with national statistical-office releases and the World Bank, IMF, ILO, and WHO series, so that macro or social averages are not mistaken for administrative reach. Primary country evidence is archived in `sources/documents/official/official_statistics_policy_upgrade_2026.md`, and the interpretive literature is summarized in `sources/documents/academic/academic_literature_upgrade_2026.md`.

The labor-market chapter for the Philippines should be read beyond the conventional unemployment rate. The central issue is whether employment growth is sufficiently inclusive, productive, and institutionally supported to convert macroeconomic expansion into durable human-capital development. In this respect, women's labor-force participation, youth unemployment, vulnerable work, agricultural employment, informality, skills formation, and productivity are not separate social indicators. They are linked measures of how effectively the state connects education, labor regulation, social protection, enterprise development, and regional development.

Recent statistical evidence suggests both improvement and constraint. The total labor-force participation rate stood at 61.35 percent of the population aged 15 and above in 2025, almost unchanged from 2015, when it was only 0.055 percentage points higher. This near-flat trend is administratively significant because it implies that the labor market has not substantially expanded its capacity to draw additional working-age people into paid or actively sought employment. By contrast, female labor-force participation reached 50.49 percent in 2025, increasing by 1.819 percentage points from 2015. The rise indicates gradual improvement, but the level remains well below total participation, implying that gendered barriers to work remain embedded in household responsibilities, care infrastructure, transport conditions, workplace practices, occupational segmentation, and uneven access to secure employment.

Headline unemployment appears comparatively low. Total unemployment was 2.235 percent of the labor force in 2025, a decline of 0.833 percentage points from 2015. Youth unemployment, however, remained higher at 7.041 percent in 2025, despite falling by 1.468 percentage points from 2015. The administrative interpretation is that labor-market exclusion is concentrated less in generalized open unemployment than in the transition from schooling to work, the quality of first jobs, and the capacity of firms to absorb young workers into stable employment. A low aggregate unemployment rate can coexist with underemployment, informality, unpaid family work, own-account activity, temporary work, and occupational mismatch. Therefore, this section should avoid treating low unemployment as proof of labor-market strength. In the Philippine case, the more relevant question is whether workers, especially women and youth, are entering jobs with sufficient earnings, protection, skill accumulation, and productivity growth.

Structural change provides the broader context. In 2024, GDP growth was 5.692 percent, 0.656 percentage points lower than in 2014, while GDP per capita reached US$3,985, an increase of US$1,118 from 2014. These figures indicate continued income growth but not automatic transformation in job quality. The sectoral pattern is revealing: services accounted for 63.22 percent of GDP in 2024, up 6.543 percentage points from 2014; agriculture's share fell to 9.083 percent, down 3.189 percentage points; and industry's share fell to 27.69 percent, down 3.353 percentage points. Employment in agriculture remained 20.49 percent of total employment in 2025, despite declining by 7.363 percentage points from 2015. The gap between agriculture's 9.083 percent share of output and 20.49 percent share of employment points to a persistent productivity divide. Many workers have left agriculture, but a large share remains in a sector that contributes proportionately less to national output. This is a classic sign of incomplete structural transformation: labor is moving, but not always into high-productivity, formal, skill-intensive activities.

For comparative administration, the Philippine case illustrates the difference between formal labor-market rules and implementation practice. Formally, the state may recognize labor rights, regulate employment standards, promote gender equality, invest in technical and vocational training, and maintain programs for youth employment and skills development. In practice, implementation depends on administrative reach, inspection capacity, employer compliance, local-government coordination, budget execution, and the ability to include workers outside standard wage employment. Formal rules are easier to apply in registered firms, urban establishments, and public-sector employment. They are harder to enforce in microenterprises, rural areas, platform-mediated work, family enterprises, seasonal work, subcontracted arrangements, and informal services. Consequently, the legal architecture may appear relatively comprehensive while effective protection remains uneven.

Women's employment illustrates this gap. The increase in female labor-force participation is a positive trend, but it does not by itself confirm equality in employment quality. A specialist should examine whether women are entering formal wage employment, professional occupations, public employment, and growth sectors, or whether they are concentrated in low-paid services, unpaid care, informal commerce, home-based work, and precarious self-employment. The administrative constraint is cross-sectoral: labor policy alone cannot resolve gender gaps if childcare, eldercare, public transport, anti-discrimination enforcement, reproductive health access, workplace safety, and flexible but protected employment arrangements remain weak. The institutional consequence is that women's employment becomes a test of state capacity across ministries and levels of government, not merely a labor-department outcome.

Youth employment reveals a different but related problem. The decline in youth unemployment from 2015 to 2025 suggests improvement, but the 2025 level remains more than three times the total unemployment rate. This difference indicates that young people face entry barriers that older workers do not face to the same degree. These may include lack of work experience, weak school-to-work linkages, uneven quality of secondary and tertiary education, insufficient technical training, limited apprenticeships, credential inflation, and regional mismatch between where young people live and where formal jobs are created. From an administrative perspective, youth unemployment is not only a labor-market indicator; it is also an indicator of education-system performance, career guidance, vocational-training relevance, employer participation, and the credibility of public employment services.

Vulnerable employment and informality should be treated as central, even when exact values are not included in the statistical package. The absence of a supplied vulnerable-employment or informal-employment statistic should not lead the section to ignore the issue. Instead, it should identify the missing evidence as essential for testing the argument. The best indicators would include the share of own-account and contributing family workers in total employment, informal employment as a share of non-agricultural and total employment, underemployment, time-related underemployment, wage and salaried employment, social-insurance coverage, labor-inspection coverage, minimum-wage compliance, union density, contract type, and the share of workers in micro and small enterprises. These measures would show whether employment growth is increasing secure, productive work or merely absorbing labor into low-productivity survival activities.

Skills are equally important. The Philippines has a large service-oriented economy, an important overseas-employment tradition, and a labor market shaped by English-language capacity, care work, business-process services, domestic services, tourism, construction, retail, and public employment. However, a service-heavy economy does not automatically imply a high-skill economy. Services include both high-productivity professional and digital activities and low-productivity personal, retail, transport, and informal activities. The increase in the services share of GDP to 63.22 percent in 2024 therefore requires careful interpretation. It may indicate modernization and urban job creation, but it may also conceal segmented employment, where a smaller group enters higher-productivity service work while many workers remain in low-wage, insecure activities. The key administrative issue is whether education, vocational training, certification, active labor-market programs, and employer coordination are strong enough to move workers from low-productivity to higher-productivity services and industry.

The historical and structural causes of these constraints are several. First, the economy has long combined growth in services and migration-linked employment with a relatively weaker domestic industrial employment base. The 2024 decline in industry's share of GDP compared with 2014 is relevant because manufacturing and related industrial activities often provide pathways for large-scale formal employment, skill upgrading, and productivity diffusion. Second, agricultural employment remains disproportionately high relative to agriculture's output share, indicating continuing rural productivity challenges and uneven regional transformation. Third, informality is reinforced by firm-size structure: where many workers are employed in small, household-based, or unregistered activities, enforcement of labor standards becomes administratively costly. Fourth, gendered care burdens and youth school-to-work mismatch are reproduced through institutions outside the labor ministry, including education, transport, housing, social welfare, and local governance.

The current institutional consequence is fragmented responsibility. Labor-market inclusion depends on coordination among agencies responsible for employment facilitation, education, technical training, industry policy, social protection, local economic development, migration, gender equality, and poverty reduction. Yet the people most in need of integrated support - young first-time workers, rural workers, informal workers, women with care responsibilities, workers displaced from agriculture, and low-skilled service workers - often encounter the state through separate and weakly connected programs. This fragmentation reduces policy credibility because citizens experience policy as announcements rather than reliable pathways into better work. It also weakens development strategy: a country can sustain GDP growth while failing to raise productivity for large sections of the labor force.

For state capacity, the issue is not only whether programs exist, but whether the administration can identify target groups, deliver services, monitor outcomes, and adjust policy when results are weak. A credible labor-market strategy would require more than training slots or employment fairs. It would need accurate local labor-market information, employer-linked curricula, measurable job-placement outcomes, gender-responsive support services, social-protection portability, inspection systems that can reach subcontracted and informal work, and regional strategies for moving workers out of low-productivity agriculture without simply relocating them into urban precarity. Administrative capacity should therefore be assessed through implementation indicators: placement rates after training, earnings changes, formalization rates, female retention in employment, youth transition time from school to work, coverage of employment services, and the share of program beneficiaries from vulnerable groups.

The comparative-administration implication is that the Philippines represents a common middle-income governance dilemma: formal institutions and growth performance may look stronger than the everyday labor-market experience of vulnerable workers. Compared with countries where unemployment is the dominant problem, the Philippine case requires attention to employment quality, productivity, and institutional reach. Compared with industrializing economies that use manufacturing as the main employment escalator, the Philippines must manage a service-led labor market while still addressing agricultural productivity and industrial employment weakness. This makes skills policy more complex because it must serve multiple objectives at once: basic employability, digital and professional upgrading, care-sector standards, rural diversification, industrial technical skills, and pathways for workers in informal services.

Recent evidence that should be checked includes updated labor-force survey data on underemployment, informal employment, vulnerable employment, gender wage gaps, youth not in employment, education, or training, sectoral employment by sex and age, regional employment patterns, social-protection coverage, and training-to-employment outcomes. The most useful statistics for testing this section's claims would be: female labor-force participation by age, marital status, and region; youth unemployment and youth underemployment; NEET rates; informal employment shares; vulnerable employment shares; wage and salaried employment shares; employment by sector and occupation; agriculture employment productivity; labor productivity by sector; education attainment by employment status; technical-vocational certification completion and placement rates; social-insurance coverage among workers; and underemployment by sector. These indicators would reveal whether the labor market is becoming more inclusive and productive or only absorbing workers into low-quality employment.

The institutional dilemma this section should resolve is how the Philippine state can move from labor absorption to labor upgrading. A narrow strategy would celebrate low unemployment and moderate GDP growth. A stronger administrative interpretation would ask whether women, youth, rural workers, and informal workers are gaining access to formal, productive, protected, and skill-enhancing employment. The available statistics point to gradual gains - lower unemployment, lower youth unemployment, rising female participation, rising GDP per capita, and declining agricultural employment - but they also reveal unresolved constraints: stagnant total participation, persistent youth disadvantage, a large agricultural employment share relative to output, a declining industry share, and a service-led structure that may or may not generate broad productivity gains. Future reform will be risky if it promises inclusive growth without building the institutions needed to implement it. Conversely, reform credibility will increase if labor-market policy is tied to measurable improvements in job quality, skills utilization, gender inclusion, youth transitions, rural productivity, and formalization.
