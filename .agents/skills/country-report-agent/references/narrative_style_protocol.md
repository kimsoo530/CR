# Narrative Style Protocol

For revisions spanning several sections, pair this protocol with `chapter_coherence_protocol.md`. Sentence-level variety cannot compensate for duplicated section roles or a chapter that lacks a governing question.

Use this reference when turning evidence, audit findings, statistical results, or section drafts into reader-facing book prose.

## Core Principle

Use mechanical tools for diagnosis, but do not let mechanical tools become the prose. The manuscript should read as an interpretive country report: evidence-based, specific, and analytical, but not a checklist, source inventory, or prompt transcript.

## Separate Workflow From Manuscript

Keep these materials out of reader-facing sections unless the user explicitly asks for an appendix:

- claim-evidence-source tables
- source gap tables
- audit script output
- update instructions
- prompt-like commands
- checklist headings
- raw evidence inventories
- repeated labels such as `Evidence Used`, `Reading the Evidence`, `Figure interpretation`, or `The analytical value of`

Use those materials to decide what to write. Then write prose that explains what the evidence means.

## Reader-Facing Paragraph Standard

A strong country-report paragraph should normally do at least three of these things:

- state a country-specific analytical claim
- name the relevant institution, law, policy, region, event, or reform
- explain the mechanism that connects formal rules to actual state capacity
- use evidence to test, qualify, or complicate the claim
- interpret statistics in relation to implementation, fiscal space, social pressure, political economy, or policy risk
- acknowledge uncertainty or source limits without derailing the prose
- connect the paragraph to the chapter's argument or the next paragraph

Do not require every paragraph to follow the same sequence. Vary the structure according to the material.

## Narrative Patterns

Choose the pattern that fits the section. Do not force one template across all sections.

Patterns describe argumentative movement, not mandatory headings. Never print pattern labels or their component names merely to demonstrate compliance. Select a different pattern for neighboring sections when their substantive questions differ; keep a shared pattern only when the material genuinely demands it.

### Historical Development Pattern

Use when the section explains institutional formation or reform sequence.

Shape:

```text
Background or inherited structure -> turning point or reform episode -> current institutional consequence
```

Best for chapters 2, 3, 5, 15, and 16.

### Institutional Operation Pattern

Use when the section compares formal design and actual practice.

Shape:

```text
Formal rule -> operating mechanism -> implementation constraint -> implication for state capacity
```

Best for chapters 3, 5, 6, 7, 14, and 17.

### Statistical Interpretation Pattern

Use when quantitative evidence clarifies a claim.

Shape:

```text
Substantive question -> trend or level -> comparison or turning point -> interpretation -> limitation if needed
```

Best for chapters 7 through 14 and 17.

### Comparative Position Pattern

Use when the country's position matters relative to peers, region, or income group.

Shape:

```text
Observed difference -> reason comparison matters -> likely institutional or structural explanation -> cautious conclusion
```

Use rankings sparingly and identify the comparator set.

### Contested Interpretation Pattern

Use when academic, policy, or political interpretations differ.

Shape:

```text
Issue in dispute -> competing interpretations -> evidence that supports or limits each view -> balanced judgment
```

Best for political economy, governance, conflict, decentralization, reform, and development-strategy sections.

## Statistical Prose Rules

Numbers should support the paragraph's interpretation, not replace it.

Prefer:

```text
The revenue trend points to a narrower fiscal base than the formal expenditure responsibilities imply.
```

Avoid:

```text
The tax revenue indicator was X in year Y. The GDP indicator was Z in year Y. The debt indicator was A in year Y.
```

When using statistics:

- introduce the substantive reason for the number
- give value, year, unit, and source close to the claim
- explain what the number changes about the reader's understanding
- avoid long chains of figures unless the paragraph is explicitly summarizing a table
- avoid beginning several consecutive sentences with source names

## Citation Style In Prose

Citations should support the prose without making the prose feel like a database export.

Prefer paragraph-level citations when several adjacent sentences rely on the same source. Use precise citations for laws, tables, direct quotations, and contested claims.

Avoid:

- citing every sentence in a mechanical rhythm
- stacking many institutional names before the reader knows the claim
- using source names as the main actors when the institution, law, or policy is the real subject

## Sentence Rhythm

Vary sentence openings and paragraph shapes. Avoid repeated patterns such as:

```text
This section shows...
The data indicate...
The evidence suggests...
The chapter demonstrates...
```

Use transitions that carry analysis:

- "This matters because..."
- "The result is not simply..."
- "The contrast between formal authority and implementation capacity..."
- "The main implication for public administration is..."
- "The limitation of this evidence is..."

Use them sparingly. Repetition makes even good transitions sound mechanical.

## Voice And Tone

Write as an expert analyst explaining a country, not as an assistant reporting task completion.

Use:

- precise but readable sentences
- cautious claims when evidence is incomplete
- active interpretation
- country-specific nouns
- restrained evaluation

Avoid:

- promotional language
- generic development cliches
- overconfident causal claims
- direct references to the report production process
- phrases such as `this report will`, `this section should`, `the analysis should`, or `further research is needed` unless used in a true methodology appendix

## Mechanical-Prose Red Flags

Revise if a section contains:

- the same paragraph structure repeated three or more times
- lists of source names without interpretation
- numbers presented without explaining why they matter
- generic sentences that could apply to any country
- labels copied from audit outputs or evidence plans
- every paragraph ending with a limitation sentence
- abrupt insertion of charts without narrative setup
- citations that visually dominate the paragraph
- terms such as `evidence plan`, `source register`, `audit`, or `workflow` in reader-facing prose
- repeated headings such as `Analytical claim`, `Statistical evidence`, `Mechanisms`, `What the evidence can establish`, or `Policy implications` across otherwise different sections
- identical methodological caveats or falsification paragraphs copied into multiple sections
- a figure inserted at the same structural position in every section regardless of the argument

## Chapter-Sensitive Composition

Use these as selection cues, not templates:

- Country overview: establish scale, territorial diversity, and interpretive orientation; keep navigation light.
- Historical background: organize around sequence, turning points, and institutional legacies.
- Constitutional and political institutions: move between formal rules, operating practice, conflict, and consequences; foreground contested interpretation where appropriate.
- Administration and governance: connect organizational design to implementation chains, capacity, accountability, and citizen experience.
- Fiscal, economic, infrastructure, labor, and social chapters: let the substantive question determine whether a trend, comparison, distribution, or institutional account should lead.
- Financial and external-relations chapters: connect instruments and institutional authority to transmission channels, constraints, and strategic trade-offs.
- Strategy and integrated assessment: synthesize prior evidence, surface cross-domain tensions, and prioritize choices without repeating chapter summaries.
- Method and evidence appendices: use explicit methodological organization because workflow and limitations are themselves the subject.

Within each chapter, vary section openings and endings. A section may open with a historical episode, a formal rule, a puzzle in the data, a territorial contrast, or a scholarly disagreement. End with the consequence that best advances the next section, not with a mandatory limitation or generic policy checklist.

## Human Review Questions

Before finalizing a rewritten section, ask:

1. Would a reader learn something specific about this country, not only about the template?
2. Does the strongest evidence serve an argument rather than sit beside it?
3. Are statistics interpreted in relation to institutions, state capacity, or policy risk?
4. Is the paragraph order natural for a book chapter?
5. Has workflow language been removed from reader-facing prose?
6. Does the section sound like one authorial voice rather than stitched fragments?

## Revision Move

When prose feels mechanical:

1. Identify the central claim of the paragraph.
2. Move source names, table details, and audit findings into supporting positions.
3. Put the country-specific institution, reform, constraint, or policy problem in the subject position.
4. Combine isolated evidence sentences into one interpretive paragraph.
5. Keep only the citations needed for traceability.
6. End with the implication, not with a checklist item.
