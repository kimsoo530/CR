# Chapter 3. Constitution, Territorial Order, and Competing Authorities

## 3.1 Constitutional foundations and territorial state structure

The 2008 Constitution presents Myanmar as a Union in which legislative, executive, and judicial power is separated "to the extent possible" and shared among the Union, Regions, States, and Self-Administered Areas. Its territorial design therefore combines a unitary constitutional frame with differentiated subnational institutions. The text designates seven Regions and seven States as equal in status, identifies Nay Pyi Taw as a Union Territory under the President's direct administration, and permits additional Union Territories to be created by law for areas with special defence, security, administrative, or economic circumstances (Republic of the Union of Myanmar, 2008, secs. 9, 11-18, 49-50).

The Constitution also defines an administrative hierarchy from villages and wards through townships and districts to Regions or States. That hierarchy specifies the constitutional route through which Union authority is meant to reach local government. It is not a description of a uniformly autonomous local-government system. The text allocates Union legislative power to the Pyidaungsu Hluttaw and subnational legislative power to Region and State Hluttaws, while reserving specified matters for Self-Administered Area institutions (Republic of the Union of Myanmar, 2008, secs. 12-18, 51).

The self-administered arrangement is both territorially specific and institutionally constrained. Section 56 establishes five Self-Administered Zones--Naga, Danu, Pa-O, Pa Laung, and Kokang--and the Wa Self-Administered Division. Their Leading Bodies exercise legislative authority over Schedule Three matters and corresponding executive authority, but their composition is not wholly separate from Region/State and military structures. The bodies include elected Region or State Hluttaw representatives, Defence Services personnel nominated for security or border-affairs duties, and additional representatives; the Constitution provides for Defence Services personnel to occupy one-quarter of the Leading Body where necessary. The chairperson is selected from elected area representatives, appointed by the President, serves as an ex officio Region or State minister, and is responsible through the Region or State Chief Minister to the President (Republic of the Union of Myanmar, 2008, secs. 56, 276-277).

This arrangement makes self-administration a constitutionally delimited form of authority rather than a federal division with a separate constituent constitutional status. The Union retains the power to determine territorial boundaries through constitutionally prescribed procedures, and the constitutional order makes Regions, States, Union Territories, and Self-Administered Areas parts of a single non-secession framework (Republic of the Union of Myanmar, 2008, secs. 10, 51-56). Formal territorial design is consequently inseparable from the wider distribution of executive, legislative, and security authority.

That de jure design cannot be treated as a map of post-2021 administration. The UN High Commissioner for Human Rights reported in 2024 that shifting control over different areas had brought forward governance systems involving civil society and civil-disobedience actors, the NUG, and ethnic armed organizations, with some systems providing services and justice-administration processes. The report is evidence of institutional divergence and changing local arrangements at that time; it does not establish a stable allocation of territorial control, recognition, or legal authority throughout Myanmar (United Nations Human Rights Council, 2024, paras. 45-48).

## 3.2 Institutionalized military authority and amendment rules

The 2008 Constitution embeds the Defence Services in representative institutions rather than locating the military only within the executive. It provides for up to 110 Defence Services representatives in the 440-member Pyithu Hluttaw, 56 in the 224-member Amyotha Hluttaw, and one-quarter of Region or State Hluttaw representatives. These representatives are nominated by the Commander-in-Chief rather than elected through ordinary constituencies (Republic of the Union of Myanmar, 2008, secs. 109(b), 141(b), 161(d)). The Constitution also describes the Defence Services as independently administering and adjudicating its own affairs, assigns the Commander-in-Chief as supreme commander, and gives the Defence Services a principal responsibility for safeguarding the Constitution (sec. 20).

The military's position in the legislature interacts directly with constitutional amendment. Section 436 requires approval by more than three-quarters of all representatives of the Pyidaungsu Hluttaw for amendments to protected provisions, followed by approval in a referendum by more than half of eligible voters. The text does not call this a military veto. The institutional consequence nevertheless follows from the combined design: because Defence Services representatives hold one-quarter of seats, an amendment governed by that threshold cannot obtain the required parliamentary support without votes from at least part of the military-appointed bloc (Republic of the Union of Myanmar, 2008, secs. 109(b), 141(b), 436).

Ministerial appointment rules extend this relationship into the executive. Although the President appoints Union ministers, the Constitution requires the President to select the Ministers for Defence, Home Affairs, and Border Affairs from lists submitted by the Commander-in-Chief; these ministers must be Defence Services personnel. The same text contemplates Defence Services personnel in Union, Region, State, Union Territory, Self-Administered Area, and district executives for defence, security, and border-administration functions (Republic of the Union of Myanmar, 2008, secs. 17(b), 232(b)(ii), 232(j)(ii)). The result is a constitutional allocation of security-sector authority that affects both Union policy and the administrative chain below it.

These rules are important for interpreting constitutional change before and after 2021. They do not prove how any individual institution operated in every locality or period. They do establish why military authority, territorial administration, and amendment politics cannot be analysed as separate issues in Myanmar's constitutional order.

## 3.3 Citizenship, rights, judiciary, and constitutional review

Chapter VIII of the Constitution establishes a rights framework but qualifies many rights through citizenship status, existing law, public order, and emergency provisions. Section 345 identifies citizens through descent from parents who are nationals or prior citizenship recognized when the Constitution entered into force, while section 346 leaves citizenship, naturalization, and revocation to law. Sections 347-352 provide equal legal protection, non-discrimination among citizens on listed grounds, and equal opportunity in public employment and economic activity (Republic of the Union of Myanmar, 2008, secs. 345-352).

The 1982 Burma Citizenship Law is relevant because it supplies a separate statutory architecture to which the Constitution delegates key citizenship questions. The English legal-text archive identifies citizens, associate citizens, and naturalized citizens as distinct legal categories. Its section 3 links citizenship to specified national groups and settlement before 1823, while section 4 gives the then Council of State a role in deciding whether an ethnic group is national. The law is preserved here through a UNHCR/ILO legal archive, not as a contemporary official Myanmar publication or a substitute for the Burmese original (Myanmar, 1982, secs. 2-7). This is sufficient to show that citizenship is not defined by the constitutional text alone; it is not sufficient to make a current case-specific determination about any person's status.

The Constitution protects personal liberty, expression, peaceful assembly, association, residence, property, privacy, due process, and defence rights, but the terms of protection matter. Freedom of expression, assembly, association, and cultural or religious practice under section 354 are available only where not contrary to laws enacted for Union security, law and order, community peace and tranquillity, public order, or morality. Section 353 similarly protects life and personal liberty except in accordance with existing laws. Sections 376-381 provide safeguards concerning detention, access to the Supreme Court, writs, and due process, while permitting specified limitations in war, invasion, insurrection, or emergency (Republic of the Union of Myanmar, 2008, secs. 353-355, 376-382).

The distinction between legal design and enforcement is particularly important. The 2024 UN report documented that people lacking citizenship-scrutiny cards faced risks connected with movement, extortion, arrest, and detention in the conflict environment. That finding does not amend the constitutional text or determine the legal status of all affected groups. It does demonstrate that legal documentation and rights protections must be read alongside the administrative and security conditions through which they are applied (United Nations Human Rights Council, 2024, para. 22).

The formal judiciary consists of the Supreme Court of the Union, Region and State High Courts, courts of Self-Administered Divisions and Zones, District and Township Courts, Courts-Martial, and the Constitutional Tribunal. The Supreme Court is the highest ordinary court, has final appellate jurisdiction subject to the Constitution, and may issue habeas corpus, mandamus, prohibition, quo warranto, and certiorari writs. The President nominates the Chief Justice and Supreme Court judges for Pyidaungsu Hluttaw approval; the legislature may refuse only where prescribed qualifications are clearly not met (Republic of the Union of Myanmar, 2008, secs. 293-301).

Courts-Martial are a distinct constitutional category and adjudicate Defence Services personnel. This matters because the Constitution separately grants the Defence Services the right independently to administer and adjudicate armed-forces affairs, and permits rights in Chapter VIII to be restricted or revoked by law for armed-forces discipline and duties (Republic of the Union of Myanmar, 2008, secs. 20(b), 319, 382). The provisions identify a differentiated military-justice sphere; they do not establish how Courts-Martial have operated in particular post-2021 cases.

The Constitutional Tribunal is designed as a separate constitutional-review institution rather than an appellate chamber of the Supreme Court. It has nine members: three selected by the President, three by the Speaker of the Pyithu Hluttaw, and three by the Speaker of the Amyotha Hluttaw. Its functions include interpretation of the Constitution, review of the constitutional conformity of legislation and executive measures, and resolution of specified Union-subnational disputes. Its resolutions are final and conclusive. Access provisions list national officeholders, specified subnational leaders, and at least 10 percent of either Union chamber; they do not provide an individual citizen with a direct application route (Republic of the Union of Myanmar, 2008, secs. 320-326).

The constitutional design thus combines formal judicial independence principles with appointment processes and access rules centred on state institutions. It should not be read as proof of effective judicial independence after 2021. For the competing federal-democratic framework, International IDEA's 2022 analysis of original and revised FDC material found that the interim arrangements did not provide an independent body for constitutional review and left the contours of interim judicial authority uncertain. This is an analysis of the Charter framework, not evidence that one judicial system has displaced another nationwide (International IDEA, 2022b, pp. 9-10, 18).

## 3.4 Emergency powers, the 2021 rupture, and competing constitutional orders

The Constitution's emergency provisions provide the formal route through which exceptional authority can be concentrated. Section 40(c) says that, in an emergency threatening Union disintegration, national solidarity, or sovereign power through wrongful forcible means, the Commander-in-Chief may take over and exercise State sovereign power in accordance with the Constitution. Sections 417-419 set out the President's emergency proclamation after coordination with the National Defence and Security Council, the transfer of legislative, executive, and judicial powers to the Commander-in-Chief, and the formation of bodies necessary for the exercise of State functions. Sections 425, 426, and 429 address extension, termination, and the election timetable (Republic of the Union of Myanmar, 2008, secs. 40(c), 417-419, 425-426, 429).

On 1 February 2021, the Office of the President's Order No. 1/2021 invoked section 417, cited alleged unresolved electoral issues and the convening of Hluttaws, declared a one-year emergency, and stated that section 418(a) transferred legislative, executive, and judicial powers to the Commander-in-Chief. On 2 February, the Office of the Commander-in-Chief issued Order No. 9/2021 forming the State Administration Council and explicitly citing section 419. The Union Election Commission's 5 February announcement repeated the asserted sections 417 and 418(a) basis and stated that Hluttaw legislative processes were suspended (Office of the President, 2021; Office of the Commander-in-Chief of Defence Services, 2021; Union Election Commission, 2021).

Those texts are primary evidence of the legal position taken by their issuing authorities, not an independent legal determination. The UN Special Rapporteur concluded that the alleged voter irregularities did not meet the section 417 conditions and characterized the sequence involving the removal of President Win Myint, the declaration by Myint Swe, and National Defence and Security Council procedure as constitutionally unlawful (United Nations Human Rights Council, 2021, paras. 11-14). The SAC-side records and the UN assessment therefore answer different questions: one documents an asserted constitutional basis, and the other provides an external legal-institutional evaluation of it.

The locally archived NDSC records establish selected later claims, not a complete extension chronology. The 31 January 2022 record invoked section 425 for a six-month extension and referred to elections under section 429. The 31 January 2023 record again invoked section 425 and referred to assignment of responsibilities under section 419. The 31 July 2024 record claimed a further six-month extension from 1 August under section 425. The 31 July 2025 record referred to section 426 in a claimed revocation of Order No. 1/2021 and to section 429 for an election within six months (National Defence and Security Council, 2022, 2023, 2025; National Defence and Security Council Office, 2024). No conclusion about the legal validity of those asserted steps follows from their inclusion in official records.

CRPH and NUG documents articulate a competing constitutional framework. CRPH Announcement No. 20/2021 declared the complete abolition of the 2008 Constitution on 31 March 2021. CRPH Announcement No. 19/2021 promulgated Part I of the Federal Democracy Charter, while the locally archived combined English Charter sets out a federal-democratic roadmap and interim constitutional arrangements. On 16 April 2021, CRPH Announcement No. 23/2021 stated that CRPH had formed the NUG in accordance with the Charter and listed cabinet members (Committee Representing Pyidaungsu Hluttaw, 2021a, 2021b, 2021c).

The sources establish these acts and claims by CRPH. They do not settle their legal validity, international recognition, territorial reach, or implementation. The locally archived combined English Charter identifies itself only as a 2021 text and combines Part I and Part II; its exact combined-edition history is therefore not fully established. International IDEA distinguishes the separate abolition declaration from the Charter and describes the Charter as a proto-constitutional framework rather than a final constitution, with legal and political questions remaining contested (International IDEA, 2022a, p. 15; International IDEA, 2022b, pp. 9-10).

Myanmar's constitutional condition after 2021 is consequently not a simple replacement of one text by another. The 2008 Constitution remains the primary source for the formal legal design examined in this chapter. SAC-side records assert a continuity route through emergency powers. CRPH/NUG instruments assert abolition and an alternative federal-democratic transition. Dated UN evidence documents changing local governance arrangements without resolving the nationwide reach of any order. This fragmentation shapes the analysis in Chapter 4 by making political authority a question of institutions, claims, and operating conditions; in Chapter 5 by complicating administrative chains of command; and in Chapter 6 by making formal capacity indicators insufficient without attention to where and through whom public functions are performed.
