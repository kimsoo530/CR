# Chapter 3 Constitutional Core: Review Candidate

## 3.1 Constitutional order, military participation, and amendment rules

Myanmar's 2008 Constitution did not place the armed forces outside the representative institutions. It reserved constitutionally specified seats for Defence Services personnel in each legislature: up to 110 of 440 seats in the Pyithu Hluttaw, 56 of 224 seats in the Amyotha Hluttaw, and one-quarter of seats in Region or State Hluttaws. Those representatives are nominated by the Commander-in-Chief rather than elected through the ordinary constituency process (Republic of the Union of Myanmar, 2008, arts. 109(b), 141(b), 161(d)). The design therefore makes military participation a rule of the constitutional order, rather than merely a matter of party competition or executive appointment.

The same design materially affects constitutional amendment. Section 436 requires the approval of more than 75 percent of all representatives of the Pyidaungsu Hluttaw for amendments to its protected provisions and, for those provisions, subsequent approval by more than half of eligible voters in a referendum (Republic of the Union of Myanmar, 2008, art. 436). Because appointed Defence Services representatives occupy one-quarter of the Union legislatures, a proposed amendment to a protected provision cannot clear the parliamentary threshold without at least some support from that bloc. The point is institutional rather than numerical alone: the amendment formula turns the military's constitutionally allocated parliamentary presence into a veto point over core constitutional change.

Control over three security-related ministries reinforces this arrangement. The Constitution provides that the President appoints Union ministers, but for Defence, Home Affairs, and Border Affairs the President selects from lists submitted by the Commander-in-Chief; the ministers must be Defence Services personnel (Republic of the Union of Myanmar, 2008, arts. 232(b)(ii), 232(j)(ii)). Article 20 also assigns the Defence Services a leading role in safeguarding the Constitution and grants it independent administration of armed forces personnel. Together, these provisions combine representation, ministerial nomination, and a constitutional guardianship role. They help explain why the political significance of the 2008 text cannot be assessed by looking only at elected offices.

This architecture does not settle how power would be exercised in every political crisis. It does, however, identify the legal mechanisms through which security institutions were embedded in national decision-making before 2021: reserved legislative seats, a high amendment threshold, and a distinct appointment channel for core security ministries. A fuller account of citizenship, rights, courts, and territorial administration requires separate evidence beyond this constitutional core.

## 3.4 Emergency powers and competing constitutional claims after 2021

The emergency provisions in Chapter XI created an exceptional route for the concentration of state authority. Under section 417, the President may proclaim a state of emergency after coordinating with the National Defence and Security Council where specified threats to sovereignty or the disintegration of the Union are said to exist. Section 418(a) provides for the transfer of legislative, executive, and judicial powers to the Commander-in-Chief when such an emergency is declared. Section 419 permits the Commander-in-Chief to exercise those transferred powers and to form a body or bodies necessary for the exercise of state functions. Sections 425, 426, and 429 address extension, termination, and the subsequent election timetable (Republic of the Union of Myanmar, 2008, arts. 417-419, 425-426, 429).

On 1 February 2021, the Office of the President's Order No. 1/2021 invoked section 417, referred to alleged unresolved electoral issues and the convening of Hluttaws, declared a one-year emergency, and stated that section 418(a) required the transfer of legislative, executive, and judicial powers to the Commander-in-Chief (Office of the President, 2021). The following day, the Office of the Commander-in-Chief issued Order No. 9/2021 forming the State Administration Council and expressly citing section 419 (Office of the Commander-in-Chief of Defence Services, 2021). A 5 February announcement of the Union Election Commission repeated the asserted sections 417 and 418(a) basis and stated that Hluttaw legislative processes were suspended (Union Election Commission, 2021).

These documents establish what the institutions issuing them claimed; they do not, by themselves, establish the legality of those claims. The UN Special Rapporteur concluded that the alleged electoral irregularities did not meet the conditions for a section 417 emergency and characterized the sequence involving the removal of President Win Myint, the declaration by Myint Swe, and the necessary National Defence and Security Council procedure as constitutionally unlawful (United Nations Human Rights Council, 2021, paras. 11-14). The analytical distinction matters. The official documents are primary evidence of the legal justification asserted by the SAC-side authorities, while the UN report is an external legal-institutional assessment of that justification.

The available official record also documents selected later steps in the emergency narrative, but it is not a complete chronological archive of every intervening order. The NDSC's 31 January 2022 meeting record invoked section 425 for a six-month extension and connected the claimed transition to elections under section 429. Its 31 January 2023 record again invoked section 425 for a further six-month period and referred to the assignment of state responsibilities under section 419. A 31 July 2024 NDSC Office record claimed a further six-month extension from 1 August 2024 under section 425. On 31 July 2025, an NDSC meeting record referred to section 426 in a claimed revocation of Order No. 1/2021 and to section 429 for an election within six months (National Defence and Security Council, 2022, 2023, 2025; National Defence and Security Council Office, 2024). These are official assertions about constitutional procedure, not independent confirmation that the conditions or consequences they assert were legally satisfied.

Post-2021 constitutional politics also includes competing claims to replace the 2008 order. CRPH Announcement No. 20/2021 declared the complete abolition of the 2008 Constitution on 31 March 2021. Its Federal Democracy Charter set out a political roadmap for a federal-democratic order and contains interim constitutional arrangements. On 16 April 2021, CRPH Announcement No. 23/2021 stated that CRPH had formed the National Unity Government in accordance with the Charter and listed cabinet members (Committee Representing Pyidaungsu Hluttaw, 2021a, 2021b, 2021c). These sources are direct evidence of CRPH's own acts and claimed authority. They do not independently resolve the legal validity, recognition, territorial reach, or administrative implementation of the alternative order.

International IDEA treats the Charter and the abolition declaration as distinct acts. It notes that the Charter envisages replacement of the 2008 Constitution but is not itself a final constitution, and characterizes its legal and political status as contested (International IDEA, 2022, p. 15). This distinction is useful for the report's wider governance analysis. Myanmar's constitutional problem is not only a dispute over interpretation of one text; it is also a contest between an emergency-power claim made through the 2008 Constitution and an alternative federal-democratic constitutional project. That contest has direct implications for administrative coordination, security governance, legislative authority, and the ability of public institutions to operate through a single accepted chain of authority.
