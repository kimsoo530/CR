## 5.3 Human Resource Management and Civil Service

### 5.3 Civil Service and Bureaucratic Organization

The civil-service framework supplies the personnel rules through which formal administration is supposed to operate. The 2013 law defines service-personnel organizations, specifies the scope of ministries and self-administered areas, and assigns a central role to President-appointed heads of service organizations (Union Civil Services Board, 2013, pp. 3-4). The 2014 Rules then provide procedures for appointment, transfer, promotion, training and discipline. Their structure indicates a career administration managed through formal personnel organizations and a Union Civil Service Board, with coordination between ministries, the Board, the Union Government and, in some cases, the President (Union Civil Service Board, 2014, pp. 15-24).

This legal design is separate from observed administrative capacity. The acquired legal sources say how personnel are to be appointed and managed; they do not supply current headcounts, vacancy rates, attendance, deployment, resignation or service-output data. Nor do the rules establish how a civil servant is to be classified when an office is formally retained but local authority, security conditions or instructions are disputed. The civil-service law therefore cannot serve as evidence of a functioning nationwide bureaucracy.

The Asia Foundation's 2019 analysis helps identify a pre-2021 accountability problem: state and region department heads could feel accountable both to their state/region government and to their Union ministry, while the formal allocation of responsibilities remained incomplete or ambiguous (The Asia Foundation, 2019, pp. 58-64). This is analytically different from post-2021 disruption. It shows that dual accountability existed within the constitutional arrangement before the coup; it does not establish how that arrangement now operates in areas where state institutions, departmental offices or personnel are contested.

The personnel circuit also has a territorial dimension. The Rules provide a common framework for service personnel paid from the State Budget, while the 2019 subnational study describes departments operating through State/Region and township offices. This combination creates a formal chain in which personnel rules are centrally standardized but implementation is mediated through territorial offices and intergovernmental relationships. It helps explain why administrative capacity cannot be inferred from the existence of a ministry alone: the relevant question is whether the ministry's personnel, instructions, budget and reporting channels connect to the office expected to act locally.
