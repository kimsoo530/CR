## 3.1 Constitutional Order and Amendments

### 3.2 Institutionalized military authority and amendment rules

The 2008 Constitution embeds the Defence Services in representative institutions rather than locating the military only within the executive. It provides for up to 110 Defence Services representatives in the 440-member Pyithu Hluttaw, 56 in the 224-member Amyotha Hluttaw, and one-quarter of Region or State Hluttaw representatives. These representatives are nominated by the Commander-in-Chief rather than elected through ordinary constituencies (Republic of the Union of Myanmar, 2008, secs. 109(b), 141(b), 161(d)). The Constitution also describes the Defence Services as independently administering and adjudicating its own affairs, assigns the Commander-in-Chief as supreme commander, and gives the Defence Services a principal responsibility for safeguarding the Constitution (sec. 20).

The military's position in the legislature interacts directly with constitutional amendment. Section 436 requires approval by more than three-quarters of all representatives of the Pyidaungsu Hluttaw for amendments to protected provisions, followed by approval in a referendum by more than half of eligible voters. The text does not call this a military veto. The institutional consequence nevertheless follows from the combined design: because Defence Services representatives hold one-quarter of seats, an amendment governed by that threshold cannot obtain the required parliamentary support without votes from at least part of the military-appointed bloc (Republic of the Union of Myanmar, 2008, secs. 109(b), 141(b), 436).

Ministerial appointment rules extend this relationship into the executive. Although the President appoints Union ministers, the Constitution requires the President to select the Ministers for Defence, Home Affairs, and Border Affairs from lists submitted by the Commander-in-Chief; these ministers must be Defence Services personnel. The same text contemplates Defence Services personnel in Union, Region, State, Union Territory, Self-Administered Area, and district executives for defence, security, and border-administration functions (Republic of the Union of Myanmar, 2008, secs. 17(b), 232(b)(ii), 232(j)(ii)). The result is a constitutional allocation of security-sector authority that affects both Union policy and the administrative chain below it.

These rules are important for interpreting constitutional change before and after 2021. They do not prove how any individual institution operated in every locality or period. They do establish why military authority, territorial administration, and amendment politics cannot be analysed as separate issues in Myanmar's constitutional order.
