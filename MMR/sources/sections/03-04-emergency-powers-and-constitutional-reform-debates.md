Myanmar's constitutional order is the formal grammar of public power. It defines sovereignty, executive authority, legislatures, courts, rights, territorial administration, public finance, emergency powers, and amendment rules, but those rules acquire meaning through political practice and administrative capacity.

The organization of state power determines how policy is authorized, coordinated, reviewed, and corrected. The key issue in Myanmar is not only whether the constitution names executive, legislative, and judicial organs, but how those organs interact with political parties, security institutions, audit bodies, local governments, and the bureaucracy.

Emergency powers, audit institutions, and constitutional review mechanisms deserve careful attention because they reveal how the state behaves under stress. Security crises, fiscal scandals, corruption cases, disasters, and political conflict test whether constitutional checks are routine constraints or exceptional instruments used selectively by dominant actors.

The executive often has the greatest effect on implementation capacity because it controls cabinet coordination, budget execution, appointments, security administration, and national planning. Legislatures matter when they authorize budgets, scrutinize ministries, represent territorial interests, and make laws clear enough for officials to implement. Courts and audit bodies matter when they make rules credible after decisions have been made.

A constitutional map of state power therefore becomes an administrative map. It shows where bottlenecks may arise: overcentralized executive control, weak legislative oversight, politicized courts, fragmented local authority, underfunded audit institutions, or emergency rules that displace ordinary accountability. Myanmar's later governance indicators are interpreted against this institutional map.

The evidence base combines official constitutional texts, amendment acts, parliamentary or justice-ministry materials, constitutional-court or supreme-court sources where available, and credible comparative legal compilations. Indicators such as rule of law or voice and accountability can support interpretation, but they do not substitute for legal and institutional analysis.
