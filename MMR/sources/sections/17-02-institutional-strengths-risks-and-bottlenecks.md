## 17.2 Institutional Strengths, Risks, and Bottlenecks

### 17.2 Administrative and Fiscal Capacity

Chapter 5 documents a formal administrative machinery that includes Union ministries, the General Administration Department, territorial layers and civil-service rules. It also shows why formal presence cannot be treated as uniform effective administration. Administrative continuity, personnel availability, reporting, local coordination and service delivery may diverge from the legal or organizational chart. The relevant capacity question is not whether offices exist in the abstract, but whether instructions, personnel, resources and services move through the chain in a given place and period (Kyi Pyar Chit Saw & Arnold, 2014; The Asia Foundation, 2019).

Chapter 7 adds a fiscal constraint to that chain. The verified statistical package supports selected international fiscal series for revenue, fiscal balance and general-government gross debt, with unit and time-series qualifications. It does not support a complete current account of expenditure, budget execution, audit performance, SOE exposure or conflict-related revenue. The unresolved IMF expenditure raw source remains excluded from publication-ready statistical coverage. These limitations prevent a precise national fiscal-capacity score.

The cross-chapter implication is narrower and more useful: implementation capacity is shaped by the relationship between administrative machinery and resources, but the available evidence does not show that either formal structure or aggregate fiscal indicators translated uniformly into local operation. Fiscal capacity is therefore a documented formal architecture plus partially reproducible aggregate indicators, not a fully observed national operating capacity.

### 17.3 Security, Territorial Reach and Service Delivery

Chapter 15 shows that security conditions and humanitarian pressures are not external to governance. Conflict affects access, displacement, service continuity and the practical environment in which administrative instructions can be carried out. Chapter 5 similarly requires a distinction between formal territorial administration and uneven effective reach. Together, these findings support a mechanism in which insecurity and displacement raise the cost of coordination and make service delivery dependent on access, personnel, local arrangements and the authority able to operate in a particular area.

This does not justify a current control map. The local evidence lacks a sufficiently comprehensive, dated and methodologically transparent spatial dataset covering the relevant actors. The defensible formulation is that administrative and security reach varies by locality and period, while the exact national distribution of effective control remains unresolved. Humanitarian planning figures must also retain their population definitions and reference dates; IDPs, refugees, asylum seekers and other groups cannot be combined into one undifferentiated measure.

The governance consequence is operational rather than rhetorical. Where the formal administrative chain is interrupted or access is restricted, education, health, protection and humanitarian coordination may depend on multiple institutional and non-state arrangements. The evidence supports examining these arrangements as mechanisms of service provision and authority, but not treating any single locality as representative of the whole country.
