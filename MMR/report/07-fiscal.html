Fiscal capacity is the point at which state ambition becomes administratively real. In Myanmar, revenue determines how far the government can finance social policy, infrastructure, public employment, debt service, and local transfers without relying on unstable borrowing or one-off income. Expenditure shows the scale of the state's commitments, but it has to be interpreted together with revenue, tax capacity, and the fiscal balance.

This section separates two comparable evidence layers. IMF DataMapper is used for the broad general-government picture, while World Bank WDI is used for central-government revenue excluding grants, tax revenue, and expense. These series do not always have identical institutional coverage, so they should be read as complementary diagnostics rather than mechanically interchangeable numbers.

On the IMF general-government series, revenue is 15.6% of GDP and expenditure is 20.7% of GDP, implying a spending level above revenue of 5.1% of GDP. On the World Bank central-government series, revenue excluding grants is 14.4% of GDP and expense is 13.8% of GDP. Tax revenue is 6.0% of GDP, so the gap between total revenue and tax revenue indicates the role of grants, non-tax revenue, commodity revenue, social contributions, or other public income. For Myanmar, the key fiscal question is whether recurring revenue is broad and stable enough to finance the expenditure commitments described in the development strategy. Rising expenditure can indicate stronger service provision and public investment, but if it persistently outruns revenue it also creates arrears, borrowing pressure, or cuts in maintenance and local service delivery.

The analytical test is not whether spending is high or low in isolation. A low-expenditure state may lack the administrative reach to deliver education, health, infrastructure, and social protection; a high-expenditure state may face sustainability risks if revenue is narrow, volatile, or politically costly to collect. The revenue-expenditure trend therefore links public finance to implementation capacity: it shows whether policy promises are backed by a recurring fiscal base.

Defense and public-security spending in Myanmar should be read as a fiscal commitment as well as a security indicator. Armed forces personnel account for 1.9% of the total labor force in the latest World Bank series. Military expenditure is 6.8% of GDP and 29.1% of government expenditure. A country can have modest military expenditure as a share of GDP but still face high opportunity costs if the overall budget is small, revenue is weak, or internal-security obligations compete with education, health, maintenance, and local services.
