15.1 Bamboo Diplomacy, ASEAN, and the Evolution of Vietnam’s Foreign Policy Principles

Vietnam’s foreign relations and security environment are defined by geography and historical vulnerability. China lies to the north, the South China Sea—referred to in Vietnam as the East Sea—to the east, the Mekong region to the west and south, and ASEAN provides the principal regional diplomatic setting. Vietnam’s foreign policy has therefore been shaped by a recurrent problem: how to preserve national independence while engaging external powers that are economically necessary, militarily significant, and politically constraining. The contemporary language of “bamboo diplomacy” should be understood against this longer historical background. It is not a sudden invention, but the latest formulation of a foreign-policy tradition that combines independence, flexibility, self-reliance, and pragmatic international integration.

Vietnam’s foreign-policy principles have changed across several historical phases. During the anti-colonial and revolutionary period, diplomacy was closely tied to national liberation, sovereignty, and international recognition. During the Cold War, Vietnam’s external relations were shaped by socialist solidarity, war, alliance politics, and dependence on the Soviet bloc. After reunification in 1975, Vietnam faced diplomatic isolation, conflict with Cambodia, confrontation with China, and dependence on Soviet support. In this context, foreign policy was heavily security-centered and ideologically framed.

The turning point came with Đổi Mới after 1986. Economic renovation required a corresponding diplomatic renovation. Vietnam could not build a market-oriented economy, attract foreign investment, normalize relations with former adversaries, and enter global production networks while maintaining a rigid Cold War diplomatic posture. The foreign-policy line therefore shifted toward independence, self-reliance, openness, diversification, multilateralization, and proactive international integration. Official Vietnamese descriptions of current foreign policy emphasize independence, sovereignty, openness, diversification and multilateralization of external relations, and the idea that Vietnam is willing to be a friend and reliable partner of all countries. ([Вьетнамын засгийн газрын портал][1])

ASEAN was central to this transformation. Vietnam joined ASEAN in 1995, a move that signaled its transition from postwar isolation to regional integration. ASEAN gave Vietnam a diplomatic platform through which it could normalize regional relations, widen its strategic options, and embed its development strategy in Southeast Asian cooperation. ASEAN also helped Vietnam manage asymmetry with larger powers. Through ASEAN-led mechanisms, Vietnam could address the South China Sea/East Sea issue, Mekong cooperation, trade integration, connectivity, and regional security without relying exclusively on bilateral confrontation or alliance dependence. For this reason, ASEAN is not a peripheral foreign-policy arena. It is the institutional environment within which Vietnam practices autonomy, regional legitimacy, and strategic balancing.

The concept of “bamboo diplomacy” gives a more recent name to this older strategy. Associated especially with General Secretary Nguyễn Phú Trọng, the metaphor presents Vietnamese diplomacy as having “strong roots, a firm trunk, and flexible branches.” Its core meaning is that Vietnam should be firm on principles but flexible in methods. The roots are national independence, sovereignty, socialist political continuity, and national interests. The trunk is internal political cohesion and strategic self-reliance. The branches are tactical flexibility, multilateral engagement, and diversified partnerships. Recent analyses note that this concept was formally articulated under Nguyễn Phú Trọng and became a central description of Vietnam’s foreign-policy style. ([Diplomacy.edu][2])

This approach is closely connected to Vietnam’s defense principle often summarized as the “four no’s.” The 2019 National Defence White Paper states that Vietnam does not participate in military alliances, does not side with one country against another, does not allow foreign countries to establish military bases or use Vietnamese territory against other countries, and does not use force or threaten the use of force in international relations. This principle does not mean passivity. Rather, it preserves room for defense modernization, maritime security cooperation, strategic partnerships, and international defense engagement while avoiding formal alliance dependence. ([en.baochinhphu.vn][3])

Vietnam’s bamboo diplomacy is therefore best understood as strategic hedging rather than neutrality in a passive sense. Vietnam deepens relations with multiple major powers, including China, the United States, Japan, South Korea, India, Russia, Australia, and the European Union. It seeks access to markets, technology, investment, defense cooperation, infrastructure finance, energy partnerships, and diplomatic support. At the same time, it avoids binding itself too closely to any single power bloc. This is particularly important because China is simultaneously Vietnam’s largest structural security concern, a major trading partner, a source of manufacturing inputs, and an unavoidable neighboring power. The United States, Japan, Korea, India, Australia, and the EU widen Vietnam’s policy space, but they do not remove the geographic reality of China.

ASEAN remains essential because it converts Vietnam’s balancing strategy into a regional diplomatic framework. ASEAN centrality allows Vietnam to link national concerns to collective regional principles: peaceful settlement of disputes, respect for international law, freedom of navigation, regional connectivity, supply-chain resilience, and inclusive economic integration. Vietnam’s recent official statements continue to emphasize ASEAN’s central role in the regional architecture and the importance of working with ASEAN to uphold international law, strengthen peace and stability, expand economic linkages, and deepen relations with partners. ([Vietnam+ (VietnamPlus)][4])

This foreign-policy evolution has direct administrative implications. Diplomacy is not separate from domestic governance. Strategic balancing affects defense planning, industrial policy, port and logistics investment, energy security, cybersecurity, digital infrastructure, education, trade administration, border management, and provincial development. For example, supply-chain diversification requires customs modernization, industrial-zone governance, investment screening, standards compliance, labor training, and infrastructure coordination. Maritime disputes require coast guard capacity, legal expertise, fisheries management, port development, and diplomatic communication. Mekong water security requires data systems, interministerial coordination, environmental monitoring, and regional negotiation capacity. Foreign policy thus becomes an administrative capability, not confined to diplomatic doctrine.

The Mekong, East Sea, and great-power competition create a complex risk environment. The Mekong connects Vietnam’s agricultural security, water governance, energy decisions, and relations with upstream countries. The East Sea connects sovereignty, maritime resources, fisheries, shipping lanes, offshore energy, and international law. Great-power competition affects technology standards, semiconductor supply chains, cyber governance, defense procurement, rare earths, energy infrastructure, and trade agreements. A professional country report should therefore integrate military expenditure, political stability, trade dependence, water security, border management, maritime governance, and regional cooperation rather than treating security as only defense spending.

Vietnam’s foreign policy is therefore not confined to external relations. It is part of the country’s broader state-building strategy: maintaining political autonomy, sustaining economic integration, managing dependence on China, using ASEAN as a regional platform, and expanding partnerships without crossing the threshold into formal alignment.

Policy Implications

Vietnam’s diplomatic doctrine has evolved from ideological alignment toward pragmatic, multi-vector integration. However, this does not mean that Vietnam has abandoned socialist political continuity or national sovereignty as organizing principles. The central policy challenge is to maintain strategic autonomy while deepening economic integration and modernizing the state apparatus. ASEAN provides the regional institutional platform for this strategy, while bamboo diplomacy provides the tactical logic. The success of this approach depends not only on diplomatic skill, but also on administrative capacity: the ability to coordinate trade, infrastructure, defense, technology, water governance, and legal diplomacy across ministries and provinces.
