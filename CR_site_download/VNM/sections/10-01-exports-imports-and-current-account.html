10.1 Export-Import Trends, Trade Dependence, and Export Transformation

ASEAN comparative lens (2026). Vietnam should be read in ASEAN comparison as ASEAN's most prominent party-led, export-manufacturing upgrading case. Unlike the Philippines' service-remittance profile, Thailand's older industrial-tourism base, Malaysia's resource-industrial federal structure, or Indonesia's archipelagic domestic-market scale, Vietnam's comparative issue is how a socialist party-state manages very open global production networks. Viet Nam's external economy should first be understood through trade flows. FDI is crucial, but it is treated more directly in section 10.2. This section focuses on three questions: how fast exports and imports have grown, how dependent the economy has become on trade, and how the export basket has changed from primary commodities toward manufacturing and technology-intensive goods.

The basic trend is striking. In 1995, merchandise exports were about USD 5.45 billion and merchandise imports about USD 8.16 billion. By 2010, exports had reached USD 72.24 billion and imports USD 84.84 billion. By 2024, World Bank WDI data put merchandise exports at USD 404.82 billion and imports at USD 380.52 billion. National Statistics Office figures for 2025 then show goods exports of USD 475.04 billion and goods imports of USD 455.01 billion. In thirty years, Viet Nam moved from a small, reforming economy with modest external trade into one of Asia's major export-manufacturing platforms.

The export-import trend shows two important features. First, exports and imports rise together. This is not accidental. Viet Nam's manufacturing model imports machinery, components, intermediate goods, raw materials, and energy inputs, then exports assembled, processed, or manufactured goods. A large export sector therefore requires a large import sector. This is why a simple reading of export growth can mislead: fast export growth is a sign of competitiveness, but it also signals dependence on imported inputs, foreign-invested supply chains, and external logistics.

Second, the trade balance has improved but remains cyclical. Viet Nam ran merchandise trade deficits in many early industrialization years, especially when investment and imported inputs grew faster than exports. Since the 2010s, the balance has more often been positive. In 2024, the merchandise trade surplus was about USD 24.31 billion in WDI data; in 2025, NSO goods figures imply a surplus of about USD 20.03 billion. The surplus supports foreign-exchange confidence and macroeconomic stability, but it should not be treated as proof of deep domestic value capture. A surplus can coexist with a production model in which foreign firms, imported components, and assembly activity account for a large share of gross export value.

10.1.1 Trade Dependence and Macroeconomic Meaning

Trade dependence is the defining macroeconomic feature of Viet Nam's external sector. WDI's trade-to-GDP ratio, which includes goods and services exports plus imports, rose from 74.7 percent of GDP in 1995 to 111.4 percent in 2000, 114.0 percent in 2010, 163.2 percent in 2020, and 173.9 percent in 2024. A ratio above 170 percent means that trade is not a side sector. It is part of the economy's operating system.

High trade dependence has four policy meanings. First, growth is highly sensitive to external demand. A slowdown in electronics, apparel, furniture, machinery, or U.S. and European consumer markets can quickly affect factories, employment, logistics, tax revenue, and provincial growth. Second, macroeconomic stability depends on export performance and import management. Exchange-rate policy, reserves, inflation, and credit conditions cannot be separated from trade cycles. Third, infrastructure becomes macroeconomic policy. Ports, customs, expressways, inland waterways, industrial-zone roads, power supply, and digital customs systems directly shape export competitiveness. Fourth, trade diplomacy becomes development policy. Market access, rules of origin, tariff risk, standards, sanctions, and supply-chain politics affect domestic growth.

The dependence is productive but risky. It has allowed Viet Nam to grow faster than a more inward-looking model would have allowed. It has also made the country vulnerable to global demand shocks, tariff pressure, supply-chain relocation, imported-input shortages, shipping costs, and geopolitical tension between major partners. The policy problem is therefore not whether Viet Nam should trade less. The problem is how to make high trade dependence more resilient and more valuable domestically.

10.1.2 Export Transformation: Rise of Manufacturing and Decline of Primary Commodities

The deeper story is the transformation of the export basket. In the late 1990s, Viet Nam's exports still depended heavily on food, agricultural raw materials, fuels, and other primary commodities. WDI export-composition data show that in 1997 manufactures accounted for 44.0 percent of merchandise exports, while primary commodities, measured here as food, agricultural raw materials, fuels, ores, and metals, accounted for 51.8 percent. By 2023, manufactures had risen to 85.0 percent of merchandise exports, while those primary commodity categories had fallen to 13.3 percent.

This shift shows that Viet Nam's trade expansion goes beyond selling more of the same products. The economy moved from a primary-commodity and labor-intensive export base toward a manufacturing export system. Food exports remained important, but their relative share fell from roughly 30.2 percent of merchandise exports in 1997 to 9.4 percent in 2023. Fuel exports fell even more sharply, from 18.0 percent in 1997 and 26.4 percent in 2000 to less than 1 percent by 2023. Agricultural raw materials and ores/metals stayed comparatively small. The decline of primary commodity shares does not mean agriculture, energy, or raw materials became unimportant to society. It means the export engine shifted toward manufactured goods.

The manufacturing rise has several layers. The first layer was labor-intensive processing and light manufacturing: garments, footwear, furniture, seafood processing, and consumer goods. The second layer was export-platform manufacturing linked to foreign investment, industrial zones, and global buyers. The third layer was electronics, phones, computers, components, machinery, and higher-technology manufacturing. WDI's high-technology export indicator captures this shift inside manufacturing: high-technology exports rose to 44.3 percent of manufactured exports by 2023. This does not mean Viet Nam owns all the technology embodied in those goods. It means that goods exported from Viet Nam increasingly come from technology-intensive manufacturing chains.

The distinction matters. Export sophistication can rise faster than domestic technological capability. A smartphone, computer component, or electronic device exported from Viet Nam may contain imported parts, foreign intellectual property, multinational management, and external design. The domestic value may come from labor, land, utilities, logistics, tax revenue, local services, and some supplier activity. That is still economically valuable, but it is not the same as deep domestic innovation. The next step is to move from exporting sophisticated products toward producing more of the sophisticated inputs, engineering, design, testing, logistics, and business services behind those products.

10.1.3 Import Structure and Imported-Input Dependence

Imports are more than a leakage from the economy. They are part of the production system. The rise of imports reflects industrialization, capital formation, intermediate input demand, machinery imports, consumer-market expansion, and energy needs. In an export-platform economy, imports can rise when the export sector is healthy because firms need components and materials before shipping final goods abroad.

This creates an interpretation problem. If imports grow quickly, one reading is vulnerability: domestic firms may not produce enough inputs, and the economy may depend on foreign suppliers. Another reading is productive integration: imports are being transformed into exports through factories, logistics, and labor. Both are true for Viet Nam. The development question is whether each dollar of imports is associated with rising domestic value added over time. If domestic suppliers, engineering services, packaging firms, logistics providers, testing labs, and component producers upgrade, then imported inputs can become a bridge to capability. If not, Viet Nam remains an efficient assembly platform with limited domestic spillovers.

10.1.4 What the Trend Means for State Capacity

The trade data point to a state-capacity agenda. Customs administration, standards enforcement, trade facilitation, port governance, tax administration, transport logistics, industrial-zone management, electricity reliability, environmental inspection, and trade diplomacy are all part of macroeconomic management. A highly trade-dependent economy cannot treat these as separate technical sectors. When customs is slow, ports congested, roads unreliable, electricity unstable, or standards unclear, the effect appears in exports, imports, investment decisions, and growth.

The 2025 administrative reorganization also matters here. Larger provincial-level units may help coordinate industrial corridors, ports, logistics networks, border-gate trade, and supplier ecosystems. But larger units can also create transition costs for firms if licensing, land records, tax registration, customs coordination, and local investment services are disrupted. Trade competitiveness therefore depends not only on national strategy but also on predictable provincial implementation.

10.1.5 Interpretation

The evidence supports four conclusions. First, Viet Nam's growth model is deeply trade-dependent. Trade openness near 174 percent of GDP in 2024 means that external integration shapes domestic production, employment, fiscal capacity, and macro stability. Second, exports and imports must be interpreted together. Imports should be read as the input side of the export model, not as weakness alone. Third, the export basket has transformed dramatically from primary commodities toward manufactures. This is one of Viet Nam's clearest structural achievements. Fourth, the next challenge is domestic value capture. Viet Nam now needs to increase the share of domestic firms, domestic inputs, domestic technology, and high-value services inside the export system.

This sets up the role of FDI in the next section. FDI did more than add capital; it helped build the export-manufacturing system that made the transformation possible. But FDI also makes the domestic value question sharper. The policy task is to keep the benefits of openness while moving from gross export expansion toward deeper capability, stronger local suppliers, and more resilient trade dependence.
