13.1 Education System, Skills Policy, and Public Spending

ASEAN comparative lens (2026). Vietnam should be read in ASEAN comparison as ASEAN's most prominent party-led, export-manufacturing upgrading case. Unlike the Philippines' service-remittance profile, Thailand's older industrial-tourism base, Malaysia's resource-industrial federal structure, or Indonesia's archipelagic domestic-market scale, Vietnam's comparative issue is how a socialist party-state manages very open global production networks. Education is one of Vietnam's clearest development achievements, but it is also becoming one of the main constraints on the next stage of industrial upgrading. The basic education story is strong: school participation is broad, literacy is high, and international assessments have often shown performance above what Vietnam's income level would predict. In 2025 the school system opened the 2025-2026 school year with about 26 million pupils and students, 1.6 million teachers, and roughly 52,000 educational institutions connected to the national ceremony. The 2025 high-school graduation examination had more than 1.1 million registered candidates, and the national graduation rate reached 99.25 percent (National Statistics Office of Viet Nam, 2026). These figures show that Vietnam has built a mass education state.

Yet the educational agenda has changed. Vietnam no longer needs only to expand schooling. It must convert schooling into advanced skills, technological capability, and inclusive human capital. This is why education belongs in an administrative analysis rather than only a social-sector chapter. The education system determines whether Vietnam can move from labor-intensive manufacturing to higher-value production, whether domestic firms can absorb technology from foreign-invested enterprises, whether provincial labor markets can support industrial parks, and whether ethnic minority, rural, and migrant children can participate in national development.

!Education, skills, and industrial-upgrading bottlenecks

The first issue is the difference between access and quality. Secondary enrollment is high, and tertiary enrollment has expanded over the long run, but the expansion is uneven and institutionally demanding. Vietnam's challenge extends beyond placing more young people in classrooms. It is to ensure that graduates have analytical skills, digital literacy, engineering competence, communication ability, foreign-language capacity, and practical problem-solving experience. A mass school system can produce enrollment success while still leaving employers dissatisfied with job readiness.

The second issue is the weakness of technical and vocational pathways. OECD's 2025 economic survey treats this as a productivity bottleneck: only a small share of students pursue technical and vocational education and training, collaboration between vocational institutions and employers remains limited, and completion rates are low. This matters because Vietnam's economy depends on manufacturing, logistics, construction, electronics, machinery, services, and industrial-zone employment. These sectors need technicians, maintenance workers, quality-control staff, production supervisors, automation operators, logistics coordinators, and applied engineers. A university-oriented status hierarchy can therefore produce a mismatch: families prefer academic degrees, while firms need practical technical skills.

Vocational education is also a governance problem. The 2025 institutional transfer of vocational education functions into the Ministry of Education and Training, and the 2025 discussion of an amended Law on Vocational Education, signal an attempt to build a more coherent national system. The policy direction is clear: open and flexible pathways, stronger student streaming after lower and upper secondary school, lifelong learning, and closer links between vocational schools and labor-market demand. The harder task is implementation. Effective TVET requires local employer participation in curricula, apprenticeships, credible certification, equipment investment, teacher retraining, and reliable data on graduate employment. Without those mechanisms, vocational reform remains a formal institutional change rather than a capability change.

The third issue is STEM and high-technology human resources. Vietnam's growth model increasingly depends on electronics, semiconductors, AI, digital services, automation, biotechnology, data infrastructure, and advanced manufacturing. The Ministry of Education and Training's 2025 high-technology human-resource agenda for 2025-2035 asks higher-education institutions to renew STEM curricula, strengthen practical capability, and align training with fields such as digital technology, artificial intelligence, and biotechnology. This is a strategic shift. It recognizes that Vietnam's earlier comparative advantage in disciplined low-cost labor cannot by itself sustain high-income ambitions.

But STEM expansion requires more than announcing new majors. It requires laboratories, faculty capacity, research funding, industry internships, English-language technical materials, data and computing infrastructure, intellectual-property management, and university-industry collaboration. It also requires careful quality assurance. If STEM enrollment expands faster than teaching and laboratory capacity, the system may produce credential inflation rather than technical competence. For a country trying to deepen domestic value added in global supply chains, the distinction is decisive: high-tech production located in Vietnam is not the same as high-tech capability owned by Vietnamese workers and firms.

The fourth issue is higher-education quality and financing. OECD notes that public spending on tertiary education is low relative to Vietnam's ambitions, while public universities rely heavily on tuition revenue. This creates two risks. First, financially stronger households can access better institutions, English programs, and urban labor-market networks, while poorer students may face debt or choose lower-cost, lower-quality options. Second, universities may expand fee-paying programs faster than they upgrade faculty, research, and industry partnerships. In administrative terms, Vietnam needs a higher-education financing model that combines autonomy with accountability, targeted scholarships, quality assurance, and incentives for institutions to serve strategic sectors rather than only high-demand credential markets.

The fifth issue is regional inequality. National education indicators conceal large territorial differences. Mountainous areas, ethnic minority communities, remote border provinces, disaster-prone regions, and migrant worker settlements face different barriers from Hanoi or Ho Chi Minh City. Children may face language barriers, long travel distance, lower household income, weaker digital connectivity, poorer school infrastructure, nutritional disadvantages, and limited access to tutoring or career guidance. For migrant children, residence registration, mobility, and parental working hours can complicate access to stable schooling. Education policy therefore intersects with local government finance, social protection, nutrition, transport, housing, and digital infrastructure.

The sixth issue is industrial labor mismatch. Chapters 10 and 12 show that Vietnam has become deeply integrated into export manufacturing and FDI-led supply chains. The labor-market implication is demanding: firms need workers who can operate in formal production systems, meet quality and compliance standards, use digital tools, and adapt to automation. Yet many workers still enter employment with limited certified training. NSO reported that workers with degrees or certificates accounted for only 29.2 percent in 2025. That figure is not a minor labor statistic. It is a measure of the distance between Vietnam's industrial ambition and its current skills base.

The policy response should therefore be organized around a skills ecosystem rather than a school-sector mindset. First, general education must preserve strong basic learning while improving creativity, problem solving, digital competence, and career orientation. Second, TVET must become a credible pathway, not a residual track for students who do not enter universities. Third, universities must be pushed toward quality, research relevance, and industry partnership. Fourth, scholarships and student loans should protect equity as tuition-based financing expands. Fifth, provincial governments should coordinate industrial-zone planning with local colleges, vocational schools, housing, childcare, and transport. Sixth, labor-market information systems should track graduate outcomes, employer demand, and skill shortages by region and sector.

Vietnam's education system is therefore both a success and a constraint. It has delivered mass schooling and social mobility, but the next stage requires a more selective, better-financed, and more performance-oriented system. The administrative challenge is to move from enrollment expansion to capability formation: better skills rather than student numbers alone; stronger institutions rather than university counts alone; measurable technical competence rather than STEM rhetoric; and equitable access rather than national averages for rural, minority, migrant, and low-income students.

Policy Implications

Vietnam should treat education as industrial policy, social policy, and territorial policy at the same time. The main reform priority is alignment: school curricula, TVET, universities, employers, industrial parks, and provincial development plans must be connected through real incentives. Public spending should prioritize quality, equity, and strategic skills rather than only institutional expansion. TVET reform should include workplace-based training and employer participation. STEM expansion should be tied to laboratories, faculty development, scholarships, and industry research. Higher-education autonomy should be matched with transparent quality assurance and graduate-employment accountability. Without these reforms, Vietnam may continue to produce strong basic education outcomes while still facing a shortage of the skills needed for productivity growth.
