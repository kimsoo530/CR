15.3 Defense, Police, Border, and Transnational Risks

Vietnam’s security environment is shaped by the interaction of maritime disputes, land borders, transnational crime, cyber threats, and disaster risks. Security cannot therefore be understood only through military expenditure or formal defense doctrine. Vietnam faces a broader problem of security administration: how to protect sovereignty, maintain domestic order, manage borders, respond to non-traditional risks, and preserve strategic autonomy in a contested regional environment. China to the north, the East Sea to the east, Laos and Cambodia to the west and southwest, and ASEAN as the principal regional framework together define the spatial setting of Vietnamese security policy.

The most sensitive external security issue is the East Sea. For Vietnam, the East Sea is not only a maritime boundary question. It is connected to sovereignty, fisheries, offshore energy, shipping lanes, submarine cables, marine scientific research, coast-guard operations, and national identity. China’s growing maritime presence, including coast-guard patrols, maritime militia activity, artificial-island infrastructure, research vessels, and administrative assertion over disputed areas, creates a persistent grey-zone challenge. This challenge is difficult because it often remains below the threshold of conventional war while still altering facts at sea. Vietnam must therefore rely not only on the navy, but also on coast-guard capacity, maritime domain awareness, legal documentation, diplomatic communication, fisheries administration, and coordination with coastal provinces.

Recent South China Sea developments reinforce this point. In April and May 2026, China conducted naval and air patrols near Scarborough Shoal while the United States, the Philippines, Japan, Australia, and other partners carried out large-scale Balikatan exercises near the South China Sea and the Taiwan Strait. Although these incidents directly involved the Philippines rather than Vietnam, they matter for Vietnam because they signal a more militarized regional maritime environment and a greater risk that localized maritime incidents may become connected to broader U.S.–China competition. ([Reuters][1]) Japan’s participation in live-fire maritime drills with the United States and partners in the northern Philippines further indicates that middle powers are becoming more active in regional maritime security. ([Reuters][2])

Vietnam’s defense policy responds to these pressures through cautious modernization and strategic non-alignment. The 2019 National Defence White Paper articulated the “four no’s”: no military alliances, no siding with one country against another, no foreign military bases or use of Vietnamese territory against other countries, and no use or threat of force in international relations. ([en.baochinhphu.vn][3]) This principle does not mean that Vietnam is passive. Rather, it allows Vietnam to modernize defense capabilities, expand maritime security cooperation, participate in peacekeeping, and deepen partnerships with multiple countries while avoiding formal alliance dependence. In practical terms, Vietnam’s defense posture combines deterrence, restraint, and diplomatic flexibility.

The police and internal security apparatus are equally important because many of Vietnam’s risks are non-traditional. Cybercrime, online fraud, trafficking in persons, narcotics, money laundering, environmental crime, and illicit cross-border movement connect domestic order with regional networks. UNODC’s Vietnam programming highlights cooperation on drug trafficking, money laundering, corruption, terrorism prevention, border management, human trafficking, and migrant smuggling. ([НҮБ: Эрүүл мэнд, мансууруулах бодис][4]) These risks are not peripheral to national security. They affect financial integrity, labor migration, border communities, social stability, and trust in public institutions.

Border management is especially complex because Vietnam’s land borders are economically and politically diverse. The northern border with China is central to trade, manufacturing inputs, tourism, migration control, and illicit trafficking risks. The western border with Laos is linked to mountainous terrain, ethnic minority areas, narcotics routes, forest governance, and infrastructure connectivity. The southwestern border with Cambodia is connected to labor mobility, trade, migration, trafficking, and Mekong Delta security. Effective border governance therefore requires customs capacity, immigration control, police cooperation, local administration, disease surveillance, transport infrastructure, and anti-corruption safeguards. Border security cannot be reduced to blocking movement; it is about distinguishing legal circulation from illicit networks.

The Mekong region adds another layer of security risk. Water security, upstream dam construction, drought, saltwater intrusion, fisheries decline, agricultural vulnerability, and climate change directly affect Vietnam’s Mekong Delta. These are not conventional military threats, but they can destabilize livelihoods, food security, rural incomes, and internal migration. For this reason, Mekong security requires environmental monitoring, hydrological data, regional negotiation, disaster response, agricultural adaptation, and coordination among central ministries and local governments. It also links Vietnam’s relations with China, Laos, Cambodia, Thailand, and ASEAN-led mechanisms.

Cybersecurity has become a further bridge between external security and domestic administration. Vietnam’s digital transformation, e-government programs, banking digitalization, logistics platforms, industrial upgrading, and public-service modernization increase the state’s dependence on secure data systems. Cyber risks include state-linked intrusion, ransomware, online scams, data theft, misinformation, and vulnerabilities in critical infrastructure. The administrative challenge is therefore to develop cybersecurity capacity without undermining innovation, digital commerce, and public trust. Cybersecurity also intersects with police authority, data governance, censorship, platform regulation, and national-security law.

These security issues have important institutional consequences. Vietnam’s Ministry of National Defence, Ministry of Public Security, Ministry of Foreign Affairs, coast guard, border guard, customs agencies, provincial governments, courts, procuracies, and specialized regulators must operate across overlapping jurisdictions. Maritime disputes require coordination between defense, diplomacy, energy, fisheries, transport, and local authorities. Transnational crime requires coordination among police, border guards, customs, prosecutors, courts, financial-intelligence bodies, and international partners. Disaster and climate risks require coordination among agriculture, environment, transport, health, provincial authorities, and local communities. Security governance is therefore a test of interministerial coordination.

The relationship between security and development is particularly important. Defense modernization affects shipbuilding, ports, surveillance, communications, and dual-use infrastructure. Border management affects trade facilitation and regional connectivity. Police capacity affects investment confidence, anti-corruption credibility, and financial integrity. Cybersecurity affects digital transformation and high-tech industrial policy. Disaster response affects agricultural resilience and local service delivery. In this sense, Vietnam’s security institutions support both sovereignty and administrative resilience. Their performance determines whether external pressure becomes a manageable risk or a constraint on development.

At the same time, the expansion of security functions creates governance risks. A stronger security state can improve border control, anti-trafficking efforts, cyber protection, and maritime resilience. However, it can also increase administrative discretion, limit transparency, and expand surveillance powers. The policy issue is therefore not a matter of whether Vietnam should strengthen security institutions. It is how security capacity can be professionalized, legally bounded, accountable, and coordinated with civilian administration.

The visual evidence below should therefore be read as part of a broader security-administration diagnosis. Military expenditure, maritime incidents, border trade, cybercrime exposure, trafficking routes, disaster vulnerability, and water-security indicators all show how external and transnational pressures enter domestic governance. A professional country report should not treat security as a separate defense chapter. It should show how defense, police, border, maritime, cyber, environmental, and diplomatic functions together shape Vietnam’s state capacity.

Policy Implications

Vietnam’s next-stage security challenge is not limited to defending territory. It is to build an integrated security-administration system capable of managing grey-zone maritime pressure, land-border risks, transnational crime, cyber threats, and climate-related instability. This requires stronger coast-guard and maritime-domain awareness, more professional border management, better police and customs coordination, credible legal procedures, regional intelligence sharing, and stronger cyber and disaster-response capacity. At the same time, Vietnam must preserve strategic flexibility by avoiding excessive dependence on any one partner. In this respect, defense and police reform reach beyond the security sector. They are part of Vietnam’s broader effort to maintain sovereignty, support economic integration, protect citizens, and manage the risks of a more contested regional order.
