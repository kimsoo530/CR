5.1 Central Government and Core Coordinating Institutions

Nigeria's federal centre is not a single command post. It is a set of partly overlapping coordination circuits that convert presidential priorities into cabinet decisions, administrative instructions, plans, budgets, cash releases, ministerial action and, sometimes, state-level implementation. Comparative public-sector research similarly treats coordination as a problem of linking decisions, resources and implementation across organizational boundaries rather than simply creating a central unit (World Bank, 2018). The central problem is therefore not the absence of coordinating bodies. It is whether political direction, civil-service management, planning, finance and performance monitoring are sufficiently coupled that one institution can detect where a commitment has stalled and another has both the authority and resources to correct it.

The President and Federal Executive Council (FEC) provide political direction. Ministers translate that direction into sector proposals and collectively consider major executive decisions, while the Office of the Secretary to the Government of the Federation (OSGF) supplies cabinet secretariat, institutional memory and follow-up. The OSGF defines its mandate as policy formulation, harmonization, implementation monitoring and coordination across institutions of government (Office of the Secretary to the Government of the Federation, n.d.). Its value lies less in delivering a sectoral service than in maintaining the record that connects an approved decision to a responsible ministry, deadline and subsequent report. A cabinet decision without an assigned owner or follow-up record may be politically authoritative yet administratively inert.

The Office of the Head of the Civil Service of the Federation (OHCSF) operates a different circuit. The 1999 Constitution's section 169 establishes the federal civil service, and section 171 places appointment of its Head within presidential authority. In organizational terms, ministers carry political responsibility while permanent secretaries and career officials sustain ministries across cabinet changes. The OHCSF's Performance Management System seeks to align national plans, medium-term sector strategies, annual appropriations, MDA work plans and individual objectives (Office of the Head of the Civil Service of the Federation, 2022). This creates a potentially important chain from national priority to departmental and employee performance. The policy document establishes the intended design, however; it does not demonstrate that targets are consistently measured or that appraisal results change staffing, resources or implementation.

Planning, budgeting and treasury control constitute the third circuit. The Federal Ministry of Budget and Economic Planning is responsible for national plans, alignment of sector and subnational plans, plan ceilings and monitoring, while the Budget Office develops fiscal assumptions and annual expenditure frameworks. The Office of the Accountant-General of the Federation manages federal receipts and payments, financial statements and treasury controls (Federal Ministry of Budget and Economic Planning, n.d.; Office of the Accountant-General of the Federation, n.d.). These functions impose a practical test on political priorities: a commitment becomes executable only when it can be linked to a costed programme, appropriation, release, procurement process, payment and responsible accounting officer.

This map is more accurate than a simple hierarchy because the circuits constrain one another without being identical. The planning ministry may align a programme with the national development plan but cannot by itself guarantee cash release. OHCSF may embed the priority in performance agreements but cannot resolve a procurement dispute. OSGF may record and transmit an FEC decision but does not substitute for line-ministry expertise. Where states have constitutional or operational responsibility, a federal directive may require negotiation, co-financing and comparable reporting rather than command.

The CRDCU adds a presidential monitoring and escalation circuit to this conventional machinery. Following the November 2023 cabinet retreat, ministers and permanent secretaries signed performance bonds and the President directed reactivation of the Presidential Delivery Tracker around eight priority areas (State House, 2023). The current CRDCU model uses ministerial deliverables, quarterly assessments and citizen-facing feedback. Its plausible advantage is to shorten the information gap between the Presidency and implementing MDAs: a named target, periodic evidence and escalation can focus political attention on a blocked decision. Yet the presence of a tracker does not show that the underlying programme was funded, procured, completed or effective. Delivery monitoring can complement cabinet, budget and civil-service systems; it can also become a parallel reporting layer that improves visibility without removing the bottleneck.

Fiscal evidence illustrates the distinction. Khalid's analysis of Nigeria's official budget-implementation reports for 2011–2023 finds large forecast errors and systematic optimism in capital expenditure: capital-expenditure forecast errors averaged more than 70 percent of actual executed values, while budgeted capital spending exceeded outturn in all but one year examined (Khalid, 2025). This is not a general “implementation rate,” and it should not be attributed to one coordinating institution. It nevertheless shows why appropriation and a positive delivery report are insufficient evidence of executable policy. Revenue shortfalls, project preparation, cash management and MDA capacity can break the chain after political approval.

A 2018 study of 4,700 federal projects adds a second caution. Rasul and Rogger found that greater managerial autonomy was positively associated with project completion, whereas management practices emphasizing performance incentives and monitoring were negatively associated with completion in the Nigerian organizations studied (Rasul & Rogger, 2018). The study is observational and based on an earlier administrative period, so it does not establish the effect of the current CRDCU or OHCSF system. Its implication is narrower but important: more central monitoring is not automatically better management. Coordination should clarify objectives, ensure resources, resolve cross-agency obstacles and preserve appropriate operational discretion rather than multiply indicators or induce defensive reporting.

The two competing interpretations can now be stated precisely. One sees layered coordination as adaptive specialization: cabinet, civil service, budget institutions and the delivery unit contribute distinct information and control. The other sees coordination substitution: new presidential units are created because ordinary institutions do not reliably connect decisions to execution, thereby adding another circuit without repairing the old ones. Publicly available institutional descriptions cannot decide between these accounts. The relevant evidence is end-to-end traceability for the same deliverable.

Nigeria could make that test possible by assigning a common identifier to every priority deliverable and carrying it through the FEC or OSGF decision record, MDA work plan, MTEF or annual budget line, release, procurement record, payment, physical output and outcome indicator. Quarterly reporting should record the reason for variance—finance, procurement, staffing, legal authority, intergovernmental coordination, contractor performance, data or policy change—and the institution responsible for correction. The key measure would not be the number of meetings or reports, but the share of deliverables that can be followed from authorization to a verified result, together with the time spent at each stage. This turns central coordination from an organizational chart into an auditable implementation chain and sets up the next question: whether Nigeria's repeated reorganizations and reform programmes have reduced mandate overlap or merely rearranged it.

5.2 Organizational Management and Bureaucratic Reform

Nigeria's recurring administrative-reform problem is not a shortage of diagnoses. Since the 1970s, commissions and governments have repeatedly recommended new ministry structures, clearer mandates, professional management, lower overhead and stronger service orientation. The harder task has been to convert a restructuring decision into changed law, appropriations, establishments, assets, records, reporting relationships and operating routines. Organizational reform is therefore better understood as a transition process than as an announcement that an agency has been merged or abolished.

The reform sequence reveals both institutional learning and repeated reversal.

The Udoji Commission illustrates selective implementation. Constituted in September 1972, it was instructed to examine organization, management, recruitment, employment conditions and staff development and reported in 1974 (Federal Republic of Nigeria, 1974). Its grading and pay architecture left a visible institutional legacy, but the broader ambition to install performance-oriented management depended on sustained changes in supervisory practice. This distinction matters: a salary and grade decision can be incorporated into a schedule and payroll, whereas a management reform must be reproduced through appointments, delegation, appraisal, training and corrective action across many organizations.

The 1988 reorganization went further in changing formal authority. It imposed a more uniform structure, expanded internal-audit powers, required progress reporting and sought to align responsibility more clearly with senior authority. Yet it also strengthened ministers relative to the career hierarchy and altered the position of senior administrators. Dlakwa's contemporary assessment concluded that many provisions restated earlier prescriptions whose failure arose from poor implementation rather than defective wording (Dlakwa, 1992). The episode shows why centralization and administrative capacity are not synonymous. Concentrating authority may make commands clearer while weakening continuity, professional challenge or horizontal coordination; subsequent restoration of career permanent-secretary arrangements did not by itself settle those trade-offs.

Post-1999 reforms demonstrate why changes linked to enforceable administrative systems can be more consequential than changes to an organizational chart. The World Bank's evaluation of the 1998–2007 country programme reported that civil-service reform had severed 35,700 officials and removed an estimated 8,000 ghost workers from the payroll. These are personnel-control results, not measures of service quality, and the evaluation also found that reform outcomes remained fragile and progress outside the federal level was limited (World Bank, 2008). Their analytical value lies in the mechanism: personnel verification connected an individual record to payroll and a fiscal consequence. By contrast, an overlapping mandate can survive a formal merger if two budget heads, management teams, statutory functions or information systems continue in practice. Nigeria's experience with New Public Management similarly suggests that politically attractive cost-reduction measures advanced more readily than the deeper work of institutionalization and operational change (Ikeanyibe, 2016).

The Oronsaye process makes this implementation gap unusually visible. The committee's 2012 report diagnosed duplicated and overlapping federal bodies, but a White Paper followed only in 2014. In July 2022, the federal government inaugurated another committee to review bodies created since 2014 and explicitly stated that non-implementation of earlier rationalization reports, together with creation of new agencies, had increased the cost of government (Federal Ministry of Information and National Orientation, 2022). This sequence—diagnosis, selective government response, limited transition, subsequent organizational growth and renewed review—suggests an equilibrium rather than a one-time technical error. Agencies possess enabling laws, appropriations, offices, boards, staff, contracts, sectoral clients and political sponsors. Those assets create concentrated constituencies for organizational survival, while the benefits of eliminating duplication are dispersed and often difficult to attribute.

Sectoral research supports this political-administrative interpretation. In a study of efforts to unbundle and reorganize the Nigerian petroleum bureaucracy, Ikeanyibe found that officials defended organizational jurisdictions and critical tasks, delaying or redirecting liberalization reforms (Ikeanyibe, 2018). The study concerns one sector and should not be generalized mechanically to every federal agency. It nevertheless identifies an observable mechanism that a generic claim about “lack of political will” misses: an organization can preserve itself by retaining a statutory task, control over information or a necessary approval even when its formal structure is scheduled to change.

The Federal Executive Council's February 2024 decision should consequently be treated as a new implementation attempt, not as evidence that rationalization has been completed. The official notice specified mergers, subsumptions and transfers, established an eight-member committee with a 12-week deadline, and acknowledged that some changes required legislative amendment (Federal Ministry of Information and National Orientation, 2024). That acknowledgement is decisive. An executive decision can reorganize ministerial supervision, but an agency established by legislation may require amendment or repeal; its appropriation must be migrated; staff and liabilities must be assigned; and its records and services must remain usable during the transition.

Nigeria's own merger guidance provides the appropriate test. The Bureau of Public Service Reforms warns that reducing ministries does not automatically improve efficiency and requires separate planning for legal authority, leadership, consultation, staffing, budgets, assets and liabilities, information systems, records and continuity of service (Bureau of Public Service Reforms, 2015). It also calls for baselines because a merger can temporarily disrupt the very service it is intended to improve. The absence of a consolidated public transition record in the official materials reviewed means that authorization should not be described as completed integration.

Future reorganizations could be made auditable through a transition register for every affected body. The register should identify the legal instrument and effective date; the old and successor mandate; abolished and transferred budget lines; authorized posts and actual staff dispositions; inventoried assets and verified liabilities; migrated contracts and data systems; service baselines; and the date at which the successor institution assumes operational and audit responsibility. Projected savings should be separated from realized recurrent savings and from one-off transition costs. The decisive outcome is not a lower agency count but removal of a duplicate decision right without loss of service, records or accountability.

This approach also clarifies the boundary with the next section. Machinery-of-government reform determines where a function and its resources reside. Whether the successor organization can recruit, deploy, develop and motivate personnel—and whether representation rules coexist with competence and performance—is the distinct human-resource question that follows.

5.3 Human Resource Management and Civil Service

Nigeria's civil-service problem cannot be diagnosed from a single claim that government has “too many” or “too few” employees. The prior question is who is being counted. The Constitution uses *civil service of the Federation* for staff serving the federal government in a civil capacity and distinguishes that core from the broader *public service*, which also encompasses commissions, statutory corporations, government-owned enterprises, universities, security institutions and other public bodies (Federal Republic of Nigeria, 1999). State civil services and local-government workforces add further employers outside a federal headcount. Consequently, a payroll enrolment, a core-civil-service nominal roll, a household-survey estimate and a whole-of-government employment total answer different questions.

The first administrative figure comes from a Bureau of Public Service Reforms account that said IPPIS helped reduce the federal payroll from more than 800,000 to about 720,000 (Bureau of Public Service Reforms, n.d.). The second and third come from the nationally representative 2023 corruption survey conducted by the National Bureau of Statistics with UNODC. Its report estimated that public-sector employees represented about 8 percent of the adult working population and, in a footnote discussing possible public-sector growth, reproduced IPPIS figures of roughly 700,000 employees in 2018 and more than 1 million in 2023 (National Bureau of Statistics & United Nations Office on Drugs and Crime, 2024). The report's survey share is not a census, while IPPIS is a payroll and personnel platform whose institutional coverage has changed. The difference between 720,000 and more than 1 million therefore should not be read as a measured increase of 280,000 in the same workforce.

This definitional discipline changes the substantive diagnosis. Nigeria may have payroll leakage, redundant posts, scarce technical skills, unfilled authorized positions and poorly distributed staff at the same time. A verified personnel database must therefore report not only the number paid, but the employing institution, legal establishment, cadre, grade, duty station, employment status, skills and actual post occupied. OHCSF's 2025 Personnel Audit and Skills Gap Analysis (PASGA) was explicitly launched to determine the workforce's true size, structure and skills composition and to build a verifiable personnel database extending beyond staff counts (Office of the Head of the Civil Service of the Federation, 2025a). This is evidence that the government recognizes the measurement problem; the announcement is not evidence that the audit is complete or that its results have corrected deployment.

Recruitment: merit, territorial inclusion and an auditable vacancy

Authority is distributed across institutions. The Federal Civil Service Commission (FCSC) has constitutional responsibility for appointment and disciplinary control in the federal civil service, while delegated powers allow ministries and extra-ministerial offices to conduct specified appointment, promotion and disciplinary actions (Federal Republic of Nigeria, 1999; Federal Civil Service Commission, n.d.). The Federal Character Commission (FCC) separately monitors equitable distribution of posts across states and zones. Its recruitment procedure requires an authorized staffing need, vacancies identified cadre by cadre, a job description and minimum qualifications, advertisement, monitoring and a certificate of compliance before appointment letters are released (Federal Character Commission, 2014).

Merit and federal character are therefore not formally designed as mutually exclusive rules. FCC guidance states that an applicant must first satisfy the minimum requirement and then competes for vacancies allocated to the relevant state or zone. The policy problem is whether competence and representation can both be observed rather than asserted after a selection. Recent scholarship finds that federal character can provide symbolic and distributive inclusion while its implementation remains contested, particularly when representation categories, compliance and substantive equity are not transparent (Demarest et al., 2020; Ikpebe et al., 2025). A defensible recruitment record would publish, for each cadre and grade, the authorized vacancy, job criteria, applicant and shortlist counts, selection method, state and gender distribution, FCC compliance status and appointment outcome. This protects territorial inclusion without treating minimum qualification as sufficient evidence of comparative merit.

Grade level is not the same as post, title or pay system

The federal career system uses Salary Grade Levels (SGL or GL) 01–17, but a grade level is only one coordinate in an officer's career. Current OHCSF circulars distinguish junior officers on GL 01–06, pooled officers on GL 07–14 and directorate-level officers on GL 15–17 (Office of the Head of the Civil Service of the Federation, 2026). The grouping is administratively useful, but it does not create one universal title-to-grade ladder across occupations.

The Public Service Rules make promotion competitive rather than automatic. Rule 020701 sets minimum time in post at two years for GL 06 and below, three years for GL 07–14 and four years for GL 15–17; it also requires assessment of performance, efficiency and potential, with seniority relevant when candidates have equal potential (Federal Republic of Nigeria, 2021). These are eligibility thresholds, not guaranteed promotion dates. Promotion also depends on a higher authorized vacancy, examination or assessment requirements, conduct records and approval by the competent authority. The general retirement rule is age 60 or 35 years of pensionable service, whichever occurs first, subject to specific exceptions created for some services. This creates predictable exits, but succession fails if vacancy forecasts, competence profiles and promotion pipelines are not connected.

From payroll control to capability and performance

IPPIS addresses an important but narrower question: whether an identified person is validly enrolled and paid. It does not by itself establish that the post is still required, the employee has the needed competence, the duty station has adequate staff, or work produces a service result. The existence of multiple salary structures also means that centralized payroll control must preserve legally valid sector differences without permitting untraceable exceptions. PASGA's skills portal illustrates the intended next layer: it links an assessment to an IPPIS identifier and collects ministry, department, cadre and skill information (Office of the Head of the Civil Service of the Federation, 2025b). Yet self-reported skill profiles require validation against qualifications, work products or assessed competence before they can drive deployment.

The 2022 Performance Management System (PMS) provides another layer by cascading national, MDA, departmental and individual objectives (Office of the Head of the Civil Service of the Federation, 2022). The risk is digitizing an appraisal form without changing managerial decisions. Nigerian project-level evidence has shown that management practices and operational autonomy can be associated with implementation outcomes in different directions; more intensive monitoring was not automatically linked to better completion in the organizations studied (Rasul & Rogger, 2018). Although that study predates the current PMS and cannot estimate its effects, it warns against measuring implementation by the number of signed performance agreements. A useful PMS should show whether a documented performance gap generated coaching, training, redeployment, process correction or a defensible career consequence.

The central reform implication is an interoperable but purpose-limited chain: authorized post → selected person → cadre and grade → verified competence → duty station → payroll → performance commitment → service output. OHCSF, FCSC, FCC, NSIWC, the Accountant-General and individual MDAs need not collapse into one institution, but their records require a common personnel and post identifier, version history and access controls. Three measures would make progress observable: the share of paid employees matched to an authorized post and current duty station; the share of priority posts whose required competencies are verified and whose gaps lead to funded action; and the time from a documented vacancy or performance gap to a recorded appointment, development, redeployment or exit decision. Representation should be reported separately by state or zone, gender, cadre and grade so that aggregate balance does not conceal concentration at junior or senior levels.

This approach avoids both indiscriminate downsizing and uncosted expansion. It permits Nigeria to ask where staff are missing, where payroll and establishment diverge, whether senior pipelines reproduce regional or gender imbalance, and whether training changes deployment or results. It also creates the personnel denominator required by the next section: financial administration can control a wage bill only when each payment is linked to a lawful post, an accountable institution and a verified employee.

5.4 Financial Administration, Procurement, Audit, and Inspection

Nigeria has constructed most of the formal components of a modern public financial management system: a medium-term fiscal framework, annual appropriations, centralized cash management, electronic transaction processing, procurement regulation, consolidated financial statements, external audit and legislative scrutiny. The central weakness is not the absence of control points. It is that the records produced at one point are not always timely, comparable or connected to the next. An appropriation can authorize a programme without guaranteeing cash; a cash release can occur before a procurement is completed; a payment can be recorded without showing physical delivery; and an audit finding can be published without a documented correction. Financial administration should therefore be judged by the continuity of the transaction and accountability chain, not by the fiscal deficit or the number of control institutions.

From an appropriation to a verifiable result

The Constitution requires public money to be spent under an appropriation or other lawful authorization and establishes the Auditor-General for the Federation as the external auditor of federal accounts (Federal Republic of Nigeria, 1999). The Fiscal Responsibility Act adds a medium-term expenditure framework and periodic budget-implementation reporting, while the Public Procurement Act requires procurement to be supported by an appropriation and available funds (Federal Republic of Nigeria, 2007a; Federal Republic of Nigeria, 2007b). These rules create a sequence in which political authorization, cash control, contracting, payment, reporting and review have distinct institutional owners.

The first major break occurs before expenditure. Using federal Budget Implementation Reports for 2011–2023, Khalid finds systematic optimism in Nigeria's revenue and capital-expenditure forecasts; budgeted capital expenditure exceeded outturn in all but one of the years examined, and the capital-expenditure forecast error averaged more than 70 percent of executed values (Khalid, 2025). This is not an “execution rate”: the study defines forecast error relative to actual outturn. It nevertheless shows that an approved capital envelope often overstates what the government can finance and deliver. Optimistic oil-production assumptions weaken revenue forecasts, after which cash rationing protects some recurrent obligations while capital projects absorb adjustment. The administrative consequence is not only lower spending. MDAs may delay tendering, contractors may face uncertain payment, and partially funded projects compete with new political commitments in later budgets.

The 2019 federal PEFA assessment identified the same mechanism from another angle. Aggregate expenditure reliability received a C, expenditure composition a D and revenue outturn a D; in-year budget reports received D+ because coverage was not fully comparable with the original budget, reports were issued late, and expenditure was captured at payment rather than commitment stage (PEFA Secretariat, 2019). These scores describe the federal system assessed at that time, not current performance or state governments. Their continuing relevance lies in the record design: if a report sees a transaction only when cash is paid, managers and legislators cannot observe obligations accumulating before payment.

Disclosure improved unevenly rather than linearly. Nigeria's Open Budget Survey transparency score rose from 16 in 2012 to 45 in 2021, then fell to 31 in 2023, below the 2023 global average of 45. Public participation scored 19 and combined legislative and audit oversight 61 in the 2023 assessment (International Budget Partnership, 2024). The index measures the public availability, timeliness and content of central-government budget documents through the end of 2022; it does not directly measure whether money was well spent. Its diagnosis is nonetheless concrete: in-year and mid-year reports were published too late, and the 2020 audit report was treated as internal use during the assessment period.

Procurement: legality before award, evidence after payment

The Public Procurement Act assigns the Bureau of Public Procurement (BPP) a regulatory role and places operational responsibility on each procuring entity's accounting officer. Section 16 requires an appropriation, available funds, competitive procedures and—above the applicable review threshold—a BPP Certificate of No Objection; section 20 makes the accounting officer responsible for procurement planning, tender organization, evaluation and execution (Federal Republic of Nigeria, 2007b). This allocation matters because BPP review should not displace ministerial responsibility. A certificate checks compliance at an approval point; it does not certify later contract performance.

Nigeria's digital architecture can, in principle, preserve the full contract record. The Nigeria Open Contracting Portal (NOCOPO) is designed to disclose planning, tender, award, contract and implementation stages, while the newer Procurement Compliance Monitoring Service (P-COMS) allows MDAs to submit plans, advertisements and requests for No Objection (Bureau of Public Procurement, n.d.; Bureau of Public Procurement, 2026). The existence of portals demonstrates administrative capability, not comprehensive use. BPP's public downloads page contains procurement plans for selected years and No Objection records through 2022, but the visible series are not sufficiently complete to calculate a defensible whole-of-government competition or disclosure rate. The appropriate next statistic is therefore a coverage denominator: the value and number of federal procurements recorded in GIFMIS, the share matched to a NOCOPO contract record, and the share with tender, award, beneficial-owner, milestone and payment fields complete.

This distinction between ex ante legality and ex post delivery also clarifies why procurement corruption is not solved by adding approvals. More approval layers can deter an irregular award, but they can also create queues, encourage threshold splitting or shift manipulation to contract variation and acceptance. The effective control is a common contract identifier carried from appropriation through tender, No Objection, award, variation, invoice, acceptance, payment and physical verification. Exceptions for direct, emergency or security procurement should retain a legal basis, approving authority, value, reason and later review even when sensitive details cannot be disclosed publicly.

Audit is consequential only when findings are closed

External audit reveals a different bottleneck. The OAuGF's 2024 SAI Performance Measurement Framework report states that the 2020 federal accounts were submitted to the Public Accounts Committees only in November and December 2023. The 2021 consolidated financial statements were submitted to Parliament on 31 July 2024, more than three years after the financial year ended; as of August 2024 the 2022 and 2023 statements had not been submitted by the Accountant-General for audit (Office of the Auditor-General for the Federation, 2024). The report attributes delay partly to late and revised financial statements and a prolonged vacancy in the Auditor-General's office. This shows why the constitutional 90-day audit period cannot be interpreted without its starting event: it runs after the Auditor-General receives the accounts, so late preparation upstream delays the entire accountability cycle.

More important than publication alone is disposition. The same institutional self-assessment found no documented internal mechanism or policy for systematic follow-up of financial, compliance or performance-audit recommendations; its relevant follow-up dimensions received zero (Office of the Auditor-General for the Federation, 2024). It also reported that the OAuGF lacked full financial autonomy because its releases depended on executive warrants. Because this is a self-assessment rather than an external causal evaluation, its scores should be read as a structured institutional diagnosis. They nevertheless identify a specific break: audit can produce answerability without ensuring correction.

Peer-reviewed case research adds an important qualification. Abdul-Baki, Diab and Kadir document instances in which Nigerian Auditors-General disclosed irregularities and resisted institutional pressure, while also showing that such resistance was neither uniform nor costless (Abdul-Baki et al., 2023). The implication is not that professional ethics are irrelevant. It is that a system should not depend on exceptional individual resistance. Timely accounts, protected audit resources, public PAC reports and a mandatory recommendation tracker make integrity less dependent on personal risk-taking.

Nigeria's next public-finance reform should therefore join systems that already exist rather than create another unconnected dashboard. Each programme and contract needs identifiers that remain stable across the appropriation, release, commitment, procurement, payment, physical-output and audit records. The Budget Office should publish in-year reports in the original budget classification with commitments and variance reasons; OAGF should maintain a current arrears and financial-statement calendar; BPP should publish procurement-coverage and competition denominators; and the Public Accounts Committees should disclose each finding's management response, hearing, recommendation, recovery or referral and closure date. The most revealing indicators would be reporting lag, percentage of commitments matched to cash plans, percentage of payments matched to contracts and verified milestones, repeat-audit-finding rate, and median time from an audit finding to documented closure.

This design separates fiscal outcomes from administrative performance. A deficit may be fiscally justified while transactions are well controlled, and a balanced aggregate position may coexist with opaque procurement or uncorrected audit findings. By following the same transaction to correction, Nigeria can determine whether digital controls reduce leakage, merely move discretion to another stage, or delay execution without improving value. The unresolved territorial question is whether comparable records and correction mechanisms exist beyond the federal centre—a problem taken up in the next section on state and local service delivery.

5.5 Local Administration and Service Delivery

Nigeria’s local-administration problem is not adequately described as insufficient decentralization. The federation contains 36 states, the Federal Capital Territory and 774 constitutionally listed local government areas, but authority over a local service is often divided among the federal government, a state ministry or agency, a state local-government service commission, a local council and the frontline facility. The result is a recurrent separation of mandate, money, personnel and answerability. A council may be associated with primary health care, basic education, sanitation, markets or local roads while lacking reliable control over the transfer, staff, procurement or data needed to deliver the service. Conversely, a state agency may exercise the operational authority while responsibility for failure remains attributed to “local government.” The relevant unit of analysis is therefore the service-delivery chain, not the tier named in law.

Constitutional recognition without complete operational autonomy

Section 7 of the 1999 Constitution guarantees a system of democratically elected local government councils but assigns state legislation a central role in their establishment, structure, composition, finance and functions. The Fourth Schedule lists local functions—including markets, local roads and streets, refuse disposal and participation in primary education, agriculture and health—while section 162 places local governments in the federation-account distribution system and also establishes State Joint Local Government Accounts (Federal Republic of Nigeria, 1999). These provisions recognize local government while leaving important rules of operation with the states. Suberu’s historical analysis accordingly describes Nigerian decentralization as recurrent bargaining over territorial power rather than a one-directional transfer of authority (Suberu, 2022).

The Supreme Court’s 11 July 2024 judgment altered one critical part of this arrangement. The majority held that states could not retain and use allocations due to local governments, authorized direct payment to democratically elected councils and treated caretaker administration as inconsistent with the constitutional guarantee of elected local government (Federal Republic of Nigeria, 2024). This is a material change in the payment route and in the leverage attached to council elections. It does not, by itself, settle who controls local personnel, which tier sets service standards, how state laws allocate functions, or whether a council possesses procurement, accounting and audit capacity.

Worugji and Worugji’s legal analysis reaches the same bounded conclusion. It finds that the judgment reaffirmed local status and financial autonomy but could not remove the continuing constitutional and statutory powers of states over council structure, elections, revenue sources and administration; the authors therefore characterize its practical effect as incomplete without clearer constitutional and implementation rules (Worugji & Worugji, 2026). This distinction matters for causal interpretation. Direct payment may remove one upstream veto, but it cannot be treated as evidence that resources reached a clinic, school or road, much less that outcomes improved.

Transfers matter, but conditional systems reveal the implementation mechanism

Nigeria’s subnational finances are highly heterogeneous. The National Bureau of Statistics reports that the 36 states and the FCT generated ₦2.4269 trillion in internally generated revenue in 2023 (National Bureau of Statistics, 2024). That aggregate is useful for describing the scale of state own-source revenue, but it is not a measure of local-government revenue and should not be divided by 774 or read as local fiscal capacity. Local councils remain strongly exposed to federation-account flows, and a national or state total conceals differences in population, tax bases, insecurity and administrative reach.

The State Fiscal Transparency, Accountability and Sustainability programme (SFTAS) shows what performance-linked intergovernmental reform can and cannot achieve. From 2018 to 2022, the programme offered $1.5 billion in performance-based grants to all 36 states. By its third year all states were publishing annual budgets and audited financial statements on time, 30 regularly published quarterly budget-implementation reports, 17 had at least 80 per cent of funds under a Treasury Single Account, 29 had consolidated revenue codes and 34 had updated urban property records (Isser & Zovighian, 2025). The programme therefore demonstrates that common standards, verification and financial incentives can change administrative routines across autonomous states.

Yet the same review cautions against treating publication as accountability or nominal revenue growth as stronger real capacity. States’ internally generated revenue rose from 19.6 per cent of total revenue in 2017 to 29.2 per cent in 2022 in nominal terms, while inflation eroded the real gain; citizen participation, audit independence, data quality and budget discipline remained uneven, and several practices weakened when grant incentives ended (Isser & Zovighian, 2025). Eighteen states adopted e-procurement, but only a handful maintained it after the programme. The lesson for local administration is precise: a transfer or compliance milestone can establish a routine, but sustainable service improvement requires a domestic owner, recurrent operating resources and an accountability loop that survives the incentive period.

Sector transfers expose a second mechanism—conditional access. The Universal Basic Education programme is financed in part through a statutory federal grant, but a state must provide a 50 per cent counterpart contribution and an action plan before accessing its allocation. The Universal Basic Education Commission reports that ₦61.369 billion was disbursed in 2024 to 28 states and the FCT that met the applicable requirements (Universal Basic Education Commission, 2025). This statistic measures access to and disbursement of the matching grant; it does not establish classroom completion, teacher presence or learning. It nevertheless shows why equal legal entitlement can produce different timing and territorial coverage when state fiscal space and administrative preparation determine access.

The Basic Health Care Provision Fund illustrates a related but distinct chain. Under the National Health Act, at least one per cent of the Consolidated Revenue Fund finances the BHCPF through several gateways. The NPHCDA gateway works through state primary-health agencies, local-government health authorities and facilities; 35 per cent of that gateway is designated for direct facility financing, and the current programme states a target of 17,600 fully functional primary-health-care facilities—one per ward—by 2027 (National Primary Health Care Development Agency, n.d.). The 17,600 figure is a target, not a verified count of functioning facilities. The administrative test is whether a named facility received funds, had authorized staff and essential inputs, delivered specified services and corrected a documented failure.

Why autonomy alone will not close the service gap

Three mechanisms explain why greater local financial discretion may coexist with weak delivery. First, fragmented assignment permits cost and blame shifting: each tier can claim that another controlled the staff, cash or standard. Second, weak own-source revenue and volatile transfers shorten planning horizons, making payroll and emergency spending easier to protect than maintenance or multi-year investments. Third, local political autonomy without auditable transactions can relocate discretion rather than make it answerable. Financial autonomy is therefore neither inherently corrupting nor sufficient for performance; its effect depends on electoral contestability, professional administration and records that citizens and oversight bodies can use.

This is also why a uniform solution can be misleading. Nigeria’s states and localities differ sharply in revenue base, population density, conflict exposure, urban form and staff availability. A common minimum data standard is necessary, but operating arrangements should reflect the service and territory. Dense metropolitan waste and transport systems require cross-boundary coordination; dispersed rural primary care requires reliable facility finance, staffing and referral networks; basic education requires teacher deployment and learning records that survive changes in political leadership. Centralization may preserve a national standard or purchasing scale, while local discretion may improve adaptation. The design question is which decision should be centralized, not whether the system should be labelled centralized or decentralized.

Policy design should begin with an assignment matrix for each major local service. For every function, the matrix should name the tier that sets the standard, finances the entitlement, hires and supervises staff, procures inputs, owns assets, reports outputs and answers complaints. Direct federation-account payments should carry council-level, machine-readable allocation and receipt records, but should not be conditioned on federal control over lawful local choices. Instead, minimum fiduciary conditions should require an enacted budget, bank reconciliation, procurement disclosure, payroll-to-post matching, timely accounts and an independent audit trail. Sector transfers should use the same council and facility identifiers so that education, health and infrastructure spending can be reconciled with actual personnel and outputs.

The most useful performance measures are consequently relational: median days from allocation advice to council receipt; share of payments matched to an enacted local budget and contract; share of frontline staff matched to a duty station and attendance record; share of facilities receiving the expected operating transfer on time; unit cost and service volume by facility; and median time from a citizen complaint or audit finding to a documented remedy. These measures do not assume that a richer or more autonomous council performs better. They reveal where authority, money, personnel or correction separates from the service citizens receive.

Local administration therefore connects the federal coordination and financial-control problems in sections 5.1–5.4 to the citizen-facing transaction. The next section asks whether digital identity, interoperability and online platforms can make that transaction more traceable and correctable—or instead create a new point of exclusion.

5.6 Digital Government and Administrative Accountability

Digital government should shorten the distance between an administrative decision and a citizen's ability to complete, verify and contest the resulting transaction. It can also widen that distance. When identity, payment and sector databases cannot exchange reliable records, a person may be enrolled in one system yet fail authentication in another. When an online channel becomes mandatory amid unequal connectivity and digital capability, the agency's processing cost is transferred to the user. When linked data expand administrative visibility without equally strong purpose limits, access controls and remedies, state legibility grows faster than accountability. Nigeria's central challenge is therefore not simply to put services online. It is to make the entire transaction interoperable, inclusive, auditable and correctable.

Online supply has advanced faster than universal capacity to use it

The 2024 UN E-Government Survey placed Nigeria 144th among 193 countries, with an E-Government Development Index (EGDI) of 0.4815. Its Online Service Index was 0.5372, compared with 0.4836 for telecommunications infrastructure and 0.4236 for human capital (United Nations Department of Economic and Social Affairs, 2024). The component pattern is more informative than the rank. Nigeria was classified in the middle EGDI group but the high online-service group: the assessed supply of online services had moved ahead of the infrastructure and human-capital conditions needed for broad and reliable use. EGDI nevertheless measures national readiness, not whether a particular applicant completes a transaction, receives a payment or succeeds on appeal.

The access constraint is changing but remains substantial. World Development Indicators estimate that internet use rose from 31.9 per cent of the population in 2020 to 41.2 per cent in 2024, while fixed-broadband subscriptions remained only 0.079 per 100 people in 2024 (World Bank, 2025a). This trend shows widening digital reach, not universal direct access. It also cannot identify who remains offline or whether a mobile connection is affordable and reliable enough to upload documents, authenticate identity or pursue an appeal. A service that works in an agency laboratory can therefore fail administratively when it assumes continuous connectivity, a personal device, literacy in the interface language or repeated paid data use.

Expansion of foundational identity illustrates both the achievement and the remaining inference gap. The World Bank reports that enrolment rose from 36.9 million in 2020 to 111.4 million by November 2024. Female enrolment increased from 14.9 million to 48.4 million and child enrolment from 0.75 million to 17.4 million, supported by relaxed documentary requirements for displaced people and refugees, stakeholder consultation and school-based campaigns (World Bank, 2025b). These are important reach indicators and show that institutional design can reduce documentary exclusion. They are not counts of unique active users, successful authentications or completed benefits. Administrative performance begins where enrolment statistics end: at the point where another agency must recognize the record and act on it.

From parallel identifiers to governed data exchange

Nigeria's administration has accumulated the National Identification Number (NIN), Bank Verification Number (BVN), SIM-registration records, voter records, tax identifiers, payroll systems and sector beneficiary registries. Earlier harmonization efforts, including the Nigeria e-Government Interoperability Framework, supplied a formal vocabulary for common standards and exchange. In March 2025 the Federal Ministry of Communications, Innovation and Digital Economy issued a National Digital Public Infrastructure Framework; NITDA then circulated draft technical standards for digital public infrastructure and the Nigerian Data Exchange (NGDX). Significantly, the draft itself identifies siloed platforms rooted in existing legal frameworks as an obstacle to integrated services and proposes common identifiers, transaction logging, accessibility, security and performance standards (National Information Technology Development Agency, 2025). The status matters: an issued framework and a draft technical standard demonstrate policy direction, not operational interoperability across federal, state and local services.

Research on Nigeria explains why the problem cannot be solved by an application programming interface alone. Identification harmonization can strengthen authentication and financial integrity only when registration is inclusive and the underlying database is governed by clear privacy and accountability rules (Monye & De Koker, 2022). A mixed-method study likewise identifies privacy, security, human-rights and socio-cultural risks in Nigeria's digital-ID programme (Eke et al., 2022). A political interpretation of the EndSARS protests and mandatory NIN-SIM linkage adds another mechanism: some citizens interpreted data integration through the possibility of surveillance, so technical reliability and institutional trustworthiness became inseparable (Okunoye, 2022). The latter is a qualitative commentary on a particular episode, not a population estimate of mistrust, but it identifies a design risk that enrolment totals cannot reveal.

Interoperability must consequently be established at four levels. Legal interoperability identifies the lawful purpose, controller, disclosure authority and retention rule. Organizational interoperability assigns ownership when two agencies' records conflict. Semantic interoperability gives shared meaning to fields such as resident, household, disability, facility and payment status. Technical interoperability defines exchange formats, authentication protocols, timestamps and security controls. If only the technical layer is built, an erroneous or unlawful decision can travel faster across government; if the organizational layer is absent, the citizen becomes the messenger between agencies that cannot reconcile their own records.

Privacy and administrative capability rise or fall together

The Nigeria Data Protection Act 2023 created a statutory framework for lawful and fair processing, security safeguards, data-subject rights and recourse, and established the Nigeria Data Protection Commission (Federal Republic of Nigeria, 2023). In administrative terms, these protections are production controls rather than obstacles added after digitization. Data minimization reduces the fields that can be wrong or exposed; purpose limitation clarifies which agency may act on a record; access logs identify responsibility; and rectification and human intervention can stop an identity error from propagating across services. The UN's Universal DPI Safeguards Framework similarly treats safety, inclusion and rights protection as requirements throughout the infrastructure lifecycle rather than as ex-post compliance (Office of the United Nations Secretary-General's Envoy on Technology & United Nations Development Programme, 2024).

Enforcement capacity is growing, but the available output statistics do not yet show whether citizens receive timely correction. NDPC reports 117 investigations in 2022, 177 in 2023, 213 in 2024 and 230 in 2025 (Nigeria Data Protection Commission, 2026). That sequence is a workload trend; it may reflect reporting, regulatory capacity or violations and cannot by itself establish prevalence or deterrence. The next accountability step is to publish a consistent case-flow series: complaints received and acknowledged, investigations opened, determinations and remedies, appeals, median processing time and repeat non-compliance. Without those denominators and dispositions, a higher count can be read neither as regulatory success nor as worsening privacy conditions.

Cybersecurity should be assessed at the same transaction level. Counts of secure servers, policy documents or compliant agencies do not show whether a citizen's case is protected. High-risk services require a data-flow map, role-based access, multifactor authentication for privileged users, tamper-evident logs, tested recovery and a manual continuity procedure. Audit samples should connect privileged access and system incidents to actual transactions, including whether a failed or compromised transaction was restored and the affected person notified. This turns cybersecurity from an asset inventory into evidence about service reliability and institutional responsibility.

A complete digital service ends with remedy, not login

Nigeria's inclusion strategy therefore requires channel equivalence rather than a digital-only mandate. Web, mobile, assisted service centres and lawful offline procedures should write to the same case record, apply the same eligibility rule and generate the same appeal right. Agent-assisted transactions should identify the agent and any lawful fee so that intermediation does not become an invisible source of extraction. Evidence from thirteen ministries in Cross River State reinforces the organizational side of this problem: a mixed-method study linked inadequate in-service training, staff resistance and weak capabilities in data security and information exchange to poor e-government implementation. Because it concerns one state civil service and fieldwork conducted in 2021, it should not be generalized to every Nigerian agency; it nevertheless shows why infrastructure procurement without workforce and change-management capacity may not improve service delivery (Inakefe et al., 2023).

The practical reform unit should be a small number of complete, high-value transaction chains rather than another general portal. For a selected benefit, licence, tax payment or facility transfer, government should publish the service standard; assign a single case identifier; record each status transition; provide a durable receipt; and attach the complaint to the originating transaction. Interoperability tests should report match, mismatch and manual-review rates. Inclusion tests should disaggregate completion and abandonment by sex, age, disability, location and channel where lawful and statistically adequate. Accountability tests should report unauthorized-access incidents, correction time, appeal reversals and repeat failures. These measures align with the OECD's distinction between government as a platform and user-driven service design: shared components create value only when they support a coherent service lifecycle organized around actual users (OECD, 2020).

The resulting policy test is narrower and more demanding than “digitize government.” A service is ready for digital scale only when its record can show who decided what, under which rule, using which data, whether the transaction completed and how an error was remedied. This standard connects digital government to the personnel, financial and local-delivery systems examined earlier in the chapter. Section 5.7 can then identify the recurrent administrative bottleneck: not the absence of platforms, but the loss of authority, information or corrective capacity as a decision passes between them.

5.7 Implementation Capacity and Administrative Performance

Nigeria's administrative problem is often described as an “implementation gap,” but that label is too broad to guide reform. Nigeria has policy plans, central coordinating bodies, statutory controls, reform programmes and increasingly capable digital platforms. The recurrent weakness is that decisions, money, personnel, delivery records and corrective action do not reliably remain connected from authorization to the citizen-facing result. A presidential priority can have several coordinating bodies but no official able to resolve a blocked dependency; an appropriation may not become predictable cash; a verified employee may not be matched to the required post and skill; a payment may lack a traceable physical milestone; a federation transfer may disappear from view before reaching a facility; and a digitally identified person may still be unable to complete or contest a transaction. Implementation capacity is therefore best understood as the repeated ability to maintain and repair this chain, not as the number of reform instruments adopted.

This definition also explains why a single national “capacity score” would be misleading. The institutional evidence assembled in Sections 5.1–5.6 differs in unit, year, coverage and measurement quality. More importantly, capability varies by ministry, state, local government, facility and task. An agency can administer a standardized payroll control well but struggle with a multi-agency infrastructure project; a state can publish fiscal reports on time while leaving audit recommendations unresolved. The defensible question is not whether Nigeria possesses or lacks capacity in the abstract, but where a specific delivery chain breaks, who can repair it and whether the repair becomes routine.

Implementation failure is a break in continuity, not one missing institution

The six preceding sections point to one common mechanism. At each stage, a visible administrative output can be produced without establishing the next link needed for performance. A priority list does not by itself allocate decision rights. A new organogram does not transfer statutes, staff, assets and records. Biometric payroll validation does not verify skills or duty-station presence. Financial authorization does not establish physical delivery. A transfer to a state or local account does not establish frontline production. Digital enrolment does not establish successful authentication, payment or remedy. The problem is thus not simply “weak coordination” or “insufficient monitoring”; it is a failure to preserve responsibility and evidence across organizational boundaries.

Incentives can induce compliance without making it durable

Nigeria's State Fiscal Transparency, Accountability and Sustainability (SFTAS) programme illustrates both the potential and the limits of performance incentives. Between 2018 and 2022, the programme used approximately US$1.5 billion in performance-based grants and independent verification across all 36 states. By the third year, every state was publishing annual budgets and audited financial statements on time, 30 published quarterly budget-implementation reports, 17 had Treasury Single Account coverage above 80 percent, 29 had consolidated revenue codes, 34 had updated urban property records, 33 had linked at least 95 percent of civil servants and pensioners to biometric or bank-verification identifiers, and 18 had introduced e-procurement (Isser & Zovighian, 2025). These results show that a common standard, credible verification and material incentives can overcome initial coordination costs and create new routines across highly diverse states.

Yet adoption was not equivalent to institutionalization. After the grants ended, only 20 states continued the high-level biometric or bank-verification linkage; budget deviation remained difficult to reduce; procurement transparency was uneven; and publication advanced more readily than audit independence, citizen influence or correction of identified problems (Isser & Zovighian, 2025). The contrast identifies a mechanism. A discrete and readily verified output—posting a document, enacting a code or linking a record—can be rewarded during a programme cycle. A durable capability requires recurrent budget provision, staff competence, an authority that uses the information, and a constituency able to impose a consequence when performance deteriorates. Where those complements are absent, the visible reform can persist as a formal shell or decay when the external reward disappears.

The appropriate policy response is not to abandon performance grants. It is to design them around a progression from adoption to use and then to correction. A first-stage indicator may verify that a state publishes a report in a common format. A second-stage indicator should test whether the report reconciles with budget, cash and procurement records. A third should test whether audit, legislative or citizen review produces a documented disposition and whether repeat exceptions decline. Continued financing should increasingly depend on these later links. This design makes sustainability observable before external incentives are withdrawn and reduces the risk of rewarding transparency that no decision-maker is required to use.

Control, professional autonomy and political oversight have different effects at different stages

The empirical literature also cautions against a universal prescription for tighter control. Rasul and Rogger's observational study combined management surveys with independent assessments of 4,700 Nigerian federal projects. Conditional on other management practices, autonomy- and discretion-related practices were positively associated with completion, whereas incentive- and monitoring-intensive practices were negatively associated with it (Rasul & Rogger, 2018). The design does not establish that monitoring causes failure in every agency or that estimates from the study period apply unchanged today. It does show why a target-heavy system can misfire when officials must coordinate multiple actors, adapt to unforeseen conditions or perform tasks whose quality is difficult to specify in advance.

Evidence from Ghana clarifies the task mechanism and provides a useful, though not automatically transferable, comparison. Rasul, Rogger and Williams studied more than 3,600 tasks across 45 central-government organizations. Autonomy was most positively associated with completion when tasks were difficult to specify ex ante but their achievement could be judged ex post; monitoring and incentives were less disadvantageous when tasks were clearer in advance (Rasul et al., 2021). Applied cautiously to Nigeria, the implication is that management arrangements should be selected at task level. Payroll reconciliation, tender disclosure and eligibility checks are standardized, high-risk transactions: they require clear rules, segregation of duties and complete logs. Resolving an insecure-area staffing gap, coordinating an urban project or transitioning a merged agency involves uncertainty: the responsible team needs bounded discretion to adapt, combined with an auditable decision record and ex-post scrutiny of results and adverse effects.

Political oversight creates a related trade-off. Using a nationally representative set of Nigerian public projects and an instrumental-variable strategy based on legislative committee membership, Rogger found that greater legislative influence increased the likelihood that a project was launched by 18 percent but reduced project quality by 15 percent and increased reported misuse of funds (Rogger, 2018). The estimates concern a particular historical portfolio of small public infrastructure projects, so they should not be generalized to all legislative scrutiny. Their analytical importance lies in separating mobilization from completion and quality. Political pressure can overcome bureaucratic inaction and accelerate funding, while pressure over specifications or contractors can weaken technical integrity. Oversight should therefore make completion, quality and financial exceptions visible together; counting launches or disbursements alone can reward the very intervention that later undermines value.

These studies jointly reject a choice between unchecked discretion and centralized micromanagement. The relevant design question has two dimensions: can the desired task be specified before work starts, and can the quality of the result be verified afterward? When both are clear, rules and automated checks are appropriate. When the method is uncertain but the result is verifiable, professional autonomy with ex-post review is more defensible. When neither is clear, government should narrow the problem, establish short learning cycles and avoid attaching high-powered incentives to a proxy that can be gamed. Autonomy without records can conceal capture; control without adaptive authority can produce delay, ceremonial compliance and distorted reporting.

Build an operating model around one service chain, then scale what survives correction

The preceding analysis suggests a sequence rather than another government-wide dashboard. Reform should begin with a small number of high-value, repeated service chains for which several administrative records already exist—for example, a primary-health facility transfer, a basic-education grant, a federal capital project or a social-benefit payment. For each chain, the responsible bodies should state the intended citizen-facing result, population and territory; assign the decision and escalation rights; establish stable identifiers across money, people, procurement, delivery and complaints; and publish a baseline for processing time, mismatch, failure, exclusion and correction. This turns an abstract “whole-of-government” ambition into cases that can be followed from authorization to remedy.

The next step is to review exceptions rather than merely aggregate successful cases. Managers need distributions of delay, vacancy, failed authentication, contract variation, regional disparity and complaint disposition. A dashboard can support this work only if it is a view over auditable source records and the official viewing a red status has authority to act. Creating a parallel reporting platform for the centre merely adds another discontinuity. Nor should shared identifiers collapse institutional boundaries: personnel authorities remain responsible for appointments, accounting officers for expenditure, procurement bodies for regulation, auditors for independent review, data controllers for lawful processing and elected officials for policy choices.

Finally, a pilot should be institutionalized only when it survives the correction test. This requires a recurrent budget, an authorized cadre and training path, a legal basis for necessary data exchange, an independent review route, a funded complaints function and evidence that repeat failures decline. Problem-driven iterative adaptation is useful here because it starts from a locally salient failure, permits bounded experimentation and expands demonstrated solutions (Andrews et al., 2013). In the Nigerian setting, however, adaptation must not become exemption from procurement, audit, privacy or equality obligations. The discretion to test a repair should be time-limited, documented and reviewable.

The reform theory is falsifiable. If connected records improve implementation, unmatched payments, untraceable transfers, unresolved dependencies and payroll-post mismatches should decline before broad social outcomes move. If bounded autonomy improves complex delivery, comparable teams with clearer decision rights and faster exception resolution should shorten completion times without increasing irregularity, exclusion or rights violations. If transparency has become accountability, audit and complaint dispositions should become faster and repeat findings should fall. If these intermediate changes do not occur, the presence of a new institution, portal or performance contract is not evidence that capability improved.

Nigeria's administrative performance is therefore uneven but diagnosable. SFTAS shows that common standards, verification and incentives can rapidly alter routines; project-level studies show that excessive control or political pressure can also displace quality and professional problem solving. The durable reform objective is continuity with correction: an operating system in which authority, resources, records and remedy remain connected across organizational and federal boundaries. Chapter 6 examines governance indicators and broader accountability conditions. Those indicators should be read as external signals to be explained by these concrete administrative mechanisms, not as substitutes for them.
