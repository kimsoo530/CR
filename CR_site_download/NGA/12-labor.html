12.1 Labor Force, Participation, and Informality

Nigeria's labor-market paradox is that work is widespread while secure and productive employment is scarce. In the second quarter of 2024, 79.5 percent of people aged 15 and above participated in the labor force and 76.1 percent were employed. Open unemployment was only 4.3 percent. Those figures do not describe a labor market close to full employment: 93.0 percent of employed people were in informal employment, 85.6 percent were self-employed, and only 14.4 percent worked as employees. A further 9.2 percent of employed people wanted and were available to work more hours, so the combined unemployment and time-related underemployment rate (LU2) was 13.0 percent (National Bureau of Statistics, 2024b). The central policy problem is therefore not the absence of economic activity. It is the institutional organization of work: whether activity generates adequate earnings, repeatable productivity gains, enforceable rights, social insurance, business records, and a tax base capable of financing public services.

Reading unemployment after the statistical break

The low headline rate must first be interpreted within Nigeria's redesigned labor-force survey. Since the fourth quarter of 2022, the National Bureau of Statistics (NBS) has applied the international standard under which a person is employed if he or she performed at least one hour of work for pay or profit during the previous seven days or was temporarily absent from such work. To be unemployed, a person must have no employment, have actively searched during the previous four weeks, and be available to start. Subsistence food production for own use is classified separately rather than as employment. These definitions make employment, unemployment, and non-participation mutually exclusive, bring irregular and casual work into view, and permit comparison with other countries using the same standard. They also mean that the new series cannot be spliced to the former Nigerian series, which used different hour thresholds, questionnaires, age coverage, and treatment of own-use production (National Bureau of Statistics, 2023; International Labour Organization, 2013).

This is a change in measurement, not evidence that labor distress disappeared between the old and new series. The one-hour criterion is intentionally an extensive-margin test—did any market-oriented work occur?—rather than a test of earnings adequacy or job quality. In a high-poverty setting, many adults cannot finance a long period of job search and will accept intermittent trade, transport, farm, repair, or household-enterprise work. NBS itself cautions that poverty and open unemployment can be inversely related across states because poorer people must undertake some activity to survive. A policymaker who reads the 2024 Q2 unemployment estimate of 4.3 percent as 95.7 percent satisfactory employment therefore commits a category error (National Bureau of Statistics, 2024b). The appropriate dashboard places unemployment beside hours, potential labor force, employment status, informality, earnings, and protection.

The short post-redesign sequence reinforces this interpretation. Participation and employment fluctuate by several percentage points, while informality remains tightly clustered between 92.3 and 93.5 percent. Falling time-related underemployment—from 13.7 percent in 2022 Q4 to 9.2 percent in 2024 Q2—is potentially favorable, but six non-seasonally-adjusted quarterly observations cannot establish a structural improvement. Agricultural seasons, survey sampling, inflation-driven changes in labor supply, and movement between self-employment and wage work can all affect quarter-to-quarter estimates. More importantly, the employee share fell from 16.0 percent in 2022 Q4 to 14.4 percent in 2024 Q2 (National Bureau of Statistics, 2024b). The evidence supports a bounded conclusion: Nigeria absorbs a high share of adults into some work, but the institutional form of that absorption changes much less than the headline unemployment rate.

Informality is heterogeneous, but not institutionally neutral

Informal employment is not synonymous with illegality, inactivity, or entrepreneurial failure. It includes own-account operators and workers in economic units not adequately covered by registration, labor regulation, taxation, or social protection, as well as employees whose employment relationship lacks effective protection. Some firms avoid registration strategically; many workers instead enter informality because formal wage demand is limited and household enterprises provide the most accessible means of earning. In this setting, self-employment can be a genuine opportunity, a risk-sharing response, or disguised labor-market rationing. Those categories require different policy instruments.

Evidence from Nigeria's General Household Survey illustrates the heterogeneity. Nwaka and Emeagwali (2024) studied informal service-oriented non-farm household enterprises and found that wholesale and retail trade accounted for a large share of such employment. Enterprises operating outside the home recorded higher average and median labor productivity than home-based enterprises, while the determinants and returns differed across service activities and by gender. The study does not provide a current national productivity estimate—the underlying panel covers 2010–2015—but it identifies an enduring mechanism: premises, market access, time allocation, household care obligations, capital, and enterprise location condition what self-employment can produce. Treating all own-account workers as either latent high-growth entrepreneurs or undifferentiated subsistence workers would therefore misallocate support.

The national statistics reveal the same segmentation from another angle. In 2024 Q2, self-employment reached 94.3 percent of rural employment compared with 79.7 percent in urban areas. Informality was 97.5 percent in rural areas and 90.0 percent in urban areas. It declined sharply with education: excluding agriculture, the informal share was 98.4 percent among workers with no formal education but 14.7 percent among those with post-secondary education (National Bureau of Statistics, 2024b). Education is not a simple causal treatment here—it also proxies household resources, location, occupation, and access to networks—but the gradient shows why business registration alone cannot transform the labor market. Formality is jointly produced by worker capabilities, firm demand, infrastructure, enforceable contracts, affordable compliance, and institutions able to deliver benefits in return.

This mechanism helps explain why a punitive formalization strategy can destroy livelihoods without creating better jobs. Registration, taxation, or inspection imposed on a low-productivity operator who lacks reliable electricity, working capital, premises, digital access, and customers raises fixed costs but does not necessarily raise output. Conversely, leaving the enterprise outside all public systems preserves immediate flexibility but blocks credit histories, procurement access, insurance, workplace protection, and reliable administrative data. ILO Recommendation No. 204 consequently defines transition to formality as a combined process: preserving livelihoods and entrepreneurship while extending rights, protection, sustainable enterprises, and coherent economic and social policy—not merely moving names into a registry (International Labour Organization, 2015).

From counting activity to governing employment quality

Nigeria's administrative challenge is to make labor-market information actionable across institutions. NBS measures household labor supply, but the Federal Ministry of Labour and Employment, state ministries, education and training bodies, pension and health-insurance institutions, tax administrations, business registries, and enterprise-support agencies control different parts of the transition. The International Labour Organization's assessment of Nigeria's public employment services found a fragmented system and emphasized the need for physical and digital access, employer engagement, reliable labor-market information, and a functioning National Electronic Labour Exchange rather than a portal detached from placement services (International Labour Organization, 2023). A national unemployment target alone cannot coordinate these organizations because it does not identify which workers lack hours, which enterprises could raise productivity, or which formal vacancies remain unfilled.

The same distinction matters for macroeconomic policy. The 2024 Nigeria Development Update describes weak productive job creation as part of the country's inclusive-growth constraint and connects household pressure to inflation, low productivity, and limited opportunities (World Bank, 2024). Stabilization may restore investment conditions, but the employment transmission is neither immediate nor automatic. Interest rates, exchange-rate adjustment, energy costs, and public spending affect microenterprises, wage-paying firms, and agricultural households differently. Monitoring only GDP growth and unemployment could therefore miss a shift toward more precarious self-employment even while aggregate activity expands.

Three linked policy tests follow. First, the federal statistical system should publish the full labor-underutilization dashboard—LU1 through LU4, hours, earnings, status in employment, informality, social-protection coverage, and transitions—using consistent quarterly definitions and confidence intervals. Administrative records can complement, but not replace, the household survey because payroll, pension, tax, and registry data disproportionately observe formal workers. Second, enterprise policy should segment workers and firms by productive potential and constraint. A survivalist own-account worker may need basic income security, childcare, health coverage, safe workspace, or seasonal support; an established microenterprise may benefit from reliable power, payment records, procurement access, credit, and simplified registration; a wage-paying firm requires predictable regulation and skills matching. Success should be measured by real earnings, hours, survival, employee creation, protection, and productivity—not registration counts alone.

Third, social protection should become portable across employment statuses. With nine in ten workers informal, a system tied only to conventional employer payrolls excludes most of the labor market by design. Flexible contribution schedules, subsidized minimum protection for low-income workers, interoperable identity and payment systems, and channels through cooperatives, associations, platforms, and value-chain firms can expand coverage. Yet digital enrollment must produce actual eligibility, benefits, correction, and grievance resolution; otherwise it repeats the mistake of treating registration as an outcome. The underlying principle is institutional reciprocity. Workers and enterprises are more likely to enter formal arrangements when compliance provides credible access to protection, finance, contracts, infrastructure, and dispute resolution.

Nigeria's labor-force participation is thus neither a demographic dividend already realized nor proof of widespread joblessness. It records intense economic engagement under conditions in which households bear much of the risk that formal firms and social insurance absorb elsewhere. The task for public administration is to convert that activity into a ladder: from survival work to more productive enterprise, from irregular work to protected employment, and from invisible transactions to reciprocal relations with the state. Section 12.2 examines why access to that ladder differs sharply for women, youth, people with disabilities, and residents of different regions.

12.2 Women, Youth, Vulnerable Employment, and Regional Inequality

The employment ladder described in Section 12.1 is not equally accessible. Nigeria's aggregate participation rate conceals at least four different forms of exclusion: women may work but remain concentrated in informal, low-hour, and non-wage activities; young people must cross the school-to-work boundary without a dense formal entry-level labor market; people with disabilities confront physical, informational, and discriminatory barriers before job quality can even be considered; and residents of different states encounter sharply different combinations of schooling, security, infrastructure, sectoral demand, and implementing capacity. These disadvantages interact. A young woman with a disability in a conflict-affected rural area does not experience four independent gaps that can be repaired by one generic training course.

Participation can be similar while job quality diverges

Nigeria's redesigned survey makes the distinction visible. In 2024 Q2, female and male labor-force participation was close—79.1 and 79.9 percent respectively—yet the similarity disappears further along the employment chain. Women had higher unemployment, time-related underemployment, self-employment, informality, and youth NEET rates, while their share in wage employment was lower. People with disabilities had a labor-force participation rate of only 36.7 percent, compared with 80.0 percent among people reporting no disability (National Bureau of Statistics, 2024b). The first gender result should therefore not be read as evidence that economic opportunity is equal, and the disability result should not be reduced to an individual lack of skill.

The World Bank's 2024 analysis of the same new labor-force framework reaches a compatible conclusion: employed women were more likely to be underemployed and less likely to hold wage or high-skilled jobs (World Bank, 2024). The mechanism is not a single cultural preference. Unequal schooling histories, unpaid care, safety and mobility, access to assets and finance, sectoral segregation, employer practices, and the legal and administrative environment jointly shape the set of feasible jobs. High female participation can coexist with a large opportunity gap when households depend on women's earnings but care and market institutions restrict where, when, and on what terms those earnings are generated.

Peer-reviewed workplace research adds a dimension that household-status categories cannot capture. Drawing on 52 interviews and about 200 hours of observation in Nigerian organizations, Adisa et al. (2024) find that patriarchal norms and high power distance can suppress women's workplace voice. The evidence is qualitative and does not estimate a national effect, but it identifies why obtaining a wage job is not the end of inclusion: limited ability to raise concerns, influence decisions, or challenge treatment affects retention, advancement, safety, and the quality of organizational learning. This mechanism links labor-market inequality to public administration because labor inspection, grievance systems, anti-discrimination enforcement, and employer human-resource practices determine whether formal rights are usable.

Nigeria's federal and legally plural setting makes uniform national assumptions especially risky. The World Bank's subnational Women, Business and the Law study covers the Federal Capital Territory and seven states representing all six geopolitical zones. It finds variation in statutory, customary, and Islamic-law settings and persistent gaps in supportive frameworks for pay, parenthood, childcare, and marriage. It also finds that expert assessments of outcomes do not always track the formal legal score (World Bank, 2025g). This is diagnostic rather than a representative survey of all women or all 37 jurisdictions; nevertheless, it shows why a federal law, a state program, and implementation in a local labor market are separate stages. Legal reform without childcare, enforcement, accessible services, and budgeted institutions may change the rule but not the feasible choice.

Youth exclusion is a failed transition, not one unemployment number

For young people, open unemployment is again too narrow. In 2024 Q2, unemployment among those aged 15–24 was 6.5 percent, but 12.5 percent were not in employment, education, or training (NEET); the female NEET rate was 14.3 percent compared with 10.9 percent for males. Among employed youth, time-related underemployment was 15.1 percent—well above the all-age rate of 9.2 percent (National Bureau of Statistics, 2024b). Each indicator observes a different failure. Unemployment identifies active search without work, NEET identifies detachment from three transition institutions, and underemployment identifies insufficient hours among those already counted as employed. None measures earnings, learning on the job, job stability, or whether work matches a qualification.

Research on Nigerian youth unemployment places this transition problem in a wider political economy of poverty, insecurity, weak services, and fragmented social policy (Virk et al., 2024). The review argues that Nigerian youth programs commonly emphasize training and job creation through ad hoc, selectively targeted schemes while structural and implementation constraints remain. This critique does not imply that skills are irrelevant. It means that training supply cannot create demand, safe mobility, electricity, firm capacity, or trustworthy placement services. A certificate becomes valuable only when a local production system uses the capability and when the program can trace a participant from enrollment to completion, placement, retention, and earnings.

Age boundaries also matter. The NLFS NEET measure used here concerns ages 15–24, while Nigerian youth policies and programs may use broader upper limits (National Bureau of Statistics, 2024b). Mixing those populations can make program reach appear larger or outcomes appear inconsistent. The administrative solution is not to choose one universal definition, but to publish the denominator for every objective: school-to-work transition at 15–24, early-career progression at 25–34, or a legally defined program population. Cohort and transition measures are more informative than a single total of “youth beneficiaries.”

State variation changes the policy diagnosis

The annual 2023 NLFS provides state-representative estimates and shows why neither a simple north–south narrative nor the national mean is sufficient. The national NEET rate for ages 15–24 was 15.6 percent, but estimates ranged from 4.5 percent in Zamfara and 6.0 percent in Bauchi to 36.0 percent in Rivers and 38.1 percent in Abia. Time-related underemployment ranged from 0.3 percent in Nasarawa and 1.2 percent in Sokoto to 31.1 percent in Kaduna and 33.9 percent in Plateau. The employment-to-population ratio ranged from 55.7 percent in Rivers to 88.4 percent in Bauchi. Among employees, only 26.3 percent nationally reported entitlement to either pension or health insurance, with estimates ranging from 3.9 percent in Abia to 65.9 percent in Zamfara (National Bureau of Statistics, 2024c).

These ranges should not be converted into a league table of state government performance. Sector composition, agricultural seasonality, urbanization, conflict, education, household survival strategies, sample uncertainty, and the strict unemployment-search rule all affect the estimates. A low unemployment or NEET rate may reflect successful absorption, but it may also coexist with agricultural work, early withdrawal from school, low earnings, or weak search institutions. Conversely, a high urban unemployment rate can partly reflect a labor market in which people can sustain active search for wage work. The correct use of state data is diagnostic: combine participation, LU2–LU4, NEET, employment status, informality, protection, earnings, and sector, then test the likely mechanism with administrative and qualitative evidence.

Disability makes the same point in sharper form. The 2024 Q2 participation gap of more than 40 percentage points cannot plausibly be repaired by entrepreneurship finance alone (National Bureau of Statistics, 2024b). Accessible transport and workplaces, inclusive schooling, assistive technology, non-discriminatory recruitment, benefit rules that do not punish work, accessible digital systems, and enforcement all affect whether a person can enter and remain in employment. Because disability status is self-reported and survey samples become thin after state-by-disability disaggregation, national surveys should publish uncertainty and avoid unstable rankings. Administrative systems should record accommodations and service completion without substituting registered beneficiaries for population-based outcome measurement.

An intersectional delivery model

The policy implication is to replace categorical program inventories with a transition-and-barriers model. For women, the relevant package may combine safe transport or workspace, childcare, property and finance access, enforceable workplace rights, and portable protection. For young entrants, it may combine foundational remediation, apprenticeship standards, employer-linked placement, mobility support, and income protection during search. For people with disabilities, accessibility and reasonable accommodation must be built into mainstream employment services rather than confined to a small separate scheme. State governments should adapt delivery to local sector demand and institutions while using common federal definitions and outcome measures.

Accountability should follow the transition. Every program should identify its eligible denominator, barrier diagnosis, responsible delivery chain, counterfactual or comparison strategy where feasible, and outcomes at six and twelve months. Completion and disbursement are intermediate outputs. The outcomes are employment status, hours, real earnings, job retention, movement into wage or higher-productivity self-employment, protection coverage, and closing of group gaps. Publishing these results by sex, age, disability, state, and urban-rural residence would expose whether a program reaches easier-to-serve participants while excluding those with multiple constraints.

This approach also clarifies the federal division of labor. The federal government can set survey standards, labor rights, program safeguards, portable social-protection architecture, and interoperable reporting. States and local institutions control many of the schools, transport conditions, markets, enterprise services, and enforcement interfaces that determine access. Employers, unions, cooperatives, and worker associations supply information that administrative records miss. Coordination is successful only when a person can move through these institutions without repeatedly proving identity, eligibility, disability, training, and placement status to disconnected systems—and when consent and grievance mechanisms protect against harmful data sharing.

Nigeria's labor-market inequality is thus not one average gap. It is a set of unequal transitions from school, care, disability, place, and informal work into more secure livelihoods. The next section shifts from people to the demand side of that transition: whether agriculture, industry, and services generate jobs whose productivity and skill requirements can sustain upward movement.

12.3 Agriculture, Services, Industry, and Skills Mismatch

The demand-side question is not simply whether workers leave agriculture. It is whether farms, factories, and service firms reorganize production in ways that raise value added per hour, create learnable occupations, and reward acquired skills. Nigeria can experience sectoral change without productive transformation: workers may move from smallholder farming into petty trade or other own-account services while remaining exposed to low capital intensity, volatile demand, and weak protection. Conversely, productivity growth within agriculture can improve rural incomes even before a large employment shift occurs. This section therefore distinguishes three margins: productivity within sectors, movement of workers between sectors, and the quality of the jobs created at the destination.

The agricultural employment-output gap is a diagnostic, not a verdict

Agriculture still combines a large employment role with a smaller share of measured output. In the project's World Development Indicators series, agriculture accounted for 34.1 percent of total employment in 2024, while agricultural value added was 25.9 percent of GDP (World Bank, 2025a). The gap is consistent with lower average value added per worker in agriculture than in the rest of the economy, but it is not a direct estimate of an individual farm worker's productivity. Employment and GDP use different denominators; the comparison does not adjust for hours, seasonality, multiple jobs, household production, price changes, or differences within each sector. Moreover, the WDI value-added series follows an internationally comparable historical series and should not be spliced mechanically to Nigeria's newly rebased national accounts.

The gap is a coarse signal that agriculture combines a large employment role with a smaller measured output share. It is not an estimate of an individual worker's productivity or the causal gain from leaving agriculture because hours, seasonality, multiple jobs, prices, capital, selection, and within-sector heterogeneity differ.

McCullough (2017) shows why this qualification matters. Across Sub-Saharan Africa, apparently large sectoral labor-productivity gaps narrow substantially after accounting for hours worked and observable worker characteristics. The result does not imply that Nigeria has no agricultural productivity problem. It means that a raw employment-output comparison cannot by itself establish that moving the same worker into any non-agricultural activity will generate the observed sector average. Selection, working time, capital, location, and market access all help produce that average. Policy should therefore target the constraints that change what workers and firms can produce, rather than treat sector labels as productivity interventions.

Nigeria's agricultural constraint is a linked production-and-market system. Insecurity can prevent cultivation or shorten the usable season; uncertain land rights can weaken incentives and collateral; limited irrigation and extension can reduce yields and adaptation; storage losses, poor feeder roads, and unreliable electricity can make processing unviable; fragmented standards and finance can block entry into formal value chains. A training course in improved production cannot overcome a road that becomes impassable, a processor without power, or a buyer unable to verify quality. The 2025 World Bank Group Country Private Sector Diagnostic consequently identifies cassava and soybeans as investment opportunities but ties their potential to infrastructure, finance, inputs, aggregation, standards, and market coordination rather than to farm skills alone (World Bank Group, 2025).

This chain perspective changes the appropriate unit of policy. The object is not an isolated farmer but a geographically defined value chain linking producers, aggregators, storage, processors, logistics providers, laboratories, lenders, and buyers. Public support should specify the bottleneck at each interface and measure whether it falls: travel time and post-harvest loss, reliable cold or processing hours, rejection rates against a stated standard, yield and net real income, buyer concentration, and the share of producers with repeat contracts. Subsidized inputs or training completions are outputs, not evidence that a value chain has become more productive or resilient.

Services are not one destination sector

The service sector must also be disaggregated. Household retail, transport, personal services, finance, telecommunications, professional services, health, education, and ICT-enabled business services differ sharply in capital requirements, tradability, firm size, learning, and earnings. A rising service-employment share can therefore represent at least three processes: survival self-employment absorbing workers whom firms do not hire; domestic services growing with urban demand; or higher-productivity tradable services connecting Nigeria to regional and global markets. Aggregating them as “services” hides the very mechanism that structural-transformation analysis needs to identify.

Dimova and Pela (2018), using representative Nigerian data, find that services absorbed the largest share of relatively skilled self-employed workers, yet skills among self-employed people in all three broad sectors remained below those of salaried workers, and returns to skills were particularly low in self-employed manufacturing. Their evidence predates the redesigned labor survey and should not be read as a current prevalence estimate. Its analytical value is to reject a smooth sequence in which leaving the primary sector or entering entrepreneurship automatically signals innovative, productivity-enhancing transformation. Business scale, technology, finance, demand, and the institutional environment determine whether skills can be used and rewarded.

Digital and professional services nevertheless offer a concrete route to occupational upgrading because some tasks are tradable without transporting a physical product. Nigeria's large urban markets, English-language workforce, payments ecosystem, and diaspora links can support such activities. But connectivity is only an enabling input. Section 11.3 showed that coverage does not equal meaningful use; reliable power, affordable broadband, data protection, firm management, certification, and access to customers remain complementary. A policy that counts trained coders or registered startups without observing contracts, retention, exports, real earnings, and firm survival can mistake capability supply for market demand.

The distinction is reinforced by experimental evidence. A job-training program was evaluated across five Nigerian cities (Croke et al., 2023). Two years later, participants were 26 percent more likely than the control group to work in ICT-enabled services, but they were not more likely to be employed overall. Sector-relevant skills and location affected switching, and the program also interacted with gender norms. The study supports a bounded conclusion: targeted training can help suitable candidates cross a sectoral barrier, but it does not by itself expand aggregate employment. A credible scale-up must therefore test both sides of the market—whether firms have additional vacancies and contracts, and whether trainees gain and retain those jobs—rather than extrapolate from training completion or sector entry alone.

Industry requires a production ecosystem, not a training target

Manufacturing matters because it can combine scale, standardization, supplier linkages, and on-the-job learning, but its employment effect depends on which activities expand and how they source inputs and technology. Nigerian firms face a bundle of costs: unreliable electricity and self-generation, logistics delays, exchange-rate and input-price volatility, short-tenor finance, standards compliance, insecurity, and unpredictable administration. These constraints interact. A firm may not adopt machinery when imported inputs and spare parts are uncertain; without new technology it may not demand advanced technical skills; training providers then see weak placement and employers continue to report difficulty finding experienced workers. Calling this outcome a “skills mismatch” locates the failure in workers even when the equilibrium is jointly produced by weak labor demand and a high-cost business environment.

The World Bank Group's 2025 diagnostic illustrates the complementarity. It reports private credit at 12.9 percent of GDP in 2024, compared with 20.1 percent for Sub-Saharan Africa and 34.0 percent for lower-middle-income economies, while unreliable power and low learning-adjusted schooling constrain investment and hiring (World Bank Group, 2025). These are not interchangeable obstacles. Credit reform will not compensate for an unusable feeder road, and a technical qualification will not generate a vacancy where power and demand make production unprofitable. The ILO's 2024 Nigeria policy brief similarly frames job-rich growth as a structural-transformation problem requiring a pro-employment macroeconomic framework and industrial policy, not an isolated labor-market program (International Labour Organization, 2024).

Industrial policy therefore requires disciplined coordination rather than a long list of favored sectors. A sector or cluster compact should state the market failure, the public inputs to be delivered, the firms' reciprocal commitments, measurable employment and capability outcomes, a timetable, and an exit or correction rule. Support may be justified where coordinated infrastructure, standards, supplier development, or learning generates spillovers that individual firms cannot capture. It becomes rent protection when benefits persist without verified investment, productivity, domestic linkage, or additional employment. The relevant test is additionality: what changed because of the intervention compared with a defensible baseline, and who captured the benefit?

Skills mismatch is an institutional matching problem

“Mismatch” should be reserved for evidence about the relationship between workers' capabilities and jobs, not used as a residual explanation for all low productivity. Four distinct problems require different remedies. A foundational-skills deficit concerns literacy, numeracy, and problem solving; an occupational deficit concerns competencies for a defined task; an information mismatch occurs when workers, firms, and providers cannot observe vacancies or capability; and a demand mismatch occurs when too few productive positions exist at the offered wage and location. Geography, care obligations, gender norms, disability access, security, and transport can prevent a technically qualified person from accepting a job. Credentials may also fail to signal competence when curricula, assessment, and employer needs are weakly connected.

The institutional response should begin with occupations and vacancies, not course catalogs. Employer and worker organizations can define observable competencies; providers can combine classroom instruction with supervised workplace learning; public employment services can verify vacancies and referrals; and tracer systems can measure placement, six- and twelve-month retention, real earnings, occupational match, and employer demand for repeat cohorts. The ILO's assessment of Nigeria's public employment services identifies fragmented labor-market information and limited institutional capacity as central constraints (International Labour Organization, 2023). These systems should share minimum definitions and interoperable records while protecting consent and privacy, but state and local delivery must remain responsive to the actual sector mix and commuting area.

No single training experiment establishes a national program effect, and no employer survey establishes that curricula alone caused a reported shortage. Evaluation should therefore match the intervention. Randomized or credible quasi-experimental designs can test scholarships, information, placement, or training where assignment permits. Cluster and value-chain interventions require firm and worker panels, comparable locations where feasible, and pre-specified measures of productivity, employment, earnings, survival, and displacement. Distribution also matters: a program may raise average output while excluding women, rural workers, or smaller firms, or may shift workers between firms without creating net employment.

The central policy implication is sequencing. In agriculture, solve the binding value-chain interfaces while building producer capability. In manufacturing, coordinate reliable infrastructure, finance, standards, technology adoption, and reciprocal firm commitments before promising mass placements. In tradable services, pair specialized training with connectivity, employer demand, and verified contracts. Across all three, publish the causal theory and the weakest link before announcing beneficiary targets. Nigeria's problem is not that labor occupies the “wrong” sector in the abstract; it is that too many production systems cannot consistently convert labor, capital, and knowledge into higher-value output and durable jobs.

This demand-side diagnosis prepares the human-capital question in Section 12.4. Education and health can expand capability, but the demographic dividend materializes only when productive organizations use that capability. Human-capital policy and sectoral transformation are therefore complements: better learning without job creation can produce frustration and exit, while investment without foundational and occupational capabilities can generate vacancies that firms cannot fill.

12.4 Education, Human Capital, Productivity, and the Demographic Dividend

Nigeria's demographic dividend is best understood as a conversion problem. A larger working-age share changes the ratio of potential producers to dependants, but it does not determine what those adults know, whether they are healthy enough to work, whether firms hire them, or whether workplaces deepen their skills. Section 8.1 established that the share of the population aged 15–64 rose to 55.9 percent in 2024 while the young-age dependency ratio remained 73.3 children per 100 working-age people (World Bank, 2025a). This section asks what must happen between childhood and productive adulthood for that age-structure opportunity to become higher output and welfare rather than intensified competition for weak schools, precarious work, and urban services.

Human capital is a sequence of realized capabilities

School enrollment is only the first stage. A child must enter at the appropriate age, attend regularly, learn foundational skills, progress and complete, remain healthy, acquire occupational and socioemotional capabilities, enter a job that uses them, and continue learning at work. Loss at any stage lowers the return to earlier investment. The 2024 Nigeria Demographic and Health Survey reports that 49 percent of primary-school-age children attended primary school and 46 percent of secondary-school-age children attended secondary school. These net attendance ratios should not be confused with gross enrollment, which can exceed or obscure the age-appropriate population, and neither measure establishes what attending children learned (National Population Commission and ICF, 2025).

The learning adjustment is large. The World Bank's 2024 Human Capital Public Expenditure and Institutional Review reports that Nigeria had 5.0 learning-adjusted expected years of schooling in 2020, compared with 7.8 in Indonesia, 8.5 in Kenya, and 9.2 in Türkiye. It also reports a 26 percent out-of-school rate among primary-school-age children in the underlying comparison (World Bank, 2024b). These figures combine older source years and should not be presented as 2024 observations. Their function is diagnostic: time nominally spent in school and age-appropriate access are both substantially reduced when translated into effective learning.

This evidence also identifies a data limit that should remain visible. A widely cited “learning poverty” percentage for low- and middle-income countries must not be relabeled as a Nigerian estimate. The World Bank has stated that Nigeria lacked the direct assessment data needed for the earlier country estimate. Learning-adjusted schooling and current attendance are serious warning signals, but they are not substitutes for a nationally representative, comparable assessment of reading and numeracy with state, sex, disability, language, and socioeconomic breakdowns. The administrative implication is to build the assessment and disclosure system, not to manufacture precision from a regional average.

Years of schooling do not have a fixed productivity return

Human-capital theory predicts that capabilities can raise productivity and earnings, but credentials and years of schooling are imperfect measures of those capabilities. The return depends on learning quality and on whether production systems use the acquired skills. One study used regional exposure to the introduction and withdrawal of Nigeria's 1976 Universal Primary Education program as an instrument and estimated an average earnings return of 2.8 percent per additional year of schooling in 1997–1999—low relative to common international benchmarks (Uwaifo Oyelere, 2010). The result is historically and cohort specific; it is not a current national return. Its lasting lesson is that expanding years of schooling does not guarantee a large earnings gain when learning, labor demand, technology, and institutions limit productive use.

The 2026 World Bank Human Capital Index Plus (HCI+) extends this insight across the life cycle. It reports that Nigerian student test scores in 2025 were one point below their 2010 level, an education-pillar score below the lower-middle-income median, and tertiary completion of 11.3 percent—slightly above the Sub-Saharan African average of 10.5 percent but below the lower-middle-income average of 17.6 percent. Its modeled scenario suggests that raising effective schooling to 12 years could increase long-run labor earnings by about 20 percent (World Bank Group, 2026). This is a model-based potential gain, not a forecast, budget estimate, or effect of increasing enrollment alone. “Effective” schooling incorporates learning, and the earnings translation presumes that health, employment, and production conditions allow capability to be used.

Section 12.3 demonstrated the demand-side counterpart. Training in five Nigerian cities increased participants' probability of switching into ICT-enabled services without increasing overall employment. The implication is not to reduce education investment; it is to evaluate education and job creation jointly. If graduates cannot find organizations that use advanced capabilities, returns fall, migration becomes more attractive, and households may rationally question long schooling investments. If firms adopt technology but schools and apprenticeships do not supply foundational and occupational capability, vacancies, imported expertise, and wage inequality can rise together. Productive transformation requires both sides of this matching equilibrium.

Capability is accumulated in homes, neighborhoods, and workplaces

The conventional education-versus-health division is administratively convenient but analytically incomplete. Nutrition, disease, care, violence, transport, electricity, digital access, and neighborhood safety affect whether a child can attend and learn. Adult capability then changes through work experience, supervision, task complexity, and mobility. The HCI+ framework accordingly extends human capital beyond school and clinic inputs to homes, neighborhoods, and workplaces. For Nigeria it reports that interactive caregiving is associated with much higher child development readiness and cites experimental evidence from northern Nigeria in which a multidimensional package of cash, assets, and health counseling produced a persistent reduction in stunting (World Bank Group, 2026). The package result should not be decomposed into an effect of any one component without an appropriate design.

Workplaces are especially important in Nigeria because 85.6 percent of employed people were self-employed in 2024 Q2 (National Bureau of Statistics, 2024b). A large formal employer can spread the cost of supervision, equipment, certification, and progressive task assignment across workers; an own-account operator may learn through customers and associations but lack the capital and organizational ladder needed for increasingly complex tasks. This does not make wage work intrinsically productive or self-employment intrinsically stagnant. It makes job form and firm capability part of human-capital policy. Apprenticeship quality, mentor competence, equipment, enforceable agreements, recognition of prior learning, and post-training market access determine whether workplace experience accumulates portable skill.

Public policy must therefore govern interfaces. Early-childhood and nutrition services must connect to school entry; schools must transfer records without excluding mobile or displaced children; secondary and tertiary providers must receive credible employer and occupation signals; public employment services must distinguish verified vacancies from generic aspirations; and social protection must allow workers to move between own-account, apprenticeship, and wage statuses. Each transition needs a responsible institution, a usable identifier, consent and correction rules, and an outcome measure. An enrollment database that cannot show attendance or learning, or a training registry that cannot show retention and earnings, is an administrative inventory rather than a human-capital system.

The demographic dividend is territorial and conditional

Nigeria does not have one demographic timetable. The 2024 National Transfer Accounts study estimates different economic life-cycle profiles for Nigeria, Lagos, and Kaduna. Lagos entered the modeled first-dividend period earlier and displayed a higher support ratio, while the study inferred much earlier labor income among children in Kaduna (Olasehinde et al., 2024). The two state cases cannot represent the federation, and modeled dividend dates are not observed welfare gains. They show why the same national working-age share can coexist with different fertility, schooling, child labor, earnings, and intergenerational-transfer regimes.

This territorial variation changes fiscal design. States with high child dependency, weak attendance, insecurity, and limited own-source revenue face a simultaneous need to expand capacity and improve quality. Per-pupil financing based on unreliable enrollment can reward inflated records or miss mobile children; equal nominal grants ignore differences in remoteness, language, disability access, and security costs. Federal transfers and Universal Basic Education financing should therefore combine transparent population and deprivation formulas with verified service standards and learning gains. State and local plans should publish school-age denominators, attendance, teacher presence, assessment results, transition rates, and unit costs, with independent checks and uncertainty where the population base is modeled.

The same logic applies to gender. Closing female schooling and employment gaps can affect the dividend through later and more autonomous fertility decisions, healthier children, greater household income, and a larger effectively employed workforce. But the mechanism requires safe schools and transport, continued attendance through adolescence, reproductive autonomy, childcare, protection against workplace discrimination, and jobs whose hours and location are feasible. A scholarship can relax one constraint without changing the others. Evaluation must therefore trace whether beneficiaries remain in school, learn, delay premature exit, enter further training or employment, and obtain real earnings—not merely whether funds were transferred.

A conversion dashboard for policy accountability

Nigeria should manage the dividend as a cohort pipeline rather than a slogan. For each state and sex, a minimum dashboard should connect early-child development and nutrition; age-appropriate attendance; foundational learning; completion and transition; occupational competence; NEET and labor underutilization; employment status; real earnings; protection; and employer-verified skill use. The indicators need not be collapsed into one rank. Their value lies in locating attrition: whether children fail to enter, attend without learning, leave before completion, complete without transition, or work without accumulating capability.

Policy sequencing should follow that diagnosis. Where foundational learning is the bottleneck, remedial instruction, teacher support, language-appropriate materials, attendance, and assessment integrity take priority over expanding tertiary seats. Where young people possess capability but productive vacancies are scarce, the response lies in the sectoral investment and firm constraints analyzed in Section 12.3. Where care, safety, disability, or transport blocks participation, targeted access measures are required. Where workers enter firms but tasks remain simple, technology adoption, management capability, apprenticeships, and progression standards become central. Every instrument should specify which link it repairs and what downstream outcome should change.

The demographic dividend is therefore neither a date nor a consequence of population size. It is the cumulative result of survival, learning, autonomy, productive demand, and institutional coordination over decades. Nigeria's age structure creates an opportunity, but public administration determines whether children become capable adults, whether capable adults enter productive organizations, and whether those organizations continue to deepen their skills. Chapter 13 examines the education, health, and welfare delivery systems that supply the first part of this chain; Chapter 12 closes by showing why their success must ultimately be judged in productive and equitable life-course outcomes.
