14. Financial Sector and Economic Institutions

14.1 Central Bank, Monetary Policy, and Exchange-Rate Governance14.2 Banks, Private Credit, and Financial Stability14.3 Capital Markets, Fintech, Payments, and Inclusion14.4 Financial Regulation, Investor Protection, and Reform Priorities

14.1 Central Bank, Monetary Policy, and Exchange-Rate Governance

The Central Bank of Nigeria governs monetary policy, banking supervision, payments, and exchange-rate operations. Recent reforms aimed to restore price discovery in the foreign-exchange market, tighten monetary policy, and rebuild credibility. Monetary governance is politically salient because exchange-rate movements and inflation quickly affect food prices, wages, imports, and business confidence.

14.2 Banks, Private Credit, and Financial Stability

Nigeria's banking system is regionally important, with major banks operating across Africa. Private credit, however, is constrained by macro volatility, collateral problems, government borrowing, legal enforcement, and sectoral risk. Banking reform therefore intersects with courts, land registries, credit bureaus, and monetary credibility.

14.3 Capital Markets, Fintech, Payments, and Inclusion

Capital markets, pensions, insurance, fintech, mobile money, and payments are areas of institutional sophistication. BVN, NIN-linked services, instant payments, fintech firms, and pension assets show administrative and market capacity. Inclusion gaps remain, especially for women, rural populations, informal firms, and conflict-affected communities.

14.4 Financial Regulation, Investor Protection, and Reform Priorities

Financial regulation must balance innovation and stability. Crypto, digital lending, consumer protection, AML/CFT, bank recapitalization, and foreign-exchange risk all require supervisory capacity. Nigeria's financial sector is one of the clearest examples of how strong private innovation can coexist with public regulatory stress.

Nigeria has a sophisticated banking, payments, and capital-market ecosystem, but credit depth remains shaped by inflation, government borrowing, collateral constraints, exchange-rate risk, and supervisory confidence.
