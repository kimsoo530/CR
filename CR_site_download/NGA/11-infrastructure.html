11. Infrastructure, Energy, and Digital Connectivity

11.1 Electricity Access, Power Reform, and Gas-to-Power

Nigeria's electricity problem is often described as a shortage of megawatts. That description is incomplete. Households and firms need a service that is available when required, delivered at usable voltage, metered accurately, priced transparently, and supported by a payment chain that finances maintenance and new investment. A connection that supplies only intermittent power may improve the official access rate without displacing diesel generators or enabling machinery to operate. Nigeria's central electricity challenge is therefore to convert a fragmented portfolio of grid, captive generation, mini-grids, and stand-alone systems into reliable services while making the costs and responsibilities of each part visible.

Access has risen, but connection is not reliability

The World Bank's World Development Indicators estimate that the share of Nigeria's population with access to electricity rose from 48.0 percent in 2010 to 61.2 percent in 2023. The longer series shows real expansion from 35.2 percent in 1991, but also reversals and periods of stagnation rather than a smooth transition to universal service (World Bank, 2025a, indicator EG.ELC.ACCS.ZS). Because this indicator records whether electricity is available through a grid or off-grid source, it does not reveal daily hours, outage frequency, voltage quality, affordability, or the amount of electricity that a household or firm can actually use.

The access share increased substantially over the long run, but access identifies a connection or usable off-grid source rather than daily hours, outage frequency, voltage quality, affordability, or consumption. It must therefore be read alongside NERC's operational, loss, metering, and service-quality evidence.

The distinction changes the diagnosis. Improved access can coexist with extensive self-generation when public supply remains unreliable. Household evidence shows that better grid service would significantly reduce generator use, although higher-income households would retain backup capacity because they place a high value on continuity (Oseni, 2016). The implication is not that generator ownership should be prohibited before the grid improves. It is that reliability has an economic and environmental value that an access headcount misses: every hour of dependable service can displace a higher-cost unit of petrol or diesel generation, reduce equipment damage, and make productive activity less dependent on the wealth needed to purchase backup power.

Distributed systems can close part of this service gap more quickly than national-grid extension. The Nigeria Electrification Project supported 125 mini-grids and more than one million solar-home-system sales, through which the World Bank reports that over 5.5 million people obtained electricity access. Its successor, the US$750 million Distributed Access through Renewable Energy Scale-up project, targets new or improved service for more than 17.5 million people and electricity for almost 237,000 micro, small, and medium enterprises (World Bank, 2025e). These are project objectives and reported program outputs, not proof that every connection supplies adequate power over its asset life. The more demanding test is whether systems remain operational, tariffs remain affordable, batteries are replaced, productive users increase consumption, and service persists after capital subsidies end.

The binding constraint runs through the whole electricity value chain

The Nigerian Electricity Regulatory Commission's third-quarter 2025 report makes clear why adding nominal generation capacity alone cannot solve the problem. Across 28 grid-connected plants, average available capacity was 5,430.34 MW, but the average plant-availability factor was only 39.86 percent: 60.14 percent of installed capacity was unavailable for dispatch. Average hourly generation was lower still at 4,179.15 MWh/h, reflecting mechanical outages, gas constraints, and low offtake as well as plant availability. These are different bottlenecks. A plant may be installed but mechanically unavailable; available generation may be constrained by gas or water; dispatchable energy may not be evacuated by transmission; and electricity delivered to a distribution network may not be billed or collected.

The table describes a circular rather than a linear failure. In 2025 Q3, aggregate technical, commercial, and collection loss was 33.27 percent, an improvement from 37.92 percent in the preceding quarter but still far above the 20.54 percent efficient-loss allowance used in the tariff model. NERC estimated the excess as a ₦108.75 billion quarterly revenue loss. At the same time, only 55.37 percent of active registered customers were metered, with rates ranging from 28.65 percent in Yola and 29.69 percent in Jos to more than 84 percent in Eko and Ikeja. Unmetered consumers face estimated billing and weaker confidence in the relationship between payment and service; low collection then weakens distributors' balance sheets and their capacity to invest in feeders, transformers, meters, and maintenance. Poor service thereby reduces willingness to pay, while weak payment further degrades service.

Tariff policy cannot be separated from this operational chain. In 2025 Q3 the federal government's tariff-subsidy obligation was ₦458.75 billion, equal to 58.63 percent of the generation-company invoice. The amount was lower than in Q2 mainly because distributor energy offtake fell, not because the sector had reached a sustainable cost structure (Nigerian Electricity Regulatory Commission, 2026a). A tariff increase imposed without verified service improvement and comprehensive metering transfers inefficiency and exchange-rate risk to consumers. Conversely, holding tariffs below cost without a transparent, budgeted subsidy leaves generators and distributors unable to plan and crowds out other public spending. The relevant policy sequence is feeder-level measurement, loss reduction, service verification, targeted support for vulnerable users, and then tariff adjustment tied to delivered quality—not an abstract choice between full cost recovery and universal subsidy.

This diagnosis is consistent with the longer reform literature. Unbundling and privatization altered ownership but left transmission, market liquidity, gas infrastructure, regulation, and political intervention tightly interdependent (Ogunleye, 2017). Arowolo and Perez (2020) similarly argue that incomplete privatization, an underdeveloped gas network, and weak market regulation prevented the standard electricity-reform model from functioning as intended. The failure is not evidence that ownership is irrelevant; it shows that privatizing distribution assets cannot create a viable market when contracts, tariffs, fuel, transmission, metering, and payment discipline remain misaligned.

Decentralization creates a coordination test, not an automatic solution

The Electricity Act 2023 replaced the earlier federal framework and enabled states to establish and regulate intrastate electricity markets while NERC retained authority over interstate, national-grid, and international activities (Federal Republic of Nigeria, 2023b). By May 2026, regulatory oversight had formally transferred to 15 state regulators (Nigerian Electricity Regulatory Commission, 2026b). This can bring licensing, consumer protection, distribution planning, and enforcement closer to the geography of service failure. It can also create multiple tariff methodologies, technical rules, data systems, and appeal channels around networks that remain physically and financially connected.

The decisive issue is therefore the interface between jurisdictions. A state regulator can authorize an embedded generator or mini-grid, but it cannot independently assure national-grid stability, interstate transmission, gas delivery, or settlement of legacy obligations. Nor should a transfer allow liabilities and low-performing assets to remain federal while revenues and viable customers move to a state market. NERC, the Nigerian Independent System Operator, the Transmission Service Provider, state regulators, distributors, and bulk-market institutions need a common data and settlement protocol covering feeder boundaries, energy flows, technical standards, subsidy responsibility, customer complaints, and treatment of cross-border assets.

State-level variation makes decentralization analytically valuable because it permits comparison. The 2025 metering spread among distributors is too large to be treated as a single national condition. State markets should publish a small common scorecard: hours supplied by feeder and customer band; outage frequency and duration; voltage violations; metering coverage; billing and collection efficiency; technical and commercial losses; connection time; complaints and resolution; subsidy per delivered kilowatt-hour; and safety incidents. Comparable definitions would allow citizens and regulators to distinguish genuine improvement from the administrative relabeling of the same service.

Gas is a bridge only when fuel, networks, and payments arrive together

Gas-fired plants remain central to the national grid, so domestic gas can provide dispatchable electricity alongside hydropower and variable renewable energy. Yet Nigeria's gas endowment does not automatically become power. Pipeline insecurity, insufficient gathering and transport infrastructure, weak payment by the electricity market, competing export and industrial uses, plant outages, and transmission constraints determine whether gas reaches a turbine and whether its output reaches a paying customer. NERC attributed the Q3 2025 decline in thermal generation partly to gas constraints, mechanical outages, and low load offtake. This is why a new gas plant without a bankable fuel agreement, evacuation capacity, and credible payment chain may add nominal capacity without dependable energy.

The institutional pattern is longstanding. Research on the political economy of power reform identifies gas infrastructure and transmission as connected constraints rather than independent construction gaps (Ogunleye, 2017; Arowolo & Perez, 2020). A recent Natural Resource Governance Institute assessment likewise concludes that gas cannot meet Nigeria's access ambitions by itself when supply infrastructure, transmission, and sector payments remain unresolved; distributed solar has a stronger near-term role for many unserved and underserved users (George-Ikoli & Sayne, 2026). The report is a policy diagnostic rather than a causal evaluation, but its value is to test whether gas-to-power proposals specify the full delivery chain.

Nigeria therefore needs a portfolio rather than a fuel slogan. Existing gas plants merit rehabilitation where verified fuel supply, heat-rate efficiency, transmission capacity, and payment discipline make additional output cheaper and more reliable than alternatives. Grid-connected solar, hydro, storage, and transmission investment can reduce fuel exposure and complement dispatchable plants. Mini-grids and stand-alone systems can serve locations where grid extension is slow or uneconomic, provided regulation clarifies what happens when the main grid arrives and who pays for interconnection or asset conversion. Large industrial and commercial users can support embedded generation, but regulators should prevent their exit from leaving captive residential customers with an unaffordable network.

The policy implication follows from the diagnosis: Nigeria should manage electricity as a set of measurable service contracts. Public support to a plant, distributor, state market, or mini-grid should specify the energy delivered, reliability, loss reduction, metering, affordable-service obligation, emissions and safety performance, fiscal cost, and correction mechanism. The World Bank's 2026 restructuring of its Power Sector Recovery operation is instructive because it records tariff shortfalls of about ₦1.9 trillion per year in 2024 and 2025 and shifts attention toward time-bound investments with measurable reliability gains after broader reform disbursements underperformed (World Bank, 2026c). That is not a case for abandoning system reform. It is a case for judging reform by whether electricity reaches users reliably and whether the institutions responsible for each failure can be identified, financed, and held to account.

11.2 Transport, Ports, Logistics, and Corridor Governance

Nigeria's logistics problem is not adequately described by the number of kilometres of road, port berths, or railway projects. A shipment succeeds only when it passes predictably through every link between origin and destination: a farm or factory road, consolidation point, trunk corridor, border or port, terminal, customs process, and final distributor. Delay at any interface ties up trucks and inventory, increases spoilage and finance costs, and encourages firms to hold larger stocks. The appropriate unit of analysis is therefore an end-to-end journey and its variability, not an isolated asset.

This distinction also clarifies the role of government. Customs, the Nigerian Ports Authority (NPA), terminal concessionaires, the Nigerian Railway Corporation, federal and state road agencies, police and other security bodies, municipal traffic authorities, and private logistics firms each control only part of a corridor. Individually rational actions can still produce a collectively unreliable chain. Nigeria needs both physical investment and an institutional arrangement capable of measuring time, allocating responsibility, and maintaining performance across organizational boundaries.

The comparative signal is persistent unreliability, not absence of activity

The World Bank's Logistics Performance Index (LPI) combines expert perceptions of customs, infrastructure, shipment arrangements, logistics competence, tracking, and timeliness. In the locally preserved World Development Indicators series, Nigeria's overall score moved from 2.40 in 2007 to 2.81 in 2014, then fell to 2.53 in 2018 and stood at 2.60 in the latest recorded wave. Nigeria remained far below South Africa and did not establish a sustained lead over Ghana; Kenya temporarily moved above 3.3 in 2016 before falling back. These comparisons identify a durable system-level weakness, but they are not annual measures and cannot show that a particular port, road, or reform caused a change (World Bank, 2023).

Nigeria's score fluctuated between 2.4 and 2.81 rather than showing sustained convergence toward South Africa. The perception-based survey index diagnoses the reliability of the logistics chain, not a direct journey time, and sparse waves preclude attributing a change to a single reform.

The absence of sustained improvement is compatible with substantial freight activity. It suggests that scale has grown faster than the reliability of the institutions that coordinate it. A large domestic market can generate high cargo volumes while firms still face uncertain clearance, queueing, damaged roads, security stops, weak tracking, and unpredictable delivery. For industrial policy, reliability matters as much as average cost: an exporter that cannot promise a delivery window may lose an order even when the quoted freight rate is competitive.

A productive port is a node, not a completed corridor

The NPA reports that cargo throughput increased from 71.2 million metric tons in 2023 to 103.3 million in 2024, while container throughput rose 9.7 percent to 1.745 million TEU. Average vessel turnaround improved only slightly, from 4.7 to 4.6 days, although the new Lekki deep-sea port reported 2.5 days (Nigerian Ports Authority, 2025). These administrative figures show expansion and differences across ports. They do not establish that a container cleared customs quickly, found a truck or train slot, exited the port area without delay, and reached an inland destination predictably. Throughput is also composition-sensitive: liquid bulk accounted for 55.6 percent of the reported 2024 cargo, so aggregate tonnage should not be treated as container-service performance.

Section 10.4 documented the clearance bottleneck at Tin Can Island and the importance of the National Single Window. The additional point here is that customs digitization cannot by itself remove congestion beyond the customs gate. Nigeria's landlord-port reforms transferred terminal operations to concessionaires and produced measurable changes in terminal performance, but complaints about services and the distribution of regulatory authority persisted (Onwuegbuchunam, 2018). Port reform therefore requires an interface contract among NPA, customs, terminal operators, shipping lines, truckers, rail operators, and Lagos transport agencies. It should specify appointment systems, document interoperability, empty-container handling, truck and wagon access, gate hours, incident response, and a common clock from vessel arrival to inland delivery.

This measurement architecture prevents a common policy error: moving delay from one administrative boundary to another and declaring success. Median time should be paired with the 90th percentile because a lower average can coexist with a long tail of shipments exposed to repeated inspection, informal payments, documentation problems, or scarce transport capacity. Results should also be disaggregated by port, cargo type, destination corridor, and firm size so that a reform benefiting large compliant traders is not mistaken for a universal gain.

Road dominance makes maintenance and rural access national logistics policy

Roads carry more than 90 percent of passenger and freight movements, according to the World Bank's Nigeria rural-access project appraisal. Yet the 2024 scale-up operation estimated that 80 percent of the road network was in poor condition and tied new financing to State Road Funds and State Rural Access Roads Agencies rather than construction alone (World Bank, 2024b). The institutional design is important. Federal highways, state roads, and local or rural roads form one commercial route, but their budgets, engineering standards, and maintenance responsibilities are fragmented. A repaired trunk road has limited value to farmers if feeder roads become impassable during rains; conversely, a rural road program cannot reduce wholesale prices when the main corridor remains insecure or congested.

Maintenance is therefore not a residual engineering item. Politicians receive visible credit for commissioning a new road, while routine drainage clearing, pavement inspection, bridge maintenance, and axle-load enforcement produce diffuse benefits and are easier to postpone. Deferred maintenance then converts predictable operating expenditure into a much larger rehabilitation bill. Climate exposure intensifies this cycle: higher rainfall, flooding, and heat damage surfaces and culverts, while weak drainage and unregulated development increase failure around cities. State Road Funds can help only if revenues are protected, condition surveys determine priorities, contracts are performance-based, and expenditure and road quality are publicly reported. A dedicated fund without those controls can simply create another off-budget allocation channel.

The distributive stakes are substantial. Rural access connects farms not only to markets but also to storage, processing, health care, schools, and non-farm work. Better roads may increase farm-gate prices and reduce losses, but these effects depend on competition among buyers and transporters; otherwise part of the benefit can be captured through lower purchase prices or unchanged freight margins. Monitoring should therefore pair engineering outputs with travel time, seasonal passability, freight prices, marketed volumes, spoilage, and household access. This is the evidence needed to distinguish a completed contract from an improved economic service.

Rail is valuable where service frequency and interfaces make it usable

Rail is often presented as the natural solution to overloaded roads. The comparison is more conditional. National Bureau of Statistics data record 2.18 million rail passengers in 2023 and a 102 percent annual rise in rail cargo volume, but the absolute freight volume remained only about 318,000 tons (National Bureau of Statistics, 2024). A Lagos-Kano corridor study using 2017-2018 operational data found road freight overwhelmingly larger and faster: trucks completed the route in roughly 2-3 days, compared with 5-6 days by rail. Rail nevertheless performed better in freight ton-kilometres per vehicle-hour and throughput per unit of energy (Daramola, 2022). The evidence explains why lower unit cost in principle does not guarantee modal shift. Shippers purchase frequency, routing flexibility, security, and delivery time as well as transport capacity.

The appropriate rail strategy is thus commodity- and corridor-specific. Regular container or bulk flows can support scheduled rail services where port sidings, inland terminals, rolling stock, loading equipment, and last-mile road connections operate as one system. Rehabilitation that increases track speed but leaves a wagon shortage or an unreliable timetable unresolved will not attract freight. Nor should passenger and freight objectives be mixed without explicit capacity allocation. Contracts should define train frequency, on-time departure and arrival, wagon availability, loss and damage, interchange time, safety, and the road-kilometres displaced—not merely kilometres of track built.

Corridor governance must govern time, territory, and accountability

A corridor is also a political territory. Gambino and Reboredo's (2024) study of the Abidjan-Lagos corridor shows how infrastructure promises generate competing expectations, contested land and labour relations, and periods of suspension as projects and livelihoods wait on uncertain decisions. These effects are not captured by a travel-time model. New highways, port expansions, and logistics hubs can shift land values, displace residents or informal workers, and redirect trade between cities and countries. Unresolved compensation and livelihood claims can delay implementation and undermine the legitimacy needed for enforcement.

The solution is not a single super-agency that replaces every legal mandate. It is a corridor performance compact with a designated convenor, shared data definitions, named owners for each failure point, and escalation rules across federal, state, local, and private actors. The World Bank's data-driven corridor framework recommends combining conventional logistics indicators with spatial and operational data to observe the full chain rather than relying on national averages (World Bank, 2024a). Lagos offers a partial institutional lesson: a metropolitan authority with a statutory transport fund can coordinate modes more effectively than project-by-project administration, although the model still requires accountability for service quality, distribution, and land-use effects (World Bank Independent Evaluation Group, 2016; World Bank, 2025d).

For Nigeria, the practical sequence is to select a small number of economically important journeys—such as port-to-inland industrial and agricultural corridors—and publish a baseline from origin to destination. Investment should then target the binding interface revealed by the data. If the main delay is documentary duplication, interoperability and risk-based inspection precede new concrete. If it is gate congestion, appointments and depot capacity come first. If unreliable rail frequency is binding, operating contracts and rolling stock matter before network extension. If seasonal feeder-road failure drives food losses, maintenance and drainage dominate a flagship expressway.

This approach changes infrastructure policy from a catalogue of projects into a verifiable service strategy. The policy objective is not to maximize cargo tonnage, roads commissioned, or kilometres of rail in isolation. It is to reduce the predictable and tail risks of moving people and goods, while making the fiscal, environmental, labour, and distributional costs of that improvement explicit. A corridor-level public dashboard—covering end-to-end time, variability, cost, safety, emissions, maintenance condition, and grievance resolution—would give firms a planning signal and allow citizens and governments to see which institution is responsible when the chain fails.

11.3 Digital Connectivity, Broadband, and Cyber-Institutional Capacity

Nigeria's mobile networks, digital payments, software firms, fintech platforms, and creative industries demonstrate that private actors can scale digital services under difficult infrastructure conditions. Yet a country can have a dynamic digital market without possessing a capable digital state. Public digital capacity requires more than connectivity: people must be able to afford and use a service; agencies must know which record is authoritative; systems must exchange only necessary data under a lawful purpose; users must be able to correct errors and appeal exclusion; and critical services must withstand cyber incidents. The central issue is therefore whether connectivity is being converted into trustworthy, inclusive, and resilient public infrastructure.

Network reach does not equal meaningful use

World Development Indicators show a long expansion in internet use, from 11.5 percent of Nigeria's population in 2010 to 41.2 percent in 2024. Mobile subscriptions rose much faster and were close to one subscription per person in 2022-2023 before the reported series fell to 70.8 per 100 people in 2024. The two indicators are not directly comparable: internet use refers to people, whereas mobile subscriptions count active SIMs, including multiple subscriptions held by one person. The 2024 subscription break should be read with changes in subscriber verification and administrative records, not as proof that almost three in ten Nigerians suddenly lost a handset. Neither measure reveals whether a user has a smartphone, affordable data, reliable electricity, adequate speed, digital skills, or confidence in online services.

Internet use reached 41.2 percent of the population in 2024, while mobile subscriptions are SIM counts rather than unique people. The abrupt change in subscriptions per 100 people in 2024 should be read with subscriber-record and denominator changes, not as a one-year collapse in device access. Neither series measures affordability, skills, service quality, or the safe use of digital public services.

The gap between coverage and meaningful use is socially structured. GSMA survey evidence for 2022 found that women were 38 percent less likely than men to use mobile internet in Nigeria. Although reported mobile ownership was high for both men and women, only 25 percent of men and 13 percent of women used at least three mobile-internet applications daily (GSMA, 2024). This is survey-based evidence from a particular year, not a current census, but it identifies the mechanism that national subscription totals miss: device cost, data affordability, literacy, perceived relevance, safety, and control over household resources shape whether nominal coverage becomes regular use.

The policy implication is to evaluate connectivity as a service ladder. Coverage maps and subscriptions remain useful, but they should be joined by median and lower-decile speed, outage time, data cost relative to income, smartphone access, regular and diverse use, and gender, income, disability, age, rural, and state-level gaps. Infrastructure subsidies should be conditioned on independently verified coverage and quality in underserved locations. Public services should be designed for low bandwidth, small screens, local-language support, assistive technology, and intermittent connectivity, with an assisted or offline channel when denial of access would affect a legal entitlement.

Digital identity creates value only through reliable authentication and redress

Nigeria's National Identification Number (NIN) has expanded rapidly. The National Identity Management Commission reported 121,404,601 unique records at 30 June 2025, of which 52.9 million were recorded as female and 68.5 million as male (National Identity Management Commission, 2025). World Bank reporting traces enrolment from 36.9 million at project launch to 111.4 million by November 2024 and notes efforts to remove documentation barriers for internally displaced people and refugees and to expand child and female enrolment (World Bank, 2025b). These are important gains in foundational coverage. They are not equivalent to universal identity, successful authentication, or access to a particular benefit.

An identity system produces public value when an eligible person can enrol, authenticate, update a record, correct an error, recover credentials, and complete a service without excessive travel, payment, or discretion. Linking the NIN to banking, telecommunications, welfare, tax, health, education, and civil registration can reduce duplicate records and make transactions more convenient. The same linkage can also propagate an error across systems or turn failure to authenticate into simultaneous exclusion from multiple services. Coverage must therefore be paired with authentication success by channel and population group, correction time, rejected or duplicate records, enrolment quality, grievance resolution, and the share of public programs that can verify identity without retaining unnecessary biometric data.

Research conducted before passage of the Nigeria Data Protection Act 2023 remains useful for identifying these design risks. Eke and colleagues (2022) found that privacy, security, human-rights, ethical, and sociocultural concerns were central to public evaluations of the national ID program. Okunoye (2022) distinguishes public trust from system trustworthiness and links acceptance to limits on data sharing, surveillance risk, and meaningful legal safeguards. The 2023 Act addressed an important legislative gap by establishing data-processing principles, data-subject rights, and the Nigeria Data Protection Commission (Federal Republic of Nigeria, 2023). But a statute does not automatically repair legacy databases, specify every inter-agency purpose, or give a person an effective remedy when an automated match fails.

Nigeria should therefore treat NIN as a credential and public trust infrastructure, not as a universal database key that authorizes unrestricted data pooling. Each use needs a defined legal purpose, minimum necessary attributes, retention period, accountable controller, access log, correction route, and independent audit. Authentication can often answer a limited question—such as whether a person is unique, above an age threshold, or enrolled in a program—without transferring the full identity record. Federated or tokenized verification can reduce concentration and breach risk, but technical architecture is not a substitute for legal limits and accessible redress.

Interoperability is an institutional agreement before it is an API

Nigeria already has major functional identifiers and platforms, including NIN, Bank Verification Number, tax, passport, voter, company, payroll, payment, and social-registry systems. Their coexistence is not itself duplication: agencies often need different attributes and statutory purposes. The problem arises when records cannot be reconciled, fields have incompatible definitions, agencies repeatedly collect the same evidence, or data are exchanged without an authoritative source and accountability trail.

NITDA's 2025 draft technical standards for digital public infrastructure specify interoperability, role- and attribute-based access, data classification, immutable audit logs, multi-factor authentication, encryption, accessibility, multilingual capacity, performance targets, recovery, and vulnerability testing (National Information Technology Development Agency, 2025). These provisions identify the right operational domains, but the document was issued as a draft. Its ambitious technical targets should not be reported as achieved national performance, and compliance cannot be inferred from publishing a standard.

The binding issue is institutional ownership. Before connecting systems, agencies need a common data dictionary, an authoritative register for each attribute, lawful-purpose agreements, versioned interfaces, service-level commitments, and rules for notifying downstream systems when a record changes. A national data exchange should log which organization requested which attributes for what purpose and permit audit by both the responsible agency and the data-protection authority. It should also publish non-sensitive performance metrics: transaction volume, uptime, response time, failed matches, correction time, security events, and unresolved grievances. Interoperability without these arrangements may accelerate error and surveillance rather than service delivery.

Cybersecurity must be tested at the service and supply-chain level

Nigeria was classified in Tier 3, “Establishing,” in the ITU Global Cybersecurity Index 2024, while Ghana, Kenya, Rwanda, and Tanzania were in the highest tier (International Telecommunication Union, 2024). The index assesses national commitments across legal, technical, organizational, capacity-development, and cooperation pillars; it does not measure the probability that a specific Nigerian platform will be breached or guarantee service continuity. The peer gap is nevertheless informative: rapid digitization has outpaced the institutional integration of cybersecurity measures.

Digital identity, payments, registries, cloud services, telecom networks, and vendor software form an interdependent attack surface. Chima, Ogbar, and Abayomi-Alli (2021) emphasize that cybersecurity, identity, and data protection cannot be treated separately because compromise at enrolment, authentication, storage, or exchange can undermine the entire ecosystem. Centralization can improve standards and monitoring, but it also raises the consequence of a single credential, privileged account, software dependency, or cloud configuration failing. Outsourcing does not transfer public accountability; agencies remain responsible for vendor access, subcontractors, patching, incident reporting, recovery, and the protection of citizens whose data they process.

The relevant control system begins with an inventory of critical services, data, dependencies, owners, and recovery objectives. Agencies should use multi-factor authentication for privileged access, least-privilege permissions, encryption and key management, immutable logs, tested backups, vulnerability disclosure, patch deadlines, independent penetration tests, supplier-risk clauses, and rehearsed incident response. Metrics should distinguish compliance activity from resilience: the number of audits or staff trained is an input; time to detect and contain an incident, restored service within a tested objective, affected records, repeated vulnerabilities, and notification to users are outcomes. Public disclosure must be calibrated so that accountability improves without revealing exploitable detail.

A trustworthy digital state is measured by completed services, not portals

Nigeria's opportunity is to combine private digital innovation with a public architecture that reduces the cost of proving identity, making a payment, submitting information, and receiving a decision. The failure mode is a collection of agency portals that digitize existing forms while preserving duplicate evidence, office visits, discretion, and exclusion. International digital-government frameworks therefore emphasize user-driven, data-informed, government-as-a-platform, open-by-default, and proactive services rather than isolated websites (OECD, 2020; United Nations Department of Economic and Social Affairs, 2024).

Implementation should start with a limited set of high-volume life events—such as birth registration, school entry, business registration, tax filing, social protection, or pension claims—and map the whole user journey. For each service, government should publish completion rate, completion time, failed authentication, repeat submission, payment and travel costs, assisted-channel use, accessibility failures, grievance resolution, system uptime, and privacy or security incidents. Disaggregation is essential: a rising national completion rate can conceal the exclusion of rural residents, women, displaced people, persons with disabilities, or those whose records do not match.

The resulting policy sequence is conditional rather than technological. Extend connectivity where coverage and quality bind; subsidize devices or access where affordability binds; retain assisted channels where skills or disability bind; repair records and redress where identity failure binds; standardize data and responsibilities before connecting registries; and expand digital services only when data protection and recovery controls are operational. The UN's universal DPI safeguards framework reinforces this logic by treating safety, inclusion, accountability, and effective remedy as design requirements rather than add-ons (Office of the United Nations Secretary-General's Envoy on Technology & United Nations Development Programme, 2024). Nigeria's digital state will be credible when more people complete services safely and can correct government error—not when the number of databases, identifiers, or portals alone increases.

11.4 Infrastructure Finance, Climate Resilience, and Maintenance

Nigeria's infrastructure deficit encourages a search for more capital, but financing is only one stage in the life of an asset. A road, power line, drainage system, port, broadband network, or public building creates value only if the project is selected against alternatives, completed, operated, maintained, adapted to changing hazards, and replaced at the appropriate time. Borrowing or attracting a private partner can move the initial cost off the current capital budget; it cannot eliminate the economic cost, foreign-exchange exposure, user-charge obligation, contingent liability, or maintenance requirement. The central policy question is therefore not simply how Nigeria can finance more projects, but how it can finance reliable services over the whole asset life without transferring hidden risks to future budgets and users.

Fiscal scarcity makes project selection more important, not less

Nigeria's low public revenue and high interest burden constrain capital spending and the recurrent budgets that keep assets operational. The IMF's 2025 Article IV assessment emphasizes that interest spending absorbs fiscal space needed for infrastructure and human development and that credible budgeting and transparent implementation are essential to turn additional revenue into effective investment (International Monetary Fund, 2025). Under this condition, a long list of politically attractive projects is not an investment strategy. Spreading appropriations thinly across many sites increases the probability that contractors mobilize without predictable cash flow, work is suspended, completed components deteriorate, and costs are revised upward before benefits begin.

This failure mechanism is not new. Case studies of abandoned Nigerian water and irrigation projects identified inadequate funding, price variation, contract conditions, corruption, and project-management problems as interacting causes (Sonuga, Aliboh, & Oloke, 2002). The study is more than two decades old and cannot establish the current frequency of abandonment, but the mechanism remains relevant under inflation and exchange-rate volatility: an unrealistic cost estimate combined with annual incremental funding converts macroeconomic shocks into delay, renegotiation, and deteriorating partially built assets.

Project selection should therefore begin with a service gap and a realistic counterfactual. Appraisal must compare rehabilitation, demand management, maintenance, regulatory change, and smaller modular investments with a new megaproject. It should disclose total capital and operating cost, procurement and land schedule, foreign-currency content, climate and security exposure, demand uncertainty, distributional effects, and the public entity that will pay for operation. Independent technical review should occur before political commitment makes cancellation impossible. A project that cannot identify a funded operator and maintenance standard should not receive construction approval.

A PPP reallocates risk only when contracts and institutions can enforce the allocation

Public-private partnerships can mobilize expertise and long-term capital and can link payment to asset availability or service quality. Nigeria's Infrastructure Concession Regulatory Commission (ICRC) requires project identification, an Outline Business Case, procurement, a Full Business Case, implementation, monitoring, maturity, and handover. The process also calls for consultation with the finance ministry where a proposal creates a contingent liability (Infrastructure Concession Regulatory Commission, n.d.). This architecture correctly recognizes that government purchases a service rather than merely receives private money.

The difficult issue is credible risk allocation. A private operator can manage construction methods, routine maintenance, and some operating risks, but it cannot efficiently bear every exchange-rate shock, politically determined tariff freeze, land delay, force majeure, or demand collapse. Assigning an uncontrollable risk to the private side may simply raise the bid price, prevent financial close, or generate later renegotiation. Retaining too much risk through guarantees, minimum-revenue commitments, foreign-exchange protection, termination payments, or availability charges can make a PPP fiscally similar to debt while keeping the obligation less visible.

Comparative research on Nigeria and South Africa links PPP performance to financial-market depth, stable policy, legal credibility, transparent procurement, political support, stakeholder engagement, and public-sector capacity rather than to the contractual label alone (Deinde-Adedeji et al., 2024). For Nigeria, that finding implies that the PPP pipeline should not be used as a count of financed infrastructure. Government should publish the project stage, business-case summary, procurement outcome, signed contract, government support, currency and demand assumptions, performance reports, renegotiations, and termination or handback provisions. The finance ministry should report expected annual payments and probability-weighted contingent liabilities with debt and budget documents.

Affordability also determines legitimacy. A toll road, electricity concession, water service, or broadband project may be financially viable at a tariff that excludes poorer users or redirects traffic to unsafe alternatives. Subsidies can be justified where a service creates public or distributional benefits, but they should be explicit, targeted, budgeted, and reviewed. Hidden cross-subsidies and ad hoc tariff freezes weaken the revenue stream on which maintenance and private finance depend. The relevant test is value for money after financing cost, risk transfer, user charges, public support, and contract-management capacity—not whether initial capital appears in the private column.

Climate risk is already a fiscal and service-delivery risk

The 2022 floods demonstrate that resilience cannot remain a future scenario. A World Bank emergency-preparedness review estimates that the event affected about 3.2 million people, displaced 1.4 million, caused more than 600 deaths, and produced direct damages with a median estimate of US$6.7 billion, or about 1.6 percent of GDP. Its risk analysis estimates average annual flood damage to physical assets at US$693 million and a 1 percent annual probability that catastrophic flood damage exceeds US$10 billion—equivalent to 22.3 percent of public expenditure in the study's baseline (World Bank, 2025f). These are model- and assessment-based estimates with uncertainty, but they show why post-disaster reconstruction can crowd out planned development.

Climate exposure varies by place and asset. Coastal cities face sea-level rise, erosion, storm surge, subsidence, drainage blockage, and intense rainfall. Northern infrastructure faces heat, drought, water scarcity, and land degradation. Roads, rail, dams, power systems, telecom sites, schools, and health facilities have different failure thresholds and interdependencies. A flooded substation can interrupt water pumping and digital communications; a failed bridge can isolate markets and emergency services. Resilience therefore means maintaining an essential service under stress and recovering within a specified time, not making every structure equally resistant to every hazard.

Nigeria's Climate Change Act 2021 establishes a national council, action planning, carbon budgeting, and climate duties for public and private entities (Federal Republic of Nigeria, 2021). The legal framework creates authority for mainstreaming, but mainstreaming becomes real only when project appraisal, design standards, procurement, maintenance, and fiscal reporting incorporate hazard information. The 2026 Nigeria Country Climate and Development Report similarly frames resilient and low-emission development as an economy-wide institutional and financing task rather than an environmental-sector project list (World Bank, 2026b). An IMF assessment estimates annual adaptation needs rising toward roughly 3 percent of 2020 GDP by 2050 across agriculture, water, health, and transport and projected annual sea-level-rise costs of 0.1-0.4 percent of GDP (International Monetary Fund African Department, 2025). The estimates are scenario-dependent, but their order of magnitude makes prioritization and external concessional finance essential.

Every major appraisal should therefore include a geospatial hazard screen, asset vulnerability and criticality assessment, design alternatives under multiple climate scenarios, expected service interruption, residual risk, and a funded maintenance and emergency plan. The relevant comparison is lifecycle value: a resilient design may cost more initially but reduce expected damage, downtime, and reconstruction. Conversely, an expensive “climate” label is not justified when basic drainage maintenance, land-use enforcement, early warning, or a less exposed location would produce greater protection.

Maintenance is a budget institution and an information system

Maintenance failures are frequently attributed to insufficient funding, but information and incentives determine how scarce funds are used. Government needs a verified asset register that identifies location, owner, age, condition, replacement value, criticality, maintenance standard, contractor, and next inspection. Without it, ministries cannot calculate the liability embedded in existing infrastructure or distinguish preventive work from politically connected emergency repair. Capital budgets then add assets faster than recurrent systems can absorb them.

The budget should link each new asset to a multi-year operating and maintenance envelope and should protect high-value preventive tasks without creating unaccountable earmarks. Condition-based and performance-based contracts can pay for passability, uptime, water quality, energy availability, drainage capacity, or building safety rather than inputs alone. Their success still depends on independent measurement, auditable baselines, credible sanctions, and competition. Community reporting can identify localized failures, but it cannot replace engineering inspection or transfer statutory responsibility to residents.

Maintenance is also distributional. Wealthier firms and households can buy generators, water storage, private transport, cloud backup, or insurance when public systems fail. Poorer users face longer travel, lost work, spoiled food, interrupted schooling, unsafe water, and uncompensated disaster loss. Prioritization should therefore combine asset criticality with who depends on the service and who has alternatives. A low-cost feeder road, clinic power system, culvert, or drainage channel may have a higher welfare return than a visible expansion of a flagship facility.

Nigeria needs a single lifecycle accountability record for each major asset

The four preceding sections point to the same administrative reform. Electricity, logistics, digital services, and climate-resilient assets all fail at organizational interfaces and are often judged by installed stock rather than delivered service. Nigeria should create a lifecycle record for each major public or PPP asset, linked to procurement and budget systems but readable as a public service contract. It should identify the service objective, business-case baseline, capital and financing terms, public guarantees, construction milestones, climate assumptions, operator, maintenance schedule, condition, uptime or throughput, safety and environmental performance, user charges and subsidies, grievances, and ex-post evaluation.

Such a record would not eliminate political bargaining or uncertainty. It would make the bargaining more disciplined by showing which assumptions changed, who controls the corrective instrument, and what future obligation has already been created. Annual portfolio review could then stop or redesign weak projects before sunk-cost logic dominates, shift funds toward maintenance and completion, and aggregate currency, guarantee, climate, and operating risks across sectors. Climate finance and development-partner support would be most valuable when they strengthen this domestic pipeline—project preparation, hazard data, standards, procurement, monitoring, and maintenance—rather than funding stand-alone assets that cannot be sustained.

The chapter's overall implication is that Nigeria's infrastructure challenge is institutional as well as financial. More capital is necessary, but capital produces development only through reliable electricity, predictable journeys, usable and safe digital services, and assets that continue operating through fiscal and climate shocks. Financing, regulation, federal coordination, data, maintenance, and redress are not supporting details around construction. They are the mechanisms that determine whether an infrastructure promise becomes a public service.
