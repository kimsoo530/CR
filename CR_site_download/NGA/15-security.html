15.1 Foreign Policy, ECOWAS, AU, and Multilateral Positioning

Nigeria's foreign policy gives it a central role in ECOWAS, the African Union, peacekeeping, and West African diplomacy. Its population, economy, military, and cultural influence make it a regional anchor, but domestic security and fiscal constraints affect external leadership capacity.

15.2 Borders, Lake Chad, Gulf of Guinea, and Regional Security

Borders connect Nigeria to Sahelian insecurity, Lake Chad conflict, migration routes, trade corridors, and smuggling networks. The northeast faces Boko Haram and ISWAP dynamics; the northwest faces banditry and kidnapping; the Middle Belt faces farmer-herder and communal conflict; the Niger Delta faces oil theft and militancy risks; and the Gulf of Guinea involves piracy and maritime security.

15.3 Military, Police, Intelligence, and Internal Security Governance

Security governance is institutionally complex. The armed forces, Nigeria Police Force, intelligence agencies, civil defense, immigration, customs, state governments, local vigilante arrangements, and regional forces all interact. The constitutional centralization of policing sits uneasily with state-level demands for more localized security control.

15.4 Oil, Migration, Trafficking, Terrorism, and Strategic Geography

Strategic geography links security to oil, food systems, climate, migration, and infrastructure. A pipeline, road, port, grazing route, border market, or IDP camp can all become administrative pressure points. Nigeria's security chapter is therefore inseparable from public finance, local government, and social policy.

Military indicators do not capture the full security burden: Nigeria's internal security includes counterinsurgency, banditry, farmer-herder conflict, oil theft, piracy, policing, intelligence, and humanitarian displacement.
