8.1 Population Scale, Youth Structure, and Demographic Pressure

Nigeria's demographic scale is often described as either a development asset or a fiscal burden. Neither description is sufficient. The more defensible claim is conditional: a very large birth cohort can enlarge the future labour force, but only if fertility decline, child survival, learning, job creation, and administrative capacity change together. Otherwise, population momentum raises the number of children requiring education, health care, housing, and later employment faster than institutions can expand effective services. Nigeria's demographic challenge is therefore not simply that it is populous or young. It is that a slow and territorially unequal transition must be governed with a population denominator that remains unusually uncertain.

Scale without a recent population count

The World Bank's World Development Indicators (WDI) place Nigeria's 2024 population at 232.68 million, 46.78 million more than in 2014. Annual population growth slowed from 2.64 to 2.08 percent over the same period, while the share aged 0-14 fell from 44.4 to 41.0 percent. The working-age share consequently rose from 52.6 to 55.9 percent, and the young-age dependency ratio declined from 84.4 to 73.3 children per 100 people aged 15-64. These changes indicate an incipient age-structure transition, but not its completion: more than two in five residents are still children, and the absolute population expanded by roughly one-quarter in only a decade (World Bank, 2025a).

The status label in the table is substantively important. Nigeria's last completed national census was in 2006. The planned 2023 census was postponed, and the National Population Commission (NPC) was still describing the 2006 exercise as the latest national count in late 2025 (National Population Commission, 2023; National Population Commission, 2025). UN DESA therefore does not treat the 2024 figure as an observed headcount. Its World Population Prospects series reconciles the 1963, 1991, and 2006 censuses with adjustments for under- or over-count, age heaping, and under-enumeration of children, then incorporates survey and administrative evidence on fertility, mortality, and migration. For fertility specifically, the 2024 revision used successive DHS, MICS, MIS, and PMA surveys and several indirect estimation methods (United Nations DESA Population Division, 2024a; United Nations DESA Population Division, 2024b).

This does not make the WDI series unusable; it makes its appropriate use narrower. It is suitable for internationally comparable national trends, but it cannot substitute for a current enumeration when school catchments, primary-health staffing, constituency boundaries, disaster response, or intergovernmental transfers require small-area denominators. The problem is also institutional rather than purely technical. Research on Nigeria's census politics shows how population figures have been connected to political representation and revenue allocation, creating incentives and distrust around enumeration, and places the problem in a longer history of irregular and contested counts (Akinyoade et al., 2017; Obono & Omoluabi, 2014). These studies do not prove that every published number is manipulated. They explain why credible enumeration requires transparent cartography, independent post-enumeration checks, disclosure of adjustment rules, and protection of statistical operations from distributive bargaining.

Fertility decline is real, but Nigeria contains several transitions

The direction of change is clearer than any single point estimate. WDI/UN DESA estimates Nigeria's 2024 total fertility rate at 4.38 births per woman. The 2024 Nigeria Demographic and Health Survey (NDHS), which calculates age-specific rates from women's birth histories for the three years preceding the survey, reports 4.8. The two figures should not be used interchangeably: one is a harmonized model-based annual estimate and the other is a survey-period estimate. They nevertheless agree that fertility has declined while remaining high enough, together with a youthful age distribution, to sustain strong population momentum (National Population Commission and ICF, 2025; United Nations DESA Population Division, 2024a).

National averages conceal the main policy problem. The 2024 NDHS reports a total fertility rate of 3.9 in urban areas and 5.6 in rural areas. Across geopolitical zones it ranges from 3.3 in the South South to 6.1 in the North East, and across states from 2.9 in Rivers to 7.5 in Yobe. The educational gradient is similarly large: 6.3 births per woman among women with no education, compared with 3.0 among women with more than secondary education. The survey also reports 6.6 in the lowest household-wealth quintile and 3.3 in the highest (National Population Commission and ICF, 2025). These are descriptive associations, not isolated causal effects of residence, schooling, or income. Selection, age at marriage and first birth, child mortality, desired family size, access to contraception, and local norms can jointly generate the observed gaps.

The literature nevertheless helps identify plausible mechanisms. Analysis of the 2003-2013 DHS series found that regional, educational, wealth, marriage-timing, and family-planning variables explained distinct portions of subgroup fertility differences (Mberu & Reed, 2014). Those historical estimates should not replace the 2024 survey, but the mechanism remains consistent with the latest pattern. Wusu and Isiugo-Abanihe (2019) likewise found an inverse association between women's education and completed fertility in both northern and southern Nigeria, while warning that the magnitude differed across the north-south divide. Education can alter fertility through several pathways: it can delay marriage and first birth, increase access to information and paid work, strengthen bargaining power, and reduce the economic rationale for high fertility when child survival and expected returns to schooling improve. Because these pathways depend on school completion, quality, safety, reproductive-health access, and labour-market opportunity, simply expanding enrolment or distributing contraceptives cannot be assumed to produce the same effect everywhere.

Comparison with Ghana and Kenya clarifies the pace rather than assigning a rank. All three countries experienced declining child shares between 2014 and 2024, but Nigeria remained younger in 2024: the 0-14 share was 41.0 percent, compared with 35.8 percent in Ghana and 36.8 percent in Kenya. Nigeria's annual population growth was also higher at 2.08 percent, versus 1.88 and 1.96 percent respectively (World Bank, 2025a). Ghana is unitary, Kenya devolved, and Nigeria federal, so the comparison does not identify an institutional cause. It shows that demographic transition is not an automatic regional timetable and that Nigeria faces a larger near-term service-expansion requirement relative to its working-age population.

A demographic dividend must be produced institutionally

A falling dependency ratio creates an accounting opportunity, not a guaranteed dividend. When the working-age share rises, output and public revenue can grow faster than consumption needs if people are healthy, educated, productively employed, and able to save. If learning is weak, formal job creation is slow, women cannot enter paid work, and infrastructure constrains firms, the same cohort can experience prolonged underemployment and low productivity. Health, education, employment, and governance are therefore complementary conditions rather than automatic consequences of age-structure change (Reed & Mberu, 2014).

Recent Nigerian National Transfer Accounts research reinforces the need for a territorial perspective. Estimates produce different economic life-cycle and support-ratio profiles for Nigeria, Lagos, and Kaduna. In the model, Lagos entered the first-demographic-dividend phase earlier and generated a higher support ratio than Kaduna; the study also inferred much earlier labour income among children in Kaduna. The state cases cannot be generalized to the whole federation, and modelled dividend dates are not direct observations of welfare gains. Their value lies in demonstrating that fertility, schooling, labour participation, earnings, and intergenerational transfers combine differently within one country. A national dependency ratio can therefore improve while high-fertility, low-learning, or low-employment areas experience little dividend (Olasehinde et al., 2024).

Policy should follow those mechanisms. First, Nigeria needs a credible census and a durable population-statistics system, not only a one-off count. The next census should publish enumeration-area coverage, post-enumeration survey results, adjustment rules, and uncertainty, while civil registration, school, health, and social-protection records are progressively reconciled under clear privacy and correction procedures. The operational test is whether federal, state, and local planners can produce consistent age-sex denominators and explain revisions, not merely whether a national total is announced.

Second, fertility policy should be voluntary, rights-based, and territorially targeted. In places where high fertility coexists with low female school completion, early marriage, high child mortality, and weak primary care, the relevant package is continued schooling for girls, safe maternal and child health services, reliable access to a method mix of contraception, and removal of financial and geographic barriers. Performance should be judged by age at first birth, birth intervals, modern contraceptive demand satisfied, maternal and child survival, and school completion, disaggregated by state, residence, education, and wealth. A national contraceptive-distribution total would not reveal whether women can make and sustain informed choices.

Third, the emerging working-age cohort must be linked to productive demand. This requires measuring the transition from school to work—learning, technical skills, labour-force entry, employment quality, earnings, and female participation—rather than treating the rising 15-64 share as success. Federal policy can finance common data standards and equalizing investments, but states and local governments need differentiated service plans because Yobe, Rivers, Lagos, and Kaduna do not face the same fertility, schooling, or employment constraints. The demographic dividend will be visible only when declining dependency is accompanied by better capabilities, higher-productivity work, and narrower territorial gaps. Without those institutional conversions, Nigeria's population scale remains a demand on capacity rather than evidence of capacity itself.

8.2 Urbanization, Regional Distribution, and Metropolitan Governance

The demographic pressure identified in Section 8.1 becomes governable only when its spatial distribution is known. Nigeria's urban transition is no longer adequately described as migration into Lagos. It is a national reorganization of population, production, land, and public-service demand across a system of megacities, state capitals, commercial centres, and rapidly expanding peri-urban corridors. The newest World Development Indicators series estimates that 63.8 percent of Nigerians lived in urban areas in 2025, up from 55.7 percent in 2015. Yet this is not a new census count. The indicator applies national urban definitions to inputs collected and smoothed by the United Nations Population Division, and the series was updated in July 2026 (World Bank DataBank, 2026). A World Bank diagnostic completed with information available through June 2025 instead reported that 54 percent of the population—about 117 million people—was urban, using 2020-based sources, and identified roughly 1,200 urban agglomerations, including 18 with more than one million inhabitants (World Bank, 2025d). The difference is analytically important: Nigeria is clearly an urban-majority country, but the exact level depends on the reference year, definition, and model revision.

An urban majority whose size depends on measurement

Urbanization statistics combine at least three processes: movement from rural to urban settlements, natural increase within cities, and the reclassification or physical absorption of settlements at metropolitan edges. The *World Urbanization Prospects 2025* therefore publishes two parallel approaches. Its national-definition series preserves country-specific statistical practice, while its Degree of Urbanization classifies one-kilometre grid cells through common population-size, density, and contiguity thresholds. The latter is better suited to cross-country comparison; the former is more continuous with domestic administrative statistics. Neither should be presented as a direct substitute for a current Nigerian census. UN DESA warns that administrative criteria often diverge from actual settlement footprints and that sparse or old observations make subsequent projections less reliable (United Nations DESA Population Division, 2025).

For Nigeria, this qualification is not a technical footnote. As Section 8.1 showed, the last completed national census was conducted in 2006. A smoothly rising annual urban share can therefore give a misleading impression of annual observation. It is best read as a modelled trajectory showing the scale and direction of change, not as proof that precisely 63.8 percent of residents were enumerated as urban in 2025 (Akinyoade et al., 2017; Obono & Omoluabi, 2014; World Bank DataBank, 2026). The policy denominator should consequently be triangulated: national-definition estimates for continuity, harmonized settlement grids for functional urban footprints, and administrative records for actual service loads. School enrolment, waste collection, transport ridership, building permits, utility connections, and geocoded property records can reveal where demand is growing even before a new census resolves the population baseline.

Regional distribution further complicates a single national rate. Lagos is the country's principal commercial metropolis; Abuja is a federally governed capital region; Kano anchors a northern commercial system; Port Harcourt is shaped by the oil economy and delta ecology; and Ibadan, Kaduna, Benin City, Jos, Maiduguri, Onitsha, Aba, and many state capitals face different combinations of growth, insecurity, informality, and fiscal capacity. The World Bank's 2025 review covered 11 major cities across all six geopolitical zones and found that the four largest—Lagos, Kano, Ibadan, and Abuja—had experienced similar stagnation in real GDP per capita despite very different spatial and institutional settings. It also estimated that 11 cities generated about one-third of national GDP, with Lagos, Onitsha, and Kano contributing 14.5, 4.5, and 4.4 percent, respectively, on the report's 2021 economic data (World Bank, 2025d). Urban concentration therefore creates economic value without automatically producing productivity growth or livability.

Functional cities have outgrown governmental boundaries

The central institutional problem is a mismatch between functional urban regions and Nigeria's governmental map. The Constitution recognizes federal, state, and local governments, but not a general-purpose city or metropolitan tier. The result is what the World Bank earlier called a “missing” city level: urban land, mobility, housing, drainage, waste, and infrastructure networks cross local-government boundaries, while authority and finance remain divided among federal ministries, state agencies, local government areas (LGAs), and utilities. Outside the unusual case in which a metropolitan footprint broadly coincides with a state, no government necessarily owns the complete service chain (World Bank, 2016).

Lagos demonstrates both the costs of this mismatch and the possibility of selective institutional repair. Its built-up area expanded by an estimated 2.6 percent per year after 2000 and now extends into neighbouring Ogun State, so commuting, housing markets, watersheds, and logistics operate across a state boundary (World Bank, 2025d). Formal planning, however, remains territorially divided. Within Lagos State, the 2010 Urban and Regional Planning and Development Law assigns plan preparation, permitting, urban renewal, and building control to distinct state bodies (Lagos State Government, 2010). Land administration is additionally shaped by the Land Use Act, under which state governors control urban land and local governments have authority over non-urban land, making the urban classification itself consequential for access, title, compensation, and development approval (Federal Republic of Nigeria, 1978).

Sector-specific authorities can reduce fragmentation when their mandate, finance, and operational data cover the relevant network. The Lagos Metropolitan Area Transport Authority (LAMATA), established in 2002 and strengthened in 2007, was given responsibility for transport planning and coordination across modes, management of a declared road network, and a dedicated Transport Fund (Lagos Metropolitan Area Transport Authority, 2026). This is more than an organizational reform: it aligns a functional transport system with a lead agency and a recurrent financing mechanism. Evaluations of the Lagos urban transport programme emphasize that political backing, technical staffing, clear assignment of functions, and coordination with other agencies were essential to implementation (World Bank Independent Evaluation Group, 2016). The lesson is not that every service should be placed in one super-agency. The World Bank's 2025 diagnostic found that Lagos's transport and waste authorities still worked in silos, illustrating that sectoral coherence can coexist with cross-sector fragmentation (World Bank, 2025d).

The governance problem also cannot be reduced to a neutral shortage of administrative capacity. Fourchard's historical analysis shows that infrastructure allocation and taxation in Lagos have been shaped by federal-state rivalry and political networks rather than by an autonomous informal order outside the state (Fourchard, 2011). Badiora's 2020 survey of 362 participants in metropolitan Lagos similarly found that perceived procedural fairness and the conduct of planning officers were strongly associated with the legitimacy of planning authorities and willingness to comply with planning law (Badiora, 2020). Recent studies extend the diagnosis beyond Lagos. Survey research in Sokoto, Akure, Calabar, and Umuahia links infrastructure deficits to short political time horizons, state-local fiscal dependence, and uneven political allocation (Essien & Jesse, 2025). Research on Lagos enclaves shows how households and developers respond to unreliable collective systems by privately supplying electricity, water, transport, sewage, or security, producing “splintered” access rather than a citywide solution (Titilayo, 2025). Informality is therefore partly an institutional outcome: when public rules are costly, selective, or unpredictable, exit and self-provision can be rational even though they deepen territorial inequality.

Metropolitan coordination must connect authority, finance, and accountability

Policy should begin with the function to be coordinated, not with a uniform metropolitan template. Transport and drainage require network-wide planning; land-use control must align permits with infrastructure capacity; waste systems need collection, transfer, and disposal across jurisdictions; and housing policy must connect tenure, serviced land, mobility, and livelihoods. In each domain, a credible arrangement needs five elements: a legally defined metropolitan footprint, an identified lead body, an assignment matrix across state agencies and LGAs, a recurrent revenue source, and public performance indicators. Creating another authority without these elements would add an organizational layer while leaving the coordination failure intact.

The spatial scale must also be revisable. Lagos-Ogun commuting and development already exceed the boundary of Lagos State, whereas Jos, Maiduguri, and many secondary-city regions combine dense cores with several LGAs and rural or displacement-affected peripheries. Cross-state metropolitan compacts, joint planning boards, or corridor agreements may therefore be more feasible than constitutional creation of a new tier. Federal grants can make coordination incentive-compatible by conditioning capital finance on an agreed functional-area map, integrated land-and-transport plans, interoperable geospatial data, asset-maintenance budgets, and disclosure of service outcomes. State governments would retain constitutional authority, while LGAs and affected communities would gain specified decision and review rights rather than being consulted only after projects are designed.

Finance and accountability are the binding conditions. The 2024 Supreme Court judgment on local-government allocations may reduce one channel of state control, but direct transfers alone do not create metropolitan competence, own-source revenue, or joint asset management (Federal Republic of Nigeria, 2024). Conversely, strong state agencies can deliver network projects while bypassing neighbourhood priorities. Olajide and Lawanson's study of forced-eviction conflicts in Lagos shows that grassroots coalitions can widen participation but that invited consultation and autonomous mobilization are both constrained by the politics of redevelopment (Olajide & Lawanson, 2025). Metropolitan governance must therefore measure distribution as well as aggregate output: travel time and affordability by income and sex; serviced plots and tenure security; waste and drainage coverage by neighbourhood; permit processing and enforcement outcomes; involuntary displacement and remedy; and maintenance condition after project completion.

Nigeria's urban transition should thus be understood as a test of intergovernmental design. The policy objective is not simply to slow city growth or to reproduce Lagos institutions everywhere. It is to make governmental responsibility correspond more closely to the functional geography of each urban system while preserving local voice and making finance traceable. Progress would be visible when integrated plans govern actual investment decisions, cross-boundary services have durable operating budgets, administrative and geospatial denominators are reconciled, and poorer peripheral settlements receive measurable service improvements rather than bearing only the costs of metropolitan expansion.

8.3 Ethnicity, Language, Religion, and National Integration

Population growth and urban mobility do not merely redistribute service demand; they place people with different origins and identities under the same institutions. Nigeria's diversity is not, by itself, an explanation for political instability or weak national integration. The more consequential question is how public institutions translate identities into access to office, education, services, land, and protection. Nigeria has developed unusually explicit mechanisms for territorial inclusion: a multi-state federation, distributive electoral rules, the federal-character principle, state creation, and formal and informal power-sharing. These arrangements have helped make domination by a single group more difficult. Yet they also attach opportunity to state of origin and indigene status, creating a tension between representation of territorial communities and equal citizenship for mobile residents. National integration should therefore be evaluated not as cultural uniformity, but as the capacity of Nigerians to obtain fair treatment and effective remedies regardless of ancestry, language, religion, residence, or political connection.

Territorial inclusion protects against capture but can reproduce exclusion

The constitutional design begins from a defensible objective. The federal-character clauses require the composition and conduct of federal institutions to prevent the predominance of only a few states, ethnic groups, or sectional interests and apply a parallel principle within states and local governments. Adjacent national-integration provisions encourage mobility, intermarriage, cross-cutting associations, and loyalty to the nation, while the fundamental-rights chapter prohibits discrimination on grounds including ethnic group, place of origin, sex, religion, and political opinion (Federal Republic of Nigeria, 1999/2023). Federal character is thus not merely an ethnic quota. It is a constitutional response to fears of domination in a federation whose political opportunities and revenues have long been territorially contested.

The Federal Character Commission's distribution formula makes this response administratively concrete. In its 1997 guidelines, the Commission states that each state should ordinarily account for 2.75 percent of positions in federal bodies and that the Federal Capital Territory should account for 1 percent, while reserving vacancies initially for qualified candidates from the relevant state and then allowing zonal or national fallback when they cannot be filled (Federal Character Commission, 1997). The formula creates a visible claim to inclusion and a peaceful channel through which under-representation can be contested. Mustapha's study accordingly concludes that the Commission did not achieve its formal objectives but nevertheless helped channel ethnic grievances into non-violent institutional processes (Mustapha, 2009). The mechanism is political reassurance: a group that can observe some presence in national institutions has less reason to interpret every appointment as permanent exclusion.

Formal allocation, however, is not the same as substantive equality. Using Federal Character Commission records for 2003–2013 and interviews, Demarest, Langer, and Ukiwo found persistent representation imbalances, limited enforcement, inadequate resources, political dependence, and weak access to the personnel information needed for monitoring. They nevertheless judged that the system may have constrained capture by a single group and retained symbolic value (Demarest et al., 2020). The appropriate conclusion is neither that federal character has failed completely nor that quotas have solved horizontal inequality. It is that territorial balancing supplies a floor of recognition but cannot compensate by itself for unequal schooling, uneven recruitment pipelines, discretionary appointments, slow staff turnover, or opaque personnel data.

The deeper contradiction appears when territorial representation is administered through ancestry. The Constitution's interpretation clause defines belonging to a state by whether a person's parents or grandparents were indigenous to a community in that state (Federal Republic of Nigeria, 1999/2023). In practice, state and local institutions often use certificates of indigeneity or state of origin to allocate employment, scholarships, admission, fees, land, or political eligibility. Kraxberger describes how state creation and this exclusionary geography produced subnational containers of citizenship in which long residence does not necessarily convert a Nigerian into a local rights-holder (Kraxberger, 2005). Matfess similarly argues that ambiguous indigeneity rules interact with land tenure and centralized security institutions, complicating both conflict settlement and the reintegration of displaced people (Matfess, 2016). A rule intended to protect minorities within the federation can therefore create minorities within each state.

Survey evidence links institutional treatment to belonging more directly than lists of ethnic groups can. Tuki's analysis of Afrobarometer Rounds 7 and 8 uses two nationally sampled surveys of 1,600 Nigerian adults each, conducted in 2017 and 2020. In Round 8, 35 percent reported ethnic discrimination at least once during the previous year. Ordered-logit results associate such experience with placing greater weight on ethnic than national identity, and the association grows with reported frequency; the observational design does not establish that discrimination alone caused identity change (Tuki, 2024). The policy implication is precise: national integration is weakened when citizens experience unequal treatment, not simply when they retain ethnic identities.

Reform should preserve protection against domination while reducing ancestry-based exclusion. The Commission should publish appointment and workforce distributions by state, geopolitical zone, grade, cadre, sex, recruitment cohort, and qualification, linked to auditable personnel records rather than occasional establishment returns. Compliance assessment should separate the representation outcome from the integrity of the recruitment process: a numerically balanced workforce can still be patronage-driven, while an initial shortfall may reflect a documented skills pipeline rather than deliberate exclusion. For state and local benefits unrelated to cultural or traditional office, residence-based eligibility with a defined transition period and an appealable administrative decision would better align mobility with equal citizenship. Federal legislation and judicial review are important because no single state has a strong incentive to abandon origin rules while other states retain them.

Language policy exposes the difference between national rules and local capacity

Language presents the same inclusion problem in another form. English is a cross-regional language of federal administration, higher education, national examinations, and economic mobility, but exclusive reliance on it can disadvantage children and citizens whose strongest language is local. Conversely, a single nationwide mother-tongue rule is difficult to implement where classrooms are multilingual, teachers are not deployed by language competence, and reading materials are unevenly available. The choice is therefore not a simple contest between English and indigenous languages. It is an administrative-design question about which language is used for which function, at what stage, with what trained staff and materials, and how learners move between languages.

Obiakor's analysis of Nigeria's 2020–2021 Multiple Indicator Cluster Survey documents the implementation gap. About 81 percent of sampled children were reported as being taught in English, including 95.4 percent in private schools and 73 percent in public schools; English-medium exposure was also more common in urban and southern settings. Children taught in English had higher measured literacy achievement after regression and propensity-score adjustments, but these observational comparisons cannot establish that English itself caused the advantage. School resources, parental selection, urban location, teacher quality, and the mismatch between the language of assessment and instruction remain plausible explanations (Obiakor, 2024). Treating the result as proof that indigenous-language instruction is inherently inferior would convert an implementation inequality into a pedagogical conclusion.

Policy changed sharply in November 2025. The National Council on Education reaffirmed English as the medium of instruction from primary through tertiary education and discontinued the earlier policy prescribing mother-tongue instruction in Primary 1–3. The ministry attributed the reversal to a setting it described as having more than 500 indigenous languages, insufficient materials and teacher preparation, weak transition to English, and uneven implementation across states. It simultaneously announced an Inclusive Language Policy encouraging the learning of at least one Nigerian language alongside English (Federal Ministry of Information, 2025). This is an official account of the decision and its rationale, not independent evidence that the earlier policy caused poor examination results.

A more credible implementation strategy would avoid another unsupported national switch. States and local education authorities need school-level language maps, teacher-language inventories, material availability records, and literacy results disaggregated by home language, medium of instruction, school sector, location, sex, and disability. Bilingual transition models can then be piloted where language concentration and teacher capacity make them feasible, with common assessments that distinguish comprehension from English proficiency. In highly multilingual urban schools, structured English support combined with Nigerian-language learning may be more workable than assigning one presumed mother tongue. The performance test is whether children acquire foundational literacy and a usable bridge to later instruction—not whether a nominal policy appears in a circular.

Religious accommodation is most durable when bounded by equal citizenship

Nigeria's constitutional settlement combines a prohibition on adopting a state religion with freedom of religion and a federal judiciary that includes Sharia Courts of Appeal for specified civil matters (Federal Republic of Nigeria, 1999/2023). After Zamfara expanded Sharia criminal law in 1999, eleven other northern states followed. Suberu's comparative analysis argues that conflict over Sharia was contained through a combination of state-level autonomy and federal constraints rather than by abolishing religious difference: subnational governments could respond to local constituencies, while federal courts, rights guarantees, intergovernmental bargaining, and political restraint limited escalation (Suberu, 2009). This illustrates conditional accommodation, not a claim that the arrangement is uniformly equitable or that religion has ceased to be politically contentious.

Nor should violence labelled “religious” be treated as evidence that doctrine alone caused it. In many localities, religious affiliation overlaps with indigene–settler status, access to land and office, livelihood competition, policing, and elite mobilization. Bunte and Vinson's 2016 mixed-method study of 38 Nigerian districts finds lower interreligious violence where informal local power-sharing agreements existed. Interviews and subnational data point to two mechanisms: such arrangements gave elites stronger incentives to use conciliatory rhetoric and reduced residents' perception that religious diversity itself was threatening (Bunte & Vinson, 2016). The study supports attention to local institutions, but it does not imply that informal bargains substitute for legal rights, professional security, or accountability; closed elite pacts can also exclude women, youth, smaller communities, and residents classified as settlers.

Policy should therefore connect accommodation to enforceable equality. Local peace arrangements should specify representation, decision rules, early-warning and mediation procedures, referral to police and courts, and public reporting of incidents and remedies. Government should track whether protection, prosecution, compensation, school access, public employment, and reconstruction differ by religion, origin status, sex, or locality. Where disputes involve land or political office, those distributive questions must be adjudicated directly rather than redescribed as timeless religious hostility. Where religious or customary authority exercises public functions, its decisions should remain reviewable under constitutional rights.

The common lesson across federal character, indigeneity, language, and religion is that national integration cannot be manufactured by suppressing difference or by mechanically allocating identities. It grows when institutions make inclusion credible, convert mobility into rights, implement language policy with actual capability, and provide equal protection and remedy. Nigeria's balancing institutions remain valuable because they recognize fears of domination; their next stage must be to measure and reduce the unequal treatment that makes those fears persist.

8.4 Migration, Remittances, Diaspora, and Displacement

The preceding sections examined demographic scale, spatial redistribution, and the rights attached to place and identity. Migration brings those processes together by moving people, skills, claims, and resources across local, state, and national boundaries. Nigeria is simultaneously a country of emigration, immigration, regional circulation, return, refugee reception, and large-scale internal displacement. These categories should not be collapsed. An emigrant stock counts people from Nigeria living abroad at a point in time; a diaspora may also include descendants who maintain social or economic ties; a remittance is a balance-of-payments flow rather than a headcount; refugees have crossed an international border; and internally displaced persons (IDPs) remain within Nigeria. Each measure describes a different population, reference period, and administrative responsibility. Policy becomes incoherent when a large diaspora estimate is treated as the number of current emigrants, when remittances are equated with public revenue, or when camp closure is counted as proof that displacement has ended.

Emigration creates transnational capability and domestic sectoral losses

The United Nations' 2024 international migrant-stock matrix estimates that about 2.094 million people originating from Nigeria lived in another country. The United States accounted for approximately 565,000, the United Kingdom 324,000, Niger 200,000, and Cameroon 188,000. The pattern is both intercontinental and regional: the two largest Anglophone high-income destinations held about 42.5 percent of the estimated stock, while neighbouring Niger and Cameroon together held about 18.5 percent (Migration Policy Institute, 2026; United Nations Population Division, 2025). These are estimated stocks based primarily on country of birth or, where necessary, citizenship. They are not annual departures, do not measure irregular movements well, and exclude many descendants whom Nigeria's 2021 National Diaspora Policy includes when they maintain links and an interest in contributing to the country (Nigerians in Diaspora Commission, 2021).

This distinction matters because migration's effects depend on who moves, where, for how long, and whether capabilities circulate back. International mobility can raise household income, extend commercial and professional networks, and transfer knowledge. It can also remove scarce workers from services whose training was publicly or collectively financed. The tension is especially visible in health. OECD and WHO data for circa 2020–2021 counted about 17,060 Nigerian-born doctors and 54,480 Nigerian-born nurses working in OECD countries; relative to 2000–2001, those stocks had increased by 264 and 295 percent, respectively (OECD, 2025). Country of birth is not the same as place of professional training, and the data omit non-OECD destinations, so the figures do not measure Nigeria's exact training loss. They nevertheless show that Nigerian health personnel form a substantial component of international labour markets while Nigeria remains on the WHO Health Workforce Support and Safeguards List.

Calling this process either “brain drain” or “brain gain” is too categorical. The domestic effect depends on the balance among training capacity, vacancies, wages, working conditions, professional development, geographic deployment, return, and knowledge exchange. A doctor who trains abroad and collaborates with a Nigerian institution differs from a publicly trained clinician leaving an already understaffed rural facility; a nurse recorded as Nigerian-born may have received all professional education in the destination country. Nnabuihe and colleagues document financial and intellectual contributions by Nigerian diaspora networks, including academic exchange and capacity-building, but their cases do not demonstrate that such engagement offsets losses in every sending sector (Nnabuihe et al., 2024).

The policy response should therefore be sector-specific rather than restrictive. Health-workforce planning should link professional registration, training output, public and private employment, vacancies, exits, return, and subnational deployment. Bilateral recruitment agreements should implement the WHO code through destination-country support for additional training capacity, faculty, equipment, and temporary or circular placements, while protecting workers' freedom and employment rights. Domestic retention requires reliable pay, safe workplaces, career progression, training opportunities, and functional facilities; compulsory service or exit barriers cannot repair those conditions. For the wider diaspora, the Nigerians in Diaspora Commission can broker verified professional networks, research partnerships, investment facilitation, and short-term service arrangements, but these programmes need outcome measures—staff retained, courses delivered, ventures surviving, patents or protocols adopted—not only participant counts or pledged funds.

Remittances insure households but are not a substitute for public provision

The World Bank's current World Development Indicators series records US$22.79 billion in personal remittances received in 2025, compared with US$22.13 billion in 2024, US$19.55 billion in 2023, and US$23.81 billion in 2019 (World Bank Remittance DataBank, 2026). The indicator combines personal transfers between resident and non-resident households with compensation earned by border, seasonal, and other short-term workers, following the IMF balance-of-payments definition. It is reported in current US dollars and built from IMF balance-of-payments data and World Bank staff estimates. It therefore neither captures every informal transfer nor identifies how much came from members of the wider Nigerian diaspora.

The series shows scale and volatility, not a steadily rising development fund. Exchange-rate changes can alter the current-dollar value and the naira purchasing power in opposite directions; policy changes can shift transfers between formal and informal channels; and the GDP ratio can jump when the exchange-rate conversion or national-accounts denominator changes. The 2025 value was below the 2019 nominal-dollar level even though it represented a much larger share of the contemporaneous dollar-denominated GDP series. For this reason, remittances should not be compared with tax revenue or foreign investment without aligning definitions, currency, and denominator (World Bank Remittance DataBank, 2026).

At household level, the strongest claim is insurance and consumption support, not automatic structural transformation. Bang, Mitra, and Wunnava use the 2009 Nigerian Migration Household Survey and instrumental-variable quantile regression to find positive expenditure effects across most of the conditional distribution and poverty reduction, but also a non-uniform distributional pattern that may “hollow out” parts of the middle. The older survey and identifying assumptions limit application to current Nigeria, yet the analysis demonstrates why aggregate inflows cannot reveal who receives transfers or how gains are distributed (Bang et al., 2022). Nnabuihe and colleagues similarly identify household support and knowledge transfer, but their mixed evidence does not establish that private remittances finance public goods at scale (Nnabuihe et al., 2024).

Policy should reduce the friction and risk of private transfers without appropriating them as state finance. The World Bank's corridor data show that prices vary substantially by sending country, provider, payment instrument, access point, transfer size, and exchange-rate margin; a single “Nigeria remittance cost” can therefore be misleading (World Bank Remittance Prices Worldwide, 2026). Regulators should publish comparable corridor-level total costs, license and supervise providers proportionately, make customer identity and payment systems interoperable, and permit transparent competition in payout channels. Household surveys should identify receipt, frequency, channel, origin country, sex of sender and recipient, location, and use while protecting privacy. Productive-investment instruments, including diaspora bonds or pooled vehicles, require clear use-of-proceeds rules, independent reporting, currency-risk disclosure, and ordinary investor protection. They should be voluntary financial products, not a policy assumption that migrant households owe the state development finance.

Durable solutions require rights, services, livelihoods, and host-community capacity

Forced displacement is a different administrative problem from labour migration. The UNHCR Nigeria operational portal reported 3,711,314 IDPs and 140,362 refugees and asylum-seekers hosted in Nigeria on 1 July 2026, drawing on government, IOM, NEMA, and UNHCR sources (UNHCR Operational Data Portal, 2026). The IDP number aggregates assessments in the north-east and north-central/north-west at different collection rounds; it is an operational stock for covered areas, not a simultaneous national census. Refugees and asylum-seekers in Nigeria are also distinct from Nigerian refugees abroad. These qualifications are essential because displacement estimates change with access, registration, return, repeated movement, disasters, and the boundary between an IDP, returnee, and resident member of a host community.

The causes and effects are territorially heterogeneous. Insurgency has generated protracted displacement in the north-east; banditry and communal violence are central in parts of the north-west and north-central zones; floods and other hazards produce sudden and sometimes repeated movements; and Nigeria also hosts refugees, predominantly from neighbouring countries. Displacement is not only a humanitarian stock. It is a shock to land access, schools, clinics, housing, food markets, documentation, policing, and local public finance in places that often had weak services before newcomers arrived.

George and Adelaja combine Nigeria's General Household Survey food-consumption module with IOM displacement data and find that IDP inflows were associated with worse food-security outcomes in host communities, with the adverse effect concentrated in insurgency-driven displacement. They attribute the pattern to an immediate demand shock, rigid food production and distribution, access constraints, and slower humanitarian response; disaster- and communal-clash displacement did not show the same significant effect in their models (George & Adelaja, 2022). The result should not be read as evidence that displaced people cause insecurity. It shows that an unfinanced population shock can burden a local food system and that the cause of displacement changes agencies' ability to respond.

Nigeria's National Policy on Internally Displaced Persons recognizes conflict, disasters, and other causes and assigns responsibilities for prevention, protection, assistance, and durable solutions (Federal Ministry of Humanitarian Affairs, 2021). In 2024, Borno, Adamawa, and Yobe launched state action plans that define return, local integration, or relocation as possible pathways and shift attention from short-term needs to area-based development covering IDPs, returnees, and host communities (United Nations Nigeria, 2024). This is an important institutional advance, but a plan or physical departure from a camp is not itself a durable solution. UNHCR reported that more than 23,000 Nigerian refugees returned from Chad and Niger in 2025 to areas without an agency presence or verified return intention, demonstrating why a return count must not be equated with voluntary, safe, and sustainable reintegration (United Nations High Commissioner for Refugees, 2026).

Implementation should be tested against outcomes shared by displaced and host populations. A state action plan needs a geocoded population and service baseline, an assignment matrix among federal, state, and local agencies, a costed multiyear budget, and grievance and monitoring arrangements in which displaced people participate. Return, local integration, and relocation decisions should be voluntary and informed; documentation, tenure or land access, physical security, school enrolment, health coverage, livelihoods, and freedom of movement should be measured after assistance ends. Financing should follow the affected area rather than camp status alone, so schools, clinics, water systems, markets, and food supply expand for hosts as well as newcomers. Early warning and contingency finance are especially important where insurgency or flooding can produce repeated displacement.

Across international mobility, remittances, diaspora engagement, and forced displacement, Nigeria's challenge is not to stop movement. It is to build institutions that distinguish categories, protect rights, preserve essential domestic capabilities, lower transaction costs, and finance receiving communities. Success would be visible not in a larger remittance headline or fewer camps, but in better workforce retention and circulation, safer and cheaper transfers, reliable migration data, voluntary solutions, and restored access to services and livelihoods. Taken together, this chapter shows that population scale becomes a policy outcome only through place, membership, and movement: credible denominators identify people, territorially matched institutions organize services, equal citizenship governs belonging, and mobility systems determine whether capabilities and protection travel with them.

Nigeria's population scale is itself an administrative fact: every policy is implemented in a country larger than most regional systems, with strong regional variation in poverty, schooling, fertility, urbanization, and security.

The age structure turns education, jobs, and urban services into core state-capacity tests. The youth share can support growth only if federal and state systems deliver schooling, public health, power, and labor-market access.
