2.1 Precolonial Polities and Political Diversity

There was no single precolonial Nigerian administrative model. The territory later joined as Nigeria contained the Sokoto Caliphate and Hausa emirates, Kanem–Bornu traditions, Yoruba kingdoms and city-states, the Benin kingdom, segmentary and republican communities in much of the southeast, Niger Delta trading polities, and numerous other political orders. They varied not only in centralization but also in how rulers were selected, taxes collected, land allocated, disputes settled, markets regulated, and military or religious authority constrained. Calling all of these arrangements “traditional authority” collapses the institutional variation that later governments had to confront.

Obikili (2018) frames precolonial state formation in terms of political organization and its relationship to later development, while comparative work by Cappelen and Sorens (2018) argues that the interaction between precolonial centralization and the form of colonial rule matters more than either condition alone. The important implication is not that centralized societies were inherently better governed. Rather, centralized authority gave colonial officials an identifiable intermediary with whom they could bargain. Where power was dispersed, efforts to manufacture a chief could generate an institution with limited social legitimacy. The effects therefore depended on whether the imposed administrative arrangement cohered with local authority.

This distinction clarifies why local governance became territorially uneven. In parts of the north, emirate institutions already combined taxation, adjudication, and religious authority, although colonial rule transformed their powers and subordinated them to imperial objectives. In many southeastern communities, authority was distributed across lineages, assemblies, age grades, title societies, and other institutions; warrant chiefs installed for administrative convenience could not simply reproduce an absent hierarchy. Yoruba and Niger Delta polities exhibited still other mixtures of monarchy, councils, commerce, and local autonomy. These are stylized contrasts, not fixed ethnic essences, and internal variation was substantial.

Three contemporary mechanisms can plausibly carry this history forward. First, land and dispute-resolution systems may continue to combine statutory and customary authority. Second, officials may need different forms of consultation and brokerage across localities. Third, citizens' expectations of legitimate public authority may differ according to the institutions through which taxation, justice, and protection were historically experienced. None of these mechanisms implies that present outcomes are predetermined; migration, urbanization, constitutional reform, markets, religious change, and party politics have repeatedly altered local authority.

The administrative lesson is therefore conditional. Uniform laws can protect equal citizenship, but uniform implementation templates may fail when they assume the same social organization everywhere. Land registration, community policing, primary health, school management, and conflict mediation should begin with institutional mapping: who actually exercises authority, what legal basis that authority has, which groups are excluded, and how decisions can be appealed. Incorporating local institutions without safeguards can entrench unaccountable elites; bypassing them entirely can weaken compliance and information. Effective policy needs a transparent interface between elected government, professional administration, customary institutions, and citizens.

For the rest of this history, precolonial diversity supplies an initial condition rather than a complete explanation. Colonial indirect rule selected, altered, or invented intermediaries; regional federalism converted territorial differences into political competition; and postcolonial governments reorganized states and revenues. The question is not whether precolonial institutions survived unchanged. It is how later regimes reused and transformed them, leaving different pathways through which Nigerians encounter the state.

2.2 Colonial Amalgamation and Regional Federalism

The 1914 amalgamation joined the Northern and Southern protectorates under one colonial administration, but it did not create the mature federal bargain that Nigeria inherited at independence. It centralized ultimate imperial authority while preserving different administrative histories and uneven systems of taxation, education, law, and local rule. Afigbo (1991) consequently locates the background of Nigerian federalism not simply in ethnic plurality but in the distinct colonial administrative traditions that hardened rivalry between North and South. This distinction matters: amalgamation explains the territorial container, while later constitutional change explains how political competition became regionalized.

Indirect rule was a governing practice, not a uniform transfer of authentic tradition. In Kano, for example, Stilwell (2011) shows that emirs and African political elites actively shaped the everyday construction of colonial power rather than merely implementing British commands. Their room for maneuver existed within a coercive imperial hierarchy that redefined legitimate authority. In less centralized settings, colonial officials appointed or elevated intermediaries whose authority could be contested. The result was a differentiated state–local interface: similar administrative labels concealed different relationships among officials, chiefs, courts, taxpayers, and communities.

The constitutional sequence after the Second World War gradually converted these differences into a regional federation. The Richards Constitution of 1946 reinforced regional organization; the Macpherson Constitution of 1951 expanded regional institutions and participation; and the Lyttleton Constitution of 1954 established a more explicit federal division of powers. By independence in 1960, political parties and governing coalitions were strongly rooted in the Northern, Western, and Eastern regions. Falola (2024) emphasizes this evolution from colonial constitutions toward federalism, while Afigbo (1991) links regional administrative traditions to the rivalry that became embedded in postcolonial politics.

Regionalism brought both accountability and risk. Regional governments could adapt education, agriculture, local administration, and development programs to their political constituencies. Yet population counts, revenue allocation, minority protection, and control of the federal center became high-stakes disputes because a region's demographic and fiscal position affected national power. Export infrastructure also followed colonial commercial priorities: railways, ports, and administrative investment connected production zones to external markets unevenly. The colonial state thus left a development geography as well as a constitutional structure.

It would be too strong to claim that colonialism mechanically caused every later conflict. Post-independence leaders, military regimes, oil markets, and constitutional choices had independent effects. A more defensible mechanism is institutional path dependence: colonial boundaries and regional institutions structured the organizations, revenue claims, and political identities through which later actors competed. Evidence that the same regional cleavages were mobilized after independence supports continuity, but it does not prove that alternative choices were impossible.

The policy implication is that institutional reform must address both formal assignments and historically uneven interfaces. Local-government legislation cannot assume that customary authority has the same foundation across states. Infrastructure policy should examine whether inherited corridors continue to disadvantage interior markets. National education or judicial standards need room for implementation support without reproducing unequal citizenship. Most importantly, constitutional debate should distinguish territorial inclusion from administrative effectiveness. Regional representation may protect groups, while transparent functional assignments, fiscal responsibility, and appeal rights are needed to make government work. Nigeria's colonial history explains why these objectives became intertwined; it does not require that they remain institutionally fused.

2.3 Independence, Civil War, Military Rule, and the Fourth Republic

Independence in 1960 transferred authority to a parliamentary federation whose principal parties remained regionally anchored. Disputes over censuses, elections, minority security, and the allocation of federal power intensified until coups in 1966 ended the First Republic. Secession by the Eastern Region as Biafra produced the 1967–1970 civil war. The federal government created 12 states in 1967, both weakening the old regions and responding to minority claims. Later military governments expanded the number to 19 states in 1976, 21 in 1987, 30 in 1991, and 36 in 1996. State creation was therefore simultaneously a nation-building device, a redistribution of offices, and a reorganization of access to federal revenue (Osaghae, 2018; Suberu, 2022).

Military rule altered the balance within the federation. Command structures, federal control of petroleum revenues, nationwide bureaucratic expansion, and centrally designed reforms strengthened the center. Yet centralization was not invented solely by soldiers: Suberu's (2022) systematic account of de/centralization from 1954 to 2020 finds roots in the unitary colonial foundation and variation across policy domains and civilian–military cycles. “Soldiers and oil” remain important, but they are an incomplete causal shorthand.

Oil changed the incentives of intergovernmental administration. As petroleum displaced regionally based export taxes and royalties, political competition increasingly concerned the distribution of centrally collected revenue. Fiscal scholarship on Nigeria has long identified the tension between derivation, equality, population, need, and national control in allocation formulas (Phillips, 1991). A transfer-funded state may provide national redistribution, but it can also separate expenditure authority from local revenue mobilization. The effect is not automatic: outcomes depend on formula design, transparency, audit, and whether citizens can identify the government responsible for a service.

The Fourth Republic began in 1999 with an elected presidential system, a bicameral legislature, constitutional federalism, and a rights framework. It also inherited 36 states, 774 local government areas, centralized security institutions, the distributive politics of the Federation Account, and administrative routines shaped by long periods of military government (Federal Republic of Nigeria, 1999/2023). Civilian rule restored electoral competition and created opportunities for judicial and legislative checks, but it did not automatically redesign the territorial assignment of functions or the fiscal basis of subnational government.

This sequence explains why a simple democracy–authoritarianism contrast is insufficient for administrative analysis. Military governments could execute nationwide territorial or bureaucratic changes quickly but lacked electoral accountability and often weakened institutional checks. Democratic governments face bargaining, judicial review, and electoral turnover, which can slow coordination but also permit correction and local responsiveness. The relevant question is which civilian institutions can replace command with credible coordination.

Three policy lessons follow. First, future decentralization should be evaluated by functions, finance, personnel, and accountability rather than by creating additional units. Second, revenue allocation should combine equalization with incentives for transparent own-source mobilization and measurable service delivery. Third, reforms of police, local government, and social services need transition rules that prevent unfunded mandates and clarify responsibility. Nigeria's post-independence history shows that territorial accommodation can reduce domination without necessarily building capable governments. The Fourth Republic's challenge is to preserve the union-building gains of federalism while replacing military-era hierarchy and opaque distributive bargaining with rule-bound intergovernmental administration.

2.4 Historical Legacies in Contemporary Administration

Historical legacy is useful only when it identifies a mechanism that can be observed in present institutions. Nigeria's past does not act directly on current outcomes. It operates through rules, organizations, revenue relations, territorial boundaries, professional routines, and expectations of legitimate authority. Four such mechanisms connect the preceding history to contemporary administration.

Uneven interfaces between public and customary authority

Colonial indirect rule adapted differently to centralized and decentralized precolonial orders. Contemporary land governance, dispute resolution, local leadership, and community mobilization consequently combine statutory and customary authority in different ways. Cappelen and Sorens (2018) provide a comparative argument about the importance of coherence between precolonial centralization and colonial intermediaries; Stilwell's (2011) Kano study shows that the intermediaries themselves reshaped colonial institutions. For current policy, “engage traditional leaders” is therefore too vague. Agencies need locality-specific rules on representation, gender and minority inclusion, disclosure, and appeal.

Territorial accommodation and transfer dependence

Repeated state creation reduced the power of the former regions and widened access to subnational office. At the same time, it multiplied governments dependent to varying degrees on centrally distributed revenues. This is a trade-off, not a verdict that state creation simply failed. The observable contemporary mechanism is fiscal: where transfers dominate and assignments overlap, citizens may struggle to attribute responsibility, and governments may face weak incentives to expand accountable own-source revenue. Suberu (2022) shows that Nigeria's de/centralization has moved dynamically across periods and policy fields, cautioning against describing the federation as uniformly centralized.

Inclusion through representative bureaucracy

The federal character principle responds to a history of regional domination and unequal access to state institutions. It can enhance the perceived legitimacy of the federation by making public organizations visibly inclusive. But symbolic representation, substantive responsiveness, and professional competence are distinct outcomes. Ikpebe et al. (2025) argue that implementation of the principle must be assessed through social equity, not only numerical representation. Personnel reform should therefore publish recruitment and promotion patterns, protect transparent merit thresholds, invest in capability development, and test whether representation changes organizational decisions and citizen treatment.

Persistent spatial inequality

Archibong (2018) finds durable regional differences in wealth, education, and access to federally provided services and links them to historical patterns of administrative treatment. This is stronger than merely noting that today's North and South differ, because it specifies service channels through which inequality persisted. Even so, contemporary insecurity, urbanization, state policy, and economic structure also matter. The responsible policy conclusion is to use historical exposure as one diagnostic variable—not as destiny—when designing equalization, capital investment, and implementation support.

These mechanisms suggest an evaluation agenda. Intergovernmental reform should track transfer dependence, own-source revenue, expenditure execution, and comparable service outcomes. Local governance reform should record which public and customary institutions participate and whose interests are excluded. Federal-character audits should connect workforce composition to organizational performance and public trust. Spatial policy should compare similarly situated jurisdictions over time and distinguish historical disadvantage from present implementation choices.

The larger lesson is that Nigeria's institutional compromises often perform one task while creating costs elsewhere. Indirect rule reduced colonial staffing needs but produced uneven local authority; regional and state structures accommodated diversity but made territorial distribution politically central; military governments strengthened coordination but weakened accountability; representative rules broadened inclusion but can be implemented without substantive equity. Policy design should make these trade-offs explicit. Rather than trying to erase history through another uniform national blueprint, Nigeria can build institutions that compensate for inherited inequality, clarify responsibility, and allow local variation under enforceable standards of citizenship, transparency, and service quality.
