10.4 AfCFTA, Ports, Refineries, and Industrial Policy

AfCFTA membership, port investment, refinery capacity, and sector incentives are often presented as four separate policy achievements. Economically they form one implementation chain. A preferential tariff matters only if a firm can prove origin, meet standards, move inputs and output through a predictable border, finance the transaction, and deliver at a competitive cost. A refinery or special economic zone matters only if infrastructure, customs, suppliers, competition, and environmental rules allow productive capacity to operate rather than remain an enclave. The central question is therefore not whether Nigeria has adopted the right instruments, but whether public agencies can coordinate them and withdraw support when outcomes do not justify its cost.

AfCFTA converts a large home market into a test of regional competitiveness

Nigeria's AfCFTA implementation moved beyond signature during 2025–2026. It formally adopted a tariff-liberalization schedule in April 2025 covering 80 percent of traded goods, ratified the Digital Trade Protocol in November 2025, and submitted a services schedule to ECOWAS in October 2025. In April 2026 the authorities also adopted measures to remove selected import bans and phase out some Import Adjustment Taxes (Stojanov et al., 2026). These are consequential steps because they make preferences and rules more operational, but adoption does not establish utilization.

Preferential access is useful only when firms claim it. Utilization depends on whether exporters know the eligible tariff line, can document rules of origin, obtain sanitary and technical certification, and complete customs procedures at a cost lower than the tariff preference. Smaller firms face a larger fixed compliance burden per shipment. Digital-trade rules similarly require domestic legal recognition of electronic documents, interoperable identity and payment systems, data protection, cybersecurity, and avenues for cross-border redress. A protocol can reduce policy uncertainty, but it cannot supply these administrative complements by itself.

The agreement also exposes a tension between regional integration and discretionary import substitution. Domestic producers may need time to acquire capabilities, yet open-ended bans and high input tariffs can protect inefficient incumbents, raise consumer prices, and make exported manufactures more expensive. A 2014 World Bank simulation found that replacing Nigeria's bans and levies with a more coherent ECOWAS tariff regime could lower input costs for many firms and reduce incentives for informal trade, while also creating concentrated adjustment losses in particular industries (von Uexkull & Shui, 2014). The exact estimates are historical and should not be projected onto today's tariff schedule. The mechanism remains relevant: the costs of protection are diffuse among consumers and input-using firms, whereas the losses from reform are concentrated and politically visible.

Nigeria should therefore publish a product-level AfCFTA implementation dashboard. It should record the tariff schedule and effective date, preference claims and rejection reasons, origin-certificate time and cost, standards and sanitary failures, firm size, destination, and repeat exporting. A rising number of registered exporters is not enough if few complete a second shipment. The decisive indicators are utilization, survival, delivery reliability, domestic value added, and progression into more demanding products and markets.

Port performance is a multi-agency waiting-time problem

Physical port capacity matters, but the binding delay often lies in the sequence among shipping lines, terminal operators, Customs, the Nigerian Ports Authority, standards and food-safety agencies, banks, truck systems, and importers. Nigeria's first Time Release Study at Tincan Island Port examined the interval from booking a consignment for examination to physical gate-out. It reported an average of 3 days and 10 hours and attributed more than 84 percent of elapsed time to waiting rather than active processing. The study also acknowledged that missing integrated timestamps prevented full measurement from vessel arrival through earlier clearance stages (Nigeria Customs Service, 2026a). The number is therefore a measured segment, not total end-to-end port dwell time.

That limitation is analytically useful: a port cannot manage what its agencies do not jointly timestamp. Sequential inspection, manual delivery orders and truck dispatch, limited use of scanning, system downtime, and poor visibility create queues even when each agency reports acceptable internal processing time. The failure is relational. Optimizing Customs alone may move the bottleneck to a regulator, terminal, bank, or gate.

The Authorized Economic Operator pilot points to a possible incentive-compatible approach. The Nigeria Customs Service reported an average release time of 43 hours for certified operators, compared with five days before certification and seven days for regular operators (Nigeria Customs Service, 2025). These administrative comparisons are not a randomized evaluation: selected firms are more compliant and capable at baseline. Still, they suggest that risk-based treatment and trusted-trader status can reduce physical inspection without abandoning enforcement.

The National Single Window is valuable if it produces one submission, one shipment identifier, shared status visibility, and binding handoffs across agencies. A portal that merely places separate forms behind one webpage does not remove sequential approvals. Implementation should therefore be assessed through end-to-end timestamps, the share of declarations receiving coordinated rather than repeated inspection, scanner utilization, variance as well as average release time, unofficial payment reports, demurrage and storage cost, and the proportion of cases released without manual intervention (Nigeria Customs Service, 2026b).

Refining is a strategic gain whose development effect depends on market design

New refining capacity changes Nigeria's external economy by replacing some fuel imports and creating exportable petroleum products. Sections 10.1 and 10.2 established both the balance-of-payments improvement and the continued common exposure to hydrocarbons. The industrial-policy issue is how the gain is governed. Feedstock access, pipeline and depot infrastructure, product standards, pricing, foreign-exchange settlement, import rules, and third-party logistics determine whether refining lowers system costs and builds suppliers or creates a protected bottleneck.

Scale economies make concentration unsurprising in refining, but they do not eliminate the need for competition policy. Authorities should publish feedstock-allocation rules, benchmark domestic prices against relevant import- and export-parity measures, ensure non-discriminatory access to shared infrastructure where feasible, and monitor product quality and environmental performance. Import restrictions should not be triggered solely by nominal installed capacity; they should depend on reliable operating output, geographic distribution, inventories, and the ability to meet demand without creating scarcity rents.

The same logic applies to backward linkages. Local-content targets can support engineering, maintenance, fabrication, logistics, and professional services when domestic firms can learn and compete. If targets are detached from capability and cost, they can instead create paper compliance or privileged intermediaries. Supplier-development support should fund certification, training, and technology adoption and then test whether local firms win repeat contracts, improve productivity, and enter other markets. The desired outcome is transferable capability, not permanent dependence on one anchor firm.

Industrial policy needs performance discipline and a political strategy for adjustment

Nigeria has long used tariffs, import bans, credit, tax relief, local-content rules, procurement, free zones, and infrastructure projects to promote production. The case for intervention is real where firms face coordination failures, learning spillovers, missing standards infrastructure, or first-mover risk. The danger is that support becomes a distributive bargain that outlives learning, blocks entry, or shifts costs to consumers and input users.

Evidence from African special economic zones illustrates the implementation problem. Farole and Moberg document that Nigerian zone authorities, Customs, finance agencies, local governments, and infrastructure providers have often held misaligned mandates. In Calabar, institutional competition contributed to a flagship zone being disconnected from the port, and one-stop arrangements lacked authority over the participating agencies (Farole & Moberg, 2017). The lesson is not that zones cannot work. It is that fenced infrastructure does not substitute for governance, connectivity, and credible agency commitments.

Recent Nigerian evidence also shows why “losers” cannot be ignored. Odijie's study of the Nigeria Sugar Master Plan finds that land disputes, displaced livelihoods, and smuggling responses undermined targets despite incentives for domestic growing and refining. Groups with limited formal political power could still obstruct implementation through conflict or informal adaptation (Odijie, 2024). Adjustment assistance is therefore not merely compensation after reform. Consultation, land and livelihood safeguards, worker transition, consumer-price monitoring, and lawful market access are part of an implementable industrial policy.

Performance discipline requires an explicit contract between support and learning. Before intervention, government should state the market failure, baseline, beneficiary, fiscal and consumer cost, measurable target, review date, and exit rule. During implementation, an independent unit should publish disbursement, tax expenditure, output, productivity, employment, wages, local sourcing, exports, prices, and environmental compliance. Support should expand when verified learning and spillovers exceed cost, change when the mechanism fails, and end when firms can compete or when targets are repeatedly missed.

This is not a choice between laissez-faire and permanent protection. Nigeria needs active coordination because power, ports, standards, finance, skills, and regional rules must work together. It also needs rivalry and evaluation because connected firms can convert coordination into exclusion. AfCFTA can strengthen discipline by enlarging the market and exposing firms to regional competition, while temporary and transparent support helps capable firms cross initial thresholds.

Chapter 10's evidence converges on a single conclusion. Nigeria's external position improved, export categories widened, and direct investment began to recover. Yet merchandise earnings remain hydrocarbon-concentrated, services exports and regional transactions are incompletely measured, recent financing is portfolio-led, and trade costs arise from multi-agency implementation failures. Success should therefore be judged by whether firms use preferences, clear borders predictably, learn from investment, add domestic value, and remain competitive without indefinite protection. The external economy becomes transformative only when foreign exchange earnings, productive imports, investment, and industrial policy reinforce the same process of capability accumulation.
