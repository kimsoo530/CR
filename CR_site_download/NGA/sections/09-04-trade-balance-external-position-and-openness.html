9.4 Growth Model and Structural Vulnerabilities

Nigeria's growth model combines a very large domestic market, entrepreneurial services, extensive agriculture, hydrocarbon foreign-exchange earnings, and a federal state with exceptionally limited revenue and uneven implementation capacity. The model can generate episodes of rapid aggregate expansion, especially when oil prices, reform momentum, and capital inflows align. Its weakness is that those episodes have not consistently raised productivity, formal employment, or public investment faster than demographic pressure. The central vulnerability is therefore not one external deficit or one commodity price. It is a set of feedback loops through which oil volatility, fiscal weakness, low private investment, infrastructure deficits, informality, insecurity, and climate shocks reproduce low per-capita gains.

Stabilization has improved resilience, but prosperity requires a different test

The reforms since 2023 repaired important macroeconomic foundations. Exchange-rate unification improved price discovery, central-bank financing of fiscal deficits was curtailed, monetary policy tightened, and external reserves strengthened. Real GDP growth reached about 4.0 percent in 2025, and the IMF projects 4.1 percent in 2026. These are meaningful improvements over the stagnation that followed the 2014 oil-price shock. Yet the World Bank reports that household incomes have not fully recovered and poverty remains high. Stabilization is thus a necessary platform for growth, not evidence that the growth model has already become inclusive (International Monetary Fund, 2026a; World Bank Group, 2026).

The gap is a first-pass arithmetic indicator of potential per-capita output growth. Population values are estimate-based, and the gap does not measure distribution, labor productivity, employment quality, or welfare.

The relevant arithmetic is the sustained gap between real output growth and population growth. When GDP grows only slightly faster than population, per-capita gains are small and easily erased by inflation, conflict, or distributional inequality. The historical figure shows why occasional high-growth years are insufficient: the policy test is whether the growth–population gap remains positive through commodity and political cycles. It should also be interpreted conservatively because national population estimates remain projection-based in the absence of a recent census and the GDP series has been rebased.

World Bank analysis of the decade through 2023 reaches a similar conclusion from the labor side. Real value added grew more slowly than the labor force in most sectors, output per worker fell, and even agricultural output per worker rose only 6.3 percent over the decade. Nigeria therefore needs not only faster GDP growth but a composition that raises productivity and earnings for workers entering the labor market (World Bank, 2025). The distinction rules out a nominal GDP target, an oil-output rebound, or a services boom as sufficient measures of success.

Five reinforcing loops make the model vulnerable

The first loop connects oil, foreign exchange, and fiscal operations. Oil's domestic GDP share is small, but its export and revenue leverage remains large. A negative price or production shock reduces foreign-exchange supply, places pressure on the naira and reserves, increases imported inflation and debt-service costs, and compresses federal and state budgets. Procyclical spending during favorable periods and abrupt adjustment during downturns then weaken public investment. Sala-i-Martin and Subramanian attribute Nigeria's long-run resource-curse pattern primarily to the effect of oil rents on institutional quality, while Usman shows that the distribution of political power produces selective and episodic reform rather than a uniform predatory or developmental state (Sala-i-Martin & Subramanian, 2013; Usman, 2020). These perspectives imply that stabilization funds or fiscal rules work only if revenue measurement, withdrawals, federation-account deductions, and project selection are transparent enough to constrain coalition bargaining.

The second loop runs from low public revenue to low state capability. Nigeria's rebased GDP makes revenue and development spending ratios appear even smaller relative to the economy. Limited revenue constrains electricity, transport, security, schools, health, water, and social protection; weak services in turn reduce productivity, compliance, and confidence in taxation. Interest obligations absorb a large share of federal revenue even when the debt-to-GDP ratio appears moderate. The resulting state can formulate ambitious plans but lacks reliable finance for maintenance and implementation. Raising rates alone is not a solution: administrative improvements, a broader base, reduced exemptions and leakages, and transparent intergovernmental use of revenue must precede or accompany higher burdens so that fiscal extraction strengthens rather than weakens the social contract.

The third loop links firm structure, finance, and infrastructure. Nigeria lacks enough mid-sized and large productive firms capable of organizing suppliers, adopting technology, exporting, and providing stable wage employment. The 2026 Streamlined Country Diagnostic reports foreign direct investment of only 0.6 percent of GDP in 2024 and private-sector credit equal to 21.3 percent of GDP, compared with a 47 percent average for lower-middle-income economies. Fewer than 5 percent of micro, small, and medium enterprises had access to formal credit, and they received only 1 percent of bank lending. At the same time, high tariffs on raw materials, intermediate inputs, and capital goods raised production costs (World Bank, 2026). Firms respond by remaining small, informal, vertically integrated, or dependent on self-provided power and logistics; those responses then weaken the tax base, worker training, supplier networks, and demand for long-term finance.

The fourth loop is demographic. The World Bank's 2026 diagnostic estimates that Nigeria has 63 million people aged 15–29 and that almost 60 million young people will reach working age over the next decade (World Bank, 2026). More than 92 percent of employed youth work informally without contracts or benefits (World Bank, 2026). Demographic scale can enlarge markets and innovation, but it becomes a vulnerability when the annual flow of entrants exceeds the capacity of productive firms and public systems to provide jobs and skills. The relevant policy problem is not open unemployment alone. Low-hours work, own-account survival activity, discouraged labor-force exit, and low earnings can coexist with a high measured employment rate. Job policy must therefore track productivity, hours, earnings, formality, and worker transitions rather than a single unemployment statistic.

The fifth loop connects insecurity, climate, food prices, and poverty. Violence restricts access to farms and markets, destroys assets, changes crop choice, discourages investment, and raises transport and security costs. Climate variability amplifies these effects through flooding, heat, water stress, erosion, and coastal risk. Micro-evidence from the Boko Haram insurgency shows that greater attack intensity reduced agricultural output and productivity, particularly for staple crops, without a corresponding fall in land use—consistent with a productivity shock rather than simple abandonment (Adelaja & George, 2019). Because poor households devote a high share of expenditure to food and transport, these supply shocks rapidly become inflation and welfare shocks. The 2026 Nigeria Country Climate and Development Report therefore treats adaptation, low-emission investment, and institutional capacity as components of development strategy rather than an environmental addendum (World Bank, 2026b).

These loops interact. Insecurity lowers food supply; food inflation raises poverty and fiscal pressure for subsidies; broad subsidies reduce funds for infrastructure and targeted protection; infrastructure deficits keep agriculture and firms unproductive; weak productivity narrows the tax base; and fiscal weakness reduces the state's ability to provide security and resilience. A policy aimed at one price or sector can fail because it shifts rather than breaks the loop.

The binding constraint is coordinated implementation, not a shortage of plans

Nigeria's Agenda 2050 and sector strategies identify higher productivity, diversification, infrastructure, human capital, private investment, and poverty reduction as linked objectives (Federal Ministry of Budget and Economic Planning, 2023). The recurring problem is simultaneous ambition without binding prioritization. Electricity reform, transport corridors, agricultural commercialization, industrial policy, skills, finance, and social protection are complementary, but the federal government cannot implement all components uniformly across 36 states and 774 local governments (Federal Republic of Nigeria, 1999).

A more credible strategy would organize policy around a small number of spatial and sectoral production systems. An agricultural corridor, for example, requires security, land and water governance, feeder roads, storage, standards, finance, processing, and market access in the same places and sequence. A manufacturing or digital-service cluster requires reliable power, customs and ports, broadband, skills, supplier finance, and predictable regulation. Concentrating complementary interventions can create demonstrable productivity gains, after which successful arrangements can be adapted elsewhere. This is not a case for permanent enclaves: fiscal transfers, procurement, competition rules, and learning systems should diffuse benefits and prevent local capture.

The public sector's role is to solve coordination and credibility problems that individual firms cannot solve, while avoiding discretionary protection without performance discipline. Industrial incentives should specify the market failure, beneficiary, duration, measurable investment or export condition, and exit rule. Infrastructure should be evaluated on service reliability and maintenance, not only capital completion. Skills programs should be tied to verified employer demand and placement. Social protection should be regular, targeted, and shock-responsive so that reform costs do not force households to sell productive assets or withdraw children from school.

A falsifiable scorecard for a new growth model

The claim that Nigeria has shifted to a more resilient and inclusive growth model should be tested against outcomes that can disconfirm it. The following scorecard connects each vulnerability to an observable result rather than a project announcement.

DimensionEvidence of improvementWarning that the old model persists
Per-capita productivityReal GDP and output per worker grow persistently faster than population and labor-force growthAggregate GDP rises while per-capita output or median earnings stagnate
Jobs and firmsFormal wage employment, hours, earnings, and the number of productive mid-sized firms increaseEmployment growth is concentrated in low-earning informal trade and own-account work
Fiscal capabilityNon-oil revenue finances higher-quality infrastructure, human-capital, maintenance, and targeted protectionInterest and recurrent commitments crowd out development spending despite a moderate debt ratio
External resilienceNon-oil exports, durable FDI, net reserves, and domestic supplier capability deepenReserve gains depend mainly on borrowing or short-term portfolio inflows and oil still drives adjustment
InclusionReal household consumption recovers and poverty, food insecurity, and regional gaps fallInflation declines but lost purchasing power and unequal service access persist
Climate and securityCrop losses, displacement, outage time, and disaster recovery costs fall in exposed areasRecurrent shocks repeatedly trigger food inflation, emergency subsidies, and capital destruction

This scorecard also clarifies sequencing. Macroeconomic stability and predictable prices are the entry condition. Revenue administration, expenditure credibility, and shock-responsive protection make stabilization politically sustainable. Reliable infrastructure, finance, competition, and skills permit firms and farms to raise productivity. Export and supplier performance then test whether support generated capabilities rather than rents. Climate adaptation and security must be embedded throughout because they determine whether gains survive.

Nigeria's current position is therefore neither a failed reform story nor a completed transition. The post-2023 framework has reduced several distortions and created a better platform for investment, but the structural loops that connect oil, low revenue, small firms, demographic pressure, insecurity, and climate exposure remain. The next phase should be judged less by the announcement of a trillion-dollar economy or by a temporary macroeconomic surplus than by sustained per-capita productivity, more and better jobs, resilient non-oil revenue and exports, stronger public services, and measurable recovery in household welfare.
