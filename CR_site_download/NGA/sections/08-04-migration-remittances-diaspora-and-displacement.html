8.4 Migration, Remittances, Diaspora, and Displacement

The preceding sections examined demographic scale, spatial redistribution, and the rights attached to place and identity. Migration brings those processes together by moving people, skills, claims, and resources across local, state, and national boundaries. Nigeria is simultaneously a country of emigration, immigration, regional circulation, return, refugee reception, and large-scale internal displacement. These categories should not be collapsed. An emigrant stock counts people from Nigeria living abroad at a point in time; a diaspora may also include descendants who maintain social or economic ties; a remittance is a balance-of-payments flow rather than a headcount; refugees have crossed an international border; and internally displaced persons (IDPs) remain within Nigeria. Each measure describes a different population, reference period, and administrative responsibility. Policy becomes incoherent when a large diaspora estimate is treated as the number of current emigrants, when remittances are equated with public revenue, or when camp closure is counted as proof that displacement has ended.

Emigration creates transnational capability and domestic sectoral losses

The United Nations' 2024 international migrant-stock matrix estimates that about 2.094 million people originating from Nigeria lived in another country. The United States accounted for approximately 565,000, the United Kingdom 324,000, Niger 200,000, and Cameroon 188,000. The pattern is both intercontinental and regional: the two largest Anglophone high-income destinations held about 42.5 percent of the estimated stock, while neighbouring Niger and Cameroon together held about 18.5 percent (Migration Policy Institute, 2026; United Nations Population Division, 2025). These are estimated stocks based primarily on country of birth or, where necessary, citizenship. They are not annual departures, do not measure irregular movements well, and exclude many descendants whom Nigeria's 2021 National Diaspora Policy includes when they maintain links and an interest in contributing to the country (Nigerians in Diaspora Commission, 2021).

This distinction matters because migration's effects depend on who moves, where, for how long, and whether capabilities circulate back. International mobility can raise household income, extend commercial and professional networks, and transfer knowledge. It can also remove scarce workers from services whose training was publicly or collectively financed. The tension is especially visible in health. OECD and WHO data for circa 2020–2021 counted about 17,060 Nigerian-born doctors and 54,480 Nigerian-born nurses working in OECD countries; relative to 2000–2001, those stocks had increased by 264 and 295 percent, respectively (OECD, 2025). Country of birth is not the same as place of professional training, and the data omit non-OECD destinations, so the figures do not measure Nigeria's exact training loss. They nevertheless show that Nigerian health personnel form a substantial component of international labour markets while Nigeria remains on the WHO Health Workforce Support and Safeguards List.

Calling this process either “brain drain” or “brain gain” is too categorical. The domestic effect depends on the balance among training capacity, vacancies, wages, working conditions, professional development, geographic deployment, return, and knowledge exchange. A doctor who trains abroad and collaborates with a Nigerian institution differs from a publicly trained clinician leaving an already understaffed rural facility; a nurse recorded as Nigerian-born may have received all professional education in the destination country. Nnabuihe and colleagues document financial and intellectual contributions by Nigerian diaspora networks, including academic exchange and capacity-building, but their cases do not demonstrate that such engagement offsets losses in every sending sector (Nnabuihe et al., 2024).

The policy response should therefore be sector-specific rather than restrictive. Health-workforce planning should link professional registration, training output, public and private employment, vacancies, exits, return, and subnational deployment. Bilateral recruitment agreements should implement the WHO code through destination-country support for additional training capacity, faculty, equipment, and temporary or circular placements, while protecting workers' freedom and employment rights. Domestic retention requires reliable pay, safe workplaces, career progression, training opportunities, and functional facilities; compulsory service or exit barriers cannot repair those conditions. For the wider diaspora, the Nigerians in Diaspora Commission can broker verified professional networks, research partnerships, investment facilitation, and short-term service arrangements, but these programmes need outcome measures—staff retained, courses delivered, ventures surviving, patents or protocols adopted—not only participant counts or pledged funds.

Remittances insure households but are not a substitute for public provision

The World Bank's current World Development Indicators series records US$22.79 billion in personal remittances received in 2025, compared with US$22.13 billion in 2024, US$19.55 billion in 2023, and US$23.81 billion in 2019 (World Bank Remittance DataBank, 2026). The indicator combines personal transfers between resident and non-resident households with compensation earned by border, seasonal, and other short-term workers, following the IMF balance-of-payments definition. It is reported in current US dollars and built from IMF balance-of-payments data and World Bank staff estimates. It therefore neither captures every informal transfer nor identifies how much came from members of the wider Nigerian diaspora.

The series shows scale and volatility, not a steadily rising development fund. Exchange-rate changes can alter the current-dollar value and the naira purchasing power in opposite directions; policy changes can shift transfers between formal and informal channels; and the GDP ratio can jump when the exchange-rate conversion or national-accounts denominator changes. The 2025 value was below the 2019 nominal-dollar level even though it represented a much larger share of the contemporaneous dollar-denominated GDP series. For this reason, remittances should not be compared with tax revenue or foreign investment without aligning definitions, currency, and denominator (World Bank Remittance DataBank, 2026).

At household level, the strongest claim is insurance and consumption support, not automatic structural transformation. Bang, Mitra, and Wunnava use the 2009 Nigerian Migration Household Survey and instrumental-variable quantile regression to find positive expenditure effects across most of the conditional distribution and poverty reduction, but also a non-uniform distributional pattern that may “hollow out” parts of the middle. The older survey and identifying assumptions limit application to current Nigeria, yet the analysis demonstrates why aggregate inflows cannot reveal who receives transfers or how gains are distributed (Bang et al., 2022). Nnabuihe and colleagues similarly identify household support and knowledge transfer, but their mixed evidence does not establish that private remittances finance public goods at scale (Nnabuihe et al., 2024).

Policy should reduce the friction and risk of private transfers without appropriating them as state finance. The World Bank's corridor data show that prices vary substantially by sending country, provider, payment instrument, access point, transfer size, and exchange-rate margin; a single “Nigeria remittance cost” can therefore be misleading (World Bank Remittance Prices Worldwide, 2026). Regulators should publish comparable corridor-level total costs, license and supervise providers proportionately, make customer identity and payment systems interoperable, and permit transparent competition in payout channels. Household surveys should identify receipt, frequency, channel, origin country, sex of sender and recipient, location, and use while protecting privacy. Productive-investment instruments, including diaspora bonds or pooled vehicles, require clear use-of-proceeds rules, independent reporting, currency-risk disclosure, and ordinary investor protection. They should be voluntary financial products, not a policy assumption that migrant households owe the state development finance.

Durable solutions require rights, services, livelihoods, and host-community capacity

Forced displacement is a different administrative problem from labour migration. The UNHCR Nigeria operational portal reported 3,711,314 IDPs and 140,362 refugees and asylum-seekers hosted in Nigeria on 1 July 2026, drawing on government, IOM, NEMA, and UNHCR sources (UNHCR Operational Data Portal, 2026). The IDP number aggregates assessments in the north-east and north-central/north-west at different collection rounds; it is an operational stock for covered areas, not a simultaneous national census. Refugees and asylum-seekers in Nigeria are also distinct from Nigerian refugees abroad. These qualifications are essential because displacement estimates change with access, registration, return, repeated movement, disasters, and the boundary between an IDP, returnee, and resident member of a host community.

The causes and effects are territorially heterogeneous. Insurgency has generated protracted displacement in the north-east; banditry and communal violence are central in parts of the north-west and north-central zones; floods and other hazards produce sudden and sometimes repeated movements; and Nigeria also hosts refugees, predominantly from neighbouring countries. Displacement is not only a humanitarian stock. It is a shock to land access, schools, clinics, housing, food markets, documentation, policing, and local public finance in places that often had weak services before newcomers arrived.

George and Adelaja combine Nigeria's General Household Survey food-consumption module with IOM displacement data and find that IDP inflows were associated with worse food-security outcomes in host communities, with the adverse effect concentrated in insurgency-driven displacement. They attribute the pattern to an immediate demand shock, rigid food production and distribution, access constraints, and slower humanitarian response; disaster- and communal-clash displacement did not show the same significant effect in their models (George & Adelaja, 2022). The result should not be read as evidence that displaced people cause insecurity. It shows that an unfinanced population shock can burden a local food system and that the cause of displacement changes agencies' ability to respond.

Nigeria's National Policy on Internally Displaced Persons recognizes conflict, disasters, and other causes and assigns responsibilities for prevention, protection, assistance, and durable solutions (Federal Ministry of Humanitarian Affairs, 2021). In 2024, Borno, Adamawa, and Yobe launched state action plans that define return, local integration, or relocation as possible pathways and shift attention from short-term needs to area-based development covering IDPs, returnees, and host communities (United Nations Nigeria, 2024). This is an important institutional advance, but a plan or physical departure from a camp is not itself a durable solution. UNHCR reported that more than 23,000 Nigerian refugees returned from Chad and Niger in 2025 to areas without an agency presence or verified return intention, demonstrating why a return count must not be equated with voluntary, safe, and sustainable reintegration (United Nations High Commissioner for Refugees, 2026).

Implementation should be tested against outcomes shared by displaced and host populations. A state action plan needs a geocoded population and service baseline, an assignment matrix among federal, state, and local agencies, a costed multiyear budget, and grievance and monitoring arrangements in which displaced people participate. Return, local integration, and relocation decisions should be voluntary and informed; documentation, tenure or land access, physical security, school enrolment, health coverage, livelihoods, and freedom of movement should be measured after assistance ends. Financing should follow the affected area rather than camp status alone, so schools, clinics, water systems, markets, and food supply expand for hosts as well as newcomers. Early warning and contingency finance are especially important where insurgency or flooding can produce repeated displacement.

Across international mobility, remittances, diaspora engagement, and forced displacement, Nigeria's challenge is not to stop movement. It is to build institutions that distinguish categories, protect rights, preserve essential domestic capabilities, lower transaction costs, and finance receiving communities. Success would be visible not in a larger remittance headline or fewer camps, but in better workforce retention and circulation, safer and cheaper transfers, reliable migration data, voluntary solutions, and restored access to services and livelihoods. Taken together, this chapter shows that population scale becomes a policy outcome only through place, membership, and movement: credible denominators identify people, territorially matched institutions organize services, equal citizenship governs belonging, and mobility systems determine whether capabilities and protection travel with them.
