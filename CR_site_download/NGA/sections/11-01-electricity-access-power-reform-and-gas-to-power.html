11.1 Electricity Access, Power Reform, and Gas-to-Power

Nigeria's electricity problem is often described as a shortage of megawatts. That description is incomplete. Households and firms need a service that is available when required, delivered at usable voltage, metered accurately, priced transparently, and supported by a payment chain that finances maintenance and new investment. A connection that supplies only intermittent power may improve the official access rate without displacing diesel generators or enabling machinery to operate. Nigeria's central electricity challenge is therefore to convert a fragmented portfolio of grid, captive generation, mini-grids, and stand-alone systems into reliable services while making the costs and responsibilities of each part visible.

Access has risen, but connection is not reliability

The World Bank's World Development Indicators estimate that the share of Nigeria's population with access to electricity rose from 48.0 percent in 2010 to 61.2 percent in 2023. The longer series shows real expansion from 35.2 percent in 1991, but also reversals and periods of stagnation rather than a smooth transition to universal service (World Bank, 2025a, indicator EG.ELC.ACCS.ZS). Because this indicator records whether electricity is available through a grid or off-grid source, it does not reveal daily hours, outage frequency, voltage quality, affordability, or the amount of electricity that a household or firm can actually use.

The access share increased substantially over the long run, but access identifies a connection or usable off-grid source rather than daily hours, outage frequency, voltage quality, affordability, or consumption. It must therefore be read alongside NERC's operational, loss, metering, and service-quality evidence.

The distinction changes the diagnosis. Improved access can coexist with extensive self-generation when public supply remains unreliable. Household evidence shows that better grid service would significantly reduce generator use, although higher-income households would retain backup capacity because they place a high value on continuity (Oseni, 2016). The implication is not that generator ownership should be prohibited before the grid improves. It is that reliability has an economic and environmental value that an access headcount misses: every hour of dependable service can displace a higher-cost unit of petrol or diesel generation, reduce equipment damage, and make productive activity less dependent on the wealth needed to purchase backup power.

Distributed systems can close part of this service gap more quickly than national-grid extension. The Nigeria Electrification Project supported 125 mini-grids and more than one million solar-home-system sales, through which the World Bank reports that over 5.5 million people obtained electricity access. Its successor, the US$750 million Distributed Access through Renewable Energy Scale-up project, targets new or improved service for more than 17.5 million people and electricity for almost 237,000 micro, small, and medium enterprises (World Bank, 2025e). These are project objectives and reported program outputs, not proof that every connection supplies adequate power over its asset life. The more demanding test is whether systems remain operational, tariffs remain affordable, batteries are replaced, productive users increase consumption, and service persists after capital subsidies end.

The binding constraint runs through the whole electricity value chain

The Nigerian Electricity Regulatory Commission's third-quarter 2025 report makes clear why adding nominal generation capacity alone cannot solve the problem. Across 28 grid-connected plants, average available capacity was 5,430.34 MW, but the average plant-availability factor was only 39.86 percent: 60.14 percent of installed capacity was unavailable for dispatch. Average hourly generation was lower still at 4,179.15 MWh/h, reflecting mechanical outages, gas constraints, and low offtake as well as plant availability. These are different bottlenecks. A plant may be installed but mechanically unavailable; available generation may be constrained by gas or water; dispatchable energy may not be evacuated by transmission; and electricity delivered to a distribution network may not be billed or collected.

The table describes a circular rather than a linear failure. In 2025 Q3, aggregate technical, commercial, and collection loss was 33.27 percent, an improvement from 37.92 percent in the preceding quarter but still far above the 20.54 percent efficient-loss allowance used in the tariff model. NERC estimated the excess as a ₦108.75 billion quarterly revenue loss. At the same time, only 55.37 percent of active registered customers were metered, with rates ranging from 28.65 percent in Yola and 29.69 percent in Jos to more than 84 percent in Eko and Ikeja. Unmetered consumers face estimated billing and weaker confidence in the relationship between payment and service; low collection then weakens distributors' balance sheets and their capacity to invest in feeders, transformers, meters, and maintenance. Poor service thereby reduces willingness to pay, while weak payment further degrades service.

Tariff policy cannot be separated from this operational chain. In 2025 Q3 the federal government's tariff-subsidy obligation was ₦458.75 billion, equal to 58.63 percent of the generation-company invoice. The amount was lower than in Q2 mainly because distributor energy offtake fell, not because the sector had reached a sustainable cost structure (Nigerian Electricity Regulatory Commission, 2026a). A tariff increase imposed without verified service improvement and comprehensive metering transfers inefficiency and exchange-rate risk to consumers. Conversely, holding tariffs below cost without a transparent, budgeted subsidy leaves generators and distributors unable to plan and crowds out other public spending. The relevant policy sequence is feeder-level measurement, loss reduction, service verification, targeted support for vulnerable users, and then tariff adjustment tied to delivered quality—not an abstract choice between full cost recovery and universal subsidy.

This diagnosis is consistent with the longer reform literature. Unbundling and privatization altered ownership but left transmission, market liquidity, gas infrastructure, regulation, and political intervention tightly interdependent (Ogunleye, 2017). Arowolo and Perez (2020) similarly argue that incomplete privatization, an underdeveloped gas network, and weak market regulation prevented the standard electricity-reform model from functioning as intended. The failure is not evidence that ownership is irrelevant; it shows that privatizing distribution assets cannot create a viable market when contracts, tariffs, fuel, transmission, metering, and payment discipline remain misaligned.

Decentralization creates a coordination test, not an automatic solution

The Electricity Act 2023 replaced the earlier federal framework and enabled states to establish and regulate intrastate electricity markets while NERC retained authority over interstate, national-grid, and international activities (Federal Republic of Nigeria, 2023b). By May 2026, regulatory oversight had formally transferred to 15 state regulators (Nigerian Electricity Regulatory Commission, 2026b). This can bring licensing, consumer protection, distribution planning, and enforcement closer to the geography of service failure. It can also create multiple tariff methodologies, technical rules, data systems, and appeal channels around networks that remain physically and financially connected.

The decisive issue is therefore the interface between jurisdictions. A state regulator can authorize an embedded generator or mini-grid, but it cannot independently assure national-grid stability, interstate transmission, gas delivery, or settlement of legacy obligations. Nor should a transfer allow liabilities and low-performing assets to remain federal while revenues and viable customers move to a state market. NERC, the Nigerian Independent System Operator, the Transmission Service Provider, state regulators, distributors, and bulk-market institutions need a common data and settlement protocol covering feeder boundaries, energy flows, technical standards, subsidy responsibility, customer complaints, and treatment of cross-border assets.

State-level variation makes decentralization analytically valuable because it permits comparison. The 2025 metering spread among distributors is too large to be treated as a single national condition. State markets should publish a small common scorecard: hours supplied by feeder and customer band; outage frequency and duration; voltage violations; metering coverage; billing and collection efficiency; technical and commercial losses; connection time; complaints and resolution; subsidy per delivered kilowatt-hour; and safety incidents. Comparable definitions would allow citizens and regulators to distinguish genuine improvement from the administrative relabeling of the same service.

Gas is a bridge only when fuel, networks, and payments arrive together

Gas-fired plants remain central to the national grid, so domestic gas can provide dispatchable electricity alongside hydropower and variable renewable energy. Yet Nigeria's gas endowment does not automatically become power. Pipeline insecurity, insufficient gathering and transport infrastructure, weak payment by the electricity market, competing export and industrial uses, plant outages, and transmission constraints determine whether gas reaches a turbine and whether its output reaches a paying customer. NERC attributed the Q3 2025 decline in thermal generation partly to gas constraints, mechanical outages, and low load offtake. This is why a new gas plant without a bankable fuel agreement, evacuation capacity, and credible payment chain may add nominal capacity without dependable energy.

The institutional pattern is longstanding. Research on the political economy of power reform identifies gas infrastructure and transmission as connected constraints rather than independent construction gaps (Ogunleye, 2017; Arowolo & Perez, 2020). A recent Natural Resource Governance Institute assessment likewise concludes that gas cannot meet Nigeria's access ambitions by itself when supply infrastructure, transmission, and sector payments remain unresolved; distributed solar has a stronger near-term role for many unserved and underserved users (George-Ikoli & Sayne, 2026). The report is a policy diagnostic rather than a causal evaluation, but its value is to test whether gas-to-power proposals specify the full delivery chain.

Nigeria therefore needs a portfolio rather than a fuel slogan. Existing gas plants merit rehabilitation where verified fuel supply, heat-rate efficiency, transmission capacity, and payment discipline make additional output cheaper and more reliable than alternatives. Grid-connected solar, hydro, storage, and transmission investment can reduce fuel exposure and complement dispatchable plants. Mini-grids and stand-alone systems can serve locations where grid extension is slow or uneconomic, provided regulation clarifies what happens when the main grid arrives and who pays for interconnection or asset conversion. Large industrial and commercial users can support embedded generation, but regulators should prevent their exit from leaving captive residential customers with an unaffordable network.

The policy implication follows from the diagnosis: Nigeria should manage electricity as a set of measurable service contracts. Public support to a plant, distributor, state market, or mini-grid should specify the energy delivered, reliability, loss reduction, metering, affordable-service obligation, emissions and safety performance, fiscal cost, and correction mechanism. The World Bank's 2026 restructuring of its Power Sector Recovery operation is instructive because it records tariff shortfalls of about ₦1.9 trillion per year in 2024 and 2025 and shifts attention toward time-bound investments with measurable reliability gains after broader reform disbursements underperformed (World Bank, 2026c). That is not a case for abandoning system reform. It is a case for judging reform by whether electricity reaches users reliably and whether the institutions responsible for each failure can be identified, financed, and held to account.
