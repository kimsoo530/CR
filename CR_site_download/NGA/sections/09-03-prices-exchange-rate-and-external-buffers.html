9.3 Industrial Structure: Oil, Agriculture, Manufacturing, and Services

Nigeria's production structure is more diversified than its export and public-finance structure, but it has not yet generated enough high-productivity employment. This distinction is central to interpreting the economy. Oil and gas account for only a small share of domestic value added, while agriculture and services dominate output and employment. Yet petroleum still supplies a disproportionate share of foreign exchange and remains embedded in federal revenue bargaining. At the same time, workers have moved from agriculture mainly into services rather than manufacturing, but much of the receiving service economy is informal trade and personal activity rather than scalable, tradable, high-productivity business services. Structural transformation should therefore be assessed through four linked tests: changes in value-added shares, labor reallocation, within-sector productivity, and the capacity to earn foreign exchange and fiscal revenue.

Rebasing changed the measured structure, not the underlying constraints

The 2025 national-accounts rebasing is indispensable for reading sector shares. Using 2019 as the new base year, the National Bureau of Statistics expanded coverage to 46 industries and 217 products and incorporated newer business, agricultural, living-standard, and informal-sector information. In the new 2019 benchmark, agriculture accounted for 25.83 percent of constant-price GDP, industry 21.08 percent, and services 53.09 percent. By 2024, their respective shares were 27.81, 16.67, and 55.52 percent. Oil and gas fell from 5.85 to 3.38 percent, while the non-oil economy rose to 96.62 percent. These are real-output shares at 2019 basic prices; they should not be mixed with current-price shares or the older 2010-base series (National Bureau of Statistics, 2025).

The rebasing also changed which activities appear economically important. Better coverage moved real estate ahead of crude petroleum and natural gas in the 2019 ranking and substantially revised water transport, administrative support, entertainment, health, livestock, and forestry. It raised the measured informal-sector contribution to GDP from 41.4 to 42.5 percent. This is partly a statistical correction: previously undercounted activity became visible without being newly created. As Jerven's work on African national accounts emphasizes, revisions can alter levels and sector rankings while leaving underlying productivity and institutional constraints unresolved (Jerven, 2013). The policy lesson is not to discount the new data, but to avoid treating a larger measured service economy as proof of newly achieved modernization.

The chart is useful for the long-run direction of sector shares, but its endpoint should not replace Nigeria's newer 2019-base national accounts. The NBS rebased benchmark reports 2024 constant-price shares of 27.81 percent for agriculture, 16.67 percent for industry, and 55.52 percent for services.

The WDI figure provides a longer historical view using a harmonized international series, whereas the table above reports the newer NBS benchmark. Differences in their 2024 levels arise from base years, coverage, valuation, and revision timing. The WDI lines are suitable for reading broad direction; the NBS series is the appropriate benchmark for Nigeria's current structure. Combining the two without documenting the break would create false precision (World Bank, 2025a; National Bureau of Statistics, 2025).

Agriculture is large because labor and livelihoods remain rural, not because productivity is high

Agriculture's roughly 28 percent real-output share coexists with employment of more than one-third of the population and extensive rural poverty. This combination points to low average labor productivity rather than agricultural strength alone. Crop production dominates the sector, but livestock, forestry, and fishing are economically and regionally important. In 2024, real crop production grew by 2.91 percent, while livestock contracted by 2.14 percent and fishing by 0.61 percent. The aggregate sector grew only 1.69 percent, below population growth and far below the rate required to raise rural incomes rapidly (National Bureau of Statistics, 2025).

The causes are institutional and spatial. Rain dependence and limited irrigation expose output to weather; insecure land rights reduce incentives and collateral; insecurity disrupts cultivation and transport; weak extension systems slow technology adoption; and poor feeder roads, electricity, storage, cold chains, and market information raise losses and transaction costs. The World Bank's 2026 AGROW project assessment identifies precisely these complementary constraints and notes that they deter private investment as well as lower farm productivity (World Bank, 2026a). This means that distributing inputs or restricting imports in isolation cannot deliver structural transformation. If transport, storage, security, land access, and competition remain unresolved, subsidies raise fiscal cost without reliably creating marketable surpluses or agro-processing linkages.

Agricultural upgrading nevertheless remains central. Cross-country research shows that measured gaps between agricultural and non-agricultural productivity narrow substantially when hours worked are considered, but do not disappear; rural nonfarm activity is also strongly linked to farm production (McCullough, 2017). The implication for Nigeria is not simply to move people out of agriculture. It is to raise seasonal utilization and value added per worker while building agro-processing, logistics, finance, and business services around viable production corridors. Such within-sector productivity gains can release labor voluntarily, expand food supply, lower inflation pressure, and create demand for more productive rural and urban services.

Industry is too small and internally heterogeneous to serve as a broad employment engine

The decline of industry's real share from 21.08 percent in 2019 to 16.67 percent in 2024 does not describe manufacturing alone. The broad category combines mining, oil and gas, manufacturing, utilities, and construction. Summing the NBS manufacturing subsectors yields only about 8.3 percent of real basic-price GDP in 2024. Food, beverages, and tobacco and textiles, apparel, and footwear are the largest manufacturing components, while electrical equipment, motor-vehicle assembly, metals, chemicals, plastics, and oil refining remain comparatively small. Industrial growth therefore may rise because oil output or construction recovers even when technological upgrading and factory employment remain weak (National Bureau of Statistics, 2025).

Manufacturing faces a mutually reinforcing constraint set: unreliable and expensive electricity, dependence on imported machinery and intermediate inputs, exchange-rate and working-capital risk, congested ports and corridors, limited long-term finance, policy reversals, and weak standards and supplier networks. These conditions divide the sector between a small number of large firms able to self-provide infrastructure and hedge risk and a much larger set of micro and small producers with low capital intensity. Local-content rules or import restrictions can create demand for domestic firms, but without competitive entry, time limits, performance targets, and access to reliable inputs they can also protect high-cost incumbents and raise downstream prices.

Nigeria thus requires a selective but disciplined industrial policy. Selection should follow observable coordination failures and learning potential, not the political visibility of individual firms. Agro-processing can link farm productivity to urban demand; pharmaceuticals and basic consumer goods can build scale in a large domestic market; construction materials can leverage infrastructure demand; and digitally enabled professional services can support manufacturing logistics, finance, and design. Support should be conditional on investment, productivity, supplier development, employment quality, and export or import-competing performance. Sunset clauses and published evaluations are necessary because Nigeria's political settlement has historically enabled successful reform in some sectors, such as telecommunications, while fragmenting or blocking upgrading elsewhere (Usman, 2020).

A service economy can transform productivity only if its composition changes

Services supplied 55.52 percent of real GDP in 2024 and generated the fastest broad-sector growth, 4.43 percent. But “services” spans very different production models. Trade represented 17.73 percent of real basic-price GDP, real estate 13.58 percent, telecommunications 8.06 percent, and financial institutions 2.41 percent. Professional, scientific, and technical services, administration and support, transport, entertainment, health, education, hospitality, and public administration made up the remainder in widely differing combinations of skill, formality, and tradability (National Bureau of Statistics, 2025).

Labor has moved in the same broad direction. By 2021, services employed more than half of Nigerian workers, compared with about one-third two decades earlier, while agriculture's share moved in the opposite direction. Average service labor productivity was more than 70 percent above agriculture, but much of the employment gain occurred in wholesale and retail trade and hospitality, where productivity can be close to agriculture. High-productivity finance, information and communications technology, and professional services absorb fewer workers and require skills and infrastructure that are unevenly distributed (World Bank, 2024). The result is service-led reallocation without a commensurate increase in median earnings.

This pattern is not uniquely Nigerian. Mensah et al. show that, across sub-Saharan Africa, workers leaving agriculture have moved mainly into services and particularly trade; those activities are generally less productive and more volatile than manufacturing and modern business services. Their analysis also demonstrates why aggregate service shares are insufficient: structural change can produce static gains when workers enter somewhat more productive activities while simultaneously generating dynamic losses if the receiving sectors experience weak productivity growth (Mensah et al., 2023). Nigeria's service expansion should therefore be disaggregated into tradable and non-tradable, formal and informal, high- and low-skill, and high- and low-productivity components.

The policy opportunity lies in linking modern services to the rest of the economy rather than treating them as an enclave. Telecommunications, fintech, logistics, creative industries, and professional services can export, diffuse technology, improve payments, and reduce coordination costs for farms and factories. They will not absorb labor at sufficient scale unless broadband and electricity become reliable, firms can formalize at reasonable cost, education and vocational systems supply relevant skills, and small producers gain access to platforms and finance. Conversely, policies that celebrate digital start-ups while neglecting trade logistics, competition, worker transitions, and basic skills will widen the divide between a high-productivity urban segment and the mass service economy.

Oil dependence is a leverage problem, not a GDP-share problem

Oil's 3.38 percent share of real GDP in 2024 might suggest that Nigeria has already diversified. That conclusion confuses production share with strategic leverage. Hydrocarbons remain central to export receipts, foreign-exchange liquidity, fiscal transfers, and investor risk perceptions. An oil-price or production shock can therefore move the exchange rate and public budgets even when most domestic output is non-oil. Usman's political-settlements analysis explains the apparent paradox: non-oil services can expand while revenue and elite bargaining remain organized around concentrated oil rents, producing episodic reform rather than a stable coalition for industrial upgrading (Usman, 2020).

Diversification should consequently be evaluated against a dashboard rather than one ratio. Relevant outcomes include non-oil export earnings, internally generated tax revenue, formal employment, productivity within agriculture and services, manufacturing value added and survival, domestic supplier linkages, and resilience to an oil shock. Lowering oil's GDP share through statistical revision or faster growth elsewhere is welcome but insufficient if the budget, foreign-exchange market, and federation account remain highly exposed.

The strategic objective is a connected production system. Higher agricultural productivity should feed competitive processing and logistics; manufacturing should generate supplier, technology, and employment spillovers; modern services should lower transaction costs and become exportable; and oil revenue should finance public goods rather than recurrent protection of specific firms. Policies should be judged by whether they relax the binding constraints linking these sectors, not by whether a favored sector's headline share rises. Nigeria's economy has diversified in what it produces, but its next phase of transformation depends on diversifying productivity, jobs, exports, and public revenue at the same time.
