10. Trade, Investment, and External Economy

10.3 FDI, Portfolio Flows, and Investment Climate

Nigeria does not face a generic shortage of “foreign capital.” It faces a composition and conversion problem. Foreign direct investment (FDI) gives an investor a lasting interest and some control in an enterprise; portfolio investment purchases tradable debt or equity without the same managerial commitment; “other investment” includes loans, deposits, trade credit, and related claims. These flows can all finance useful activity, but their maturity, currency exposure, reversibility, and contribution to productive capacity differ. An investment announcement is different again: it records an intention, not a completed balance-of-payments transaction. The relevant question is therefore whether Nigeria is attracting capital that remains through shocks, expands non-oil production, transfers capabilities, and creates competitive supplier and employment linkages.

Market scale has not translated into sustained direct investment

Nigeria offers a large consumer market, abundant energy and agricultural resources, strong entrepreneurial networks, and access to ECOWAS and AfCFTA markets. Those advantages have not prevented a long decline in realized FDI. The World Bank Group reports that net inflows averaged only 0.5 percent of GDP from 2013 to 2023 and that extractives, information-technology services, and manufacturing were the main recipient sectors. Announced projects rose in 2023, especially in services and manufacturing, but the report correctly treats their realization as conditional rather than completed investment (World Bank Group, 2025).

The GDP-relative measure prevents Nigeria's economic size from overstating investment intensity. Net FDI can be negative when disinvestment or intercompany repayments exceed new inflows, and the aggregate does not identify sector, greenfield capacity, ownership, jobs, or domestic spillovers.

The historical series shows why a large-market narrative is insufficient. FDI inflows exceeded 2 percent of GDP in several years during the 2000s, then declined sharply after the early 2010s and remained below 1 percent from 2014 through 2024. The 2022 value was slightly negative because net FDI is an accounting balance that can fall below zero when disinvestment, intercompany debt repayment, or profit-related transactions exceed new inflows. The figure uses a ratio to GDP to avoid confusing Nigeria's size with investment intensity, but it does not show project quality, sector, ownership, greenfield versus merger activity, or domestic spillovers (World Bank, 2025a).

This trajectory reflects several reinforcing mechanisms. Exchange-rate segmentation and foreign-currency shortages made it difficult to price assets, import equipment, service foreign-currency obligations, and repatriate earnings. High inflation and policy reversals raised the option value of waiting. Unreliable power, port and road costs, insecurity, and weak contract enforcement increased operating and risk-insurance costs after entry. Trade restrictions raised the price of imported intermediate and capital goods needed by domestic producers. These are not separate items on a generic “ease of doing business” list: together they weaken a project's expected cash flow and raise the discount rate applied to it.

The foreign-exchange reforms since 2023 reduced one important distortion by restoring more market-based price discovery and improving access to foreign currency. Yet depreciation also raised the naira cost of imported machinery and reduced the dollar value of domestic revenues until prices and productivity adjusted. A credible regime must therefore do more than produce a flexible rate on a particular day. Investors need to know that conversion, remittance, hedging, tax, customs, and dispute-resolution rules will remain intelligible across the life of a project.

The 2025 financing recovery remained portfolio-led

The composition of the recent recovery illustrates the difference between liquidity and commitment. On provisional balance-of-payments data, the financial account recorded a net inflow of US$5.9 billion in 2025. Net FDI improved to US$2.8 billion but remained below 1 percent of GDP. Net portfolio inflows were US$7.1 billion, while other investment registered a US$4.0 billion net outflow. Foreign participation in Central Bank open-market-operation (OMO) instruments accounted for about US$5.8 billion of net inflows. The same flows helped gross reserves rise, but they also created short-term liabilities and rollover risk (International Monetary Fund, 2026a, Annex VI).

Portfolio flows reacted most strongly to the new macro-financial setting, while direct investment recovered more gradually and other investment remained volatile. The categories differ in maturity and reversibility; they should not be added into an undifferentiated measure of investor confidence.

The figure shows a four-year pattern that a total-capital headline would conceal. Portfolio inflows rose from US$3.9 billion in 2022 to US$15.2 billion in 2024 before moderating in 2025. Direct investment recovered more slowly, from a net outflow in 2022 to US$2.8 billion in 2025. Other investment moved from a US$7.2 billion inflow in 2023 to a US$19.1 billion outflow in 2024 and a US$4.0 billion outflow in 2025. These categories are provisional balance-of-payments aggregates, not project counts, and the large errors and omissions discussed in Section 10.1 limit false precision. The defensible conclusion is that external financing improved, but its most responsive component was still the more reversible one (International Monetary Fund, 2026a, Table 2).

Portfolio investment is not inherently undesirable. A deeper government-bond and equity market can broaden financing sources, improve price discovery, and create an exit route that long-term investors value. The vulnerability arises when high-yield, short-maturity instruments are needed to attract non-residents and reserve accumulation depends on their continued rollover. A fall in the interest differential, a rise in global risk aversion, or doubts about currency adjustment can reverse the flow quickly. The authorities and IMF therefore share an interest in shifting foreign participation from short-term OMO instruments toward longer-dated government securities and equities while reducing the concentration of rollover risk (International Monetary Fund, 2026a).

The binding constraint is credible coordination, not another promotion campaign

Nigeria has investment-promotion institutions, sector plans, tax incentives, free zones, public-private-partnership frameworks, and a large pipeline of proposed projects. The harder problem is that a project crosses agencies and levels of government. A manufacturing investor may need federal customs and tax decisions, a state land title and right-of-way, regulated power or self-generation, environmental approval, port access, standards certification, security, and a reliable court or arbitration pathway. A fast company-registration portal cannot compensate for an uncertain sequence across all these veto points.

The 2025 Country Private Sector Diagnostic identifies power, finance, transport, security, trade barriers, and policy uncertainty as central constraints. It reports that domestic credit to the private sector was 12.9 percent of GDP in 2024, below the Sub-Saharan African average of 20.1 percent and the lower-middle-income average of 34.0 percent. It also estimates that net FDI averaged only 0.5 percent of GDP over 2014–2023, while announcements were concentrated in oil and gas, communications, renewable energy, and chemicals (World Bank Group, 2025). The mechanism is cumulative: firms facing unreliable infrastructure self-provide power and security, but high domestic interest rates and shallow long-term finance make that self-provision more expensive; foreign investors then demand still higher returns or choose sectors with enclave revenues and foreign-currency earnings.

Investment incentives can worsen this coordination problem when they are negotiated firm by firm. Tax holidays or customs exemptions may offset a private cost without fixing the public bottleneck, while discretion creates unequal treatment and fiscal opacity. A stronger regime would publish eligibility, duration, fiscal cost, performance conditions, and exit rules. It would use time-bound facilitation teams for strategic projects, but record approvals and delays in a common case-management system so that exceptional coordination becomes administrative learning rather than permanent privilege.

FDI quality depends on sector, linkages, and additionality

The value of FDI cannot be inferred from its gross amount. Resource-seeking investment can expand exports and fiscal receipts while operating as an enclave with limited local sourcing. Market-seeking investment can serve Nigerian consumers but raise import demand if production relies on foreign inputs. Efficiency-seeking investment is more likely to join regional or global value chains, but it requires reliable power, logistics, skills, standards, and policy access to imported inputs. Greenfield projects may create capacity and jobs, whereas acquisitions can improve management and finance without adding the same immediate physical capacity. These categories overlap, so project evaluation needs evidence rather than labels.

Peer-reviewed Nigerian evidence reinforces the need for disaggregation. Okechukwu, De Vita, and Luo find a positive long-run relationship between aggregate FDI and total exports, but the relationship survives disaggregation only for oil exports; primary and manufacturing FDI were linked to total and oil exports, while services FDI showed no significant long-run relationship with exports in their 1980–2015 data (Okechukwu et al., 2018). The study uses aggregate time-series relationships and cannot establish firm-level spillovers or describe the post-2015 digital economy. Its important contribution is narrower: attracting FDI did not automatically diversify Nigerian exports.

Policy should therefore test additionality and linkage formation. For a proposed incentive, officials should ask whether the investment would occur without support, whether it introduces technology or capacity not otherwise available, how many local suppliers can meet standards, what training is verifiable, how much foreign exchange it earns or saves after imported inputs and profit repatriation, and whether competition remains open. Incentives should be released against audited milestones such as installed and operating capacity, formal employment and training, supplier certification, export orders, research collaboration, and environmental compliance—not only committed capital.

A capital-quality dashboard makes policy trade-offs visible

A useful dashboard would publish five layers. The first separates announced, approved, committed, disbursed, and operating projects. The second separates FDI, portfolio equity, portfolio debt, OMO holdings, external loans, and trade credit by maturity and currency. The third records sector, state, greenfield or acquisition status, ownership, and ultimate investor economy. The fourth measures outcomes: jobs and wages, local procurement, training, exports, domestic value added, tax paid after incentives, and environmental performance. The fifth records administrative performance: time and variance for land, customs, standards, permits, grid connection, and dispute resolution.

This framework changes the policy objective. Nigeria should not maximize FDI at any cost or reject portfolio investment because it is liquid. It should lower economy-wide uncertainty, deepen longer-term domestic markets, and reserve selective support for investments with measurable spillovers that markets cannot coordinate alone. Short-term portfolio flows can support liquidity during stabilization, but monetary authorities should avoid making high-yield OMO financing a substitute for durable reserves and productive investment. FDI policy should favor transparent rules, competition, and capability transfer over bespoke exemptions.

The 2025 improvement is consequently real but incomplete. Direct investment recovered, foreign-exchange functioning improved, and proposed non-oil projects signal renewed interest. Yet the financial account remained portfolio-led and the long-run FDI ratio remained far below its earlier level and peer benchmarks. The test of a better investment climate is not the size of the next announcement ceremony. It is whether projects reach operation, remain through exchange-rate and political cycles, widen the non-oil export base, and leave Nigerian workers and suppliers with capabilities that persist even if the original foreign investor later exits.
