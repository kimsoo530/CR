12.2 Women, Youth, Vulnerable Employment, and Regional Inequality

The employment ladder described in Section 12.1 is not equally accessible. Nigeria's aggregate participation rate conceals at least four different forms of exclusion: women may work but remain concentrated in informal, low-hour, and non-wage activities; young people must cross the school-to-work boundary without a dense formal entry-level labor market; people with disabilities confront physical, informational, and discriminatory barriers before job quality can even be considered; and residents of different states encounter sharply different combinations of schooling, security, infrastructure, sectoral demand, and implementing capacity. These disadvantages interact. A young woman with a disability in a conflict-affected rural area does not experience four independent gaps that can be repaired by one generic training course.

Participation can be similar while job quality diverges

Nigeria's redesigned survey makes the distinction visible. In 2024 Q2, female and male labor-force participation was close—79.1 and 79.9 percent respectively—yet the similarity disappears further along the employment chain. Women had higher unemployment, time-related underemployment, self-employment, informality, and youth NEET rates, while their share in wage employment was lower. People with disabilities had a labor-force participation rate of only 36.7 percent, compared with 80.0 percent among people reporting no disability (National Bureau of Statistics, 2024b). The first gender result should therefore not be read as evidence that economic opportunity is equal, and the disability result should not be reduced to an individual lack of skill.

The World Bank's 2024 analysis of the same new labor-force framework reaches a compatible conclusion: employed women were more likely to be underemployed and less likely to hold wage or high-skilled jobs (World Bank, 2024). The mechanism is not a single cultural preference. Unequal schooling histories, unpaid care, safety and mobility, access to assets and finance, sectoral segregation, employer practices, and the legal and administrative environment jointly shape the set of feasible jobs. High female participation can coexist with a large opportunity gap when households depend on women's earnings but care and market institutions restrict where, when, and on what terms those earnings are generated.

Peer-reviewed workplace research adds a dimension that household-status categories cannot capture. Drawing on 52 interviews and about 200 hours of observation in Nigerian organizations, Adisa et al. (2024) find that patriarchal norms and high power distance can suppress women's workplace voice. The evidence is qualitative and does not estimate a national effect, but it identifies why obtaining a wage job is not the end of inclusion: limited ability to raise concerns, influence decisions, or challenge treatment affects retention, advancement, safety, and the quality of organizational learning. This mechanism links labor-market inequality to public administration because labor inspection, grievance systems, anti-discrimination enforcement, and employer human-resource practices determine whether formal rights are usable.

Nigeria's federal and legally plural setting makes uniform national assumptions especially risky. The World Bank's subnational Women, Business and the Law study covers the Federal Capital Territory and seven states representing all six geopolitical zones. It finds variation in statutory, customary, and Islamic-law settings and persistent gaps in supportive frameworks for pay, parenthood, childcare, and marriage. It also finds that expert assessments of outcomes do not always track the formal legal score (World Bank, 2025g). This is diagnostic rather than a representative survey of all women or all 37 jurisdictions; nevertheless, it shows why a federal law, a state program, and implementation in a local labor market are separate stages. Legal reform without childcare, enforcement, accessible services, and budgeted institutions may change the rule but not the feasible choice.

Youth exclusion is a failed transition, not one unemployment number

For young people, open unemployment is again too narrow. In 2024 Q2, unemployment among those aged 15–24 was 6.5 percent, but 12.5 percent were not in employment, education, or training (NEET); the female NEET rate was 14.3 percent compared with 10.9 percent for males. Among employed youth, time-related underemployment was 15.1 percent—well above the all-age rate of 9.2 percent (National Bureau of Statistics, 2024b). Each indicator observes a different failure. Unemployment identifies active search without work, NEET identifies detachment from three transition institutions, and underemployment identifies insufficient hours among those already counted as employed. None measures earnings, learning on the job, job stability, or whether work matches a qualification.

Research on Nigerian youth unemployment places this transition problem in a wider political economy of poverty, insecurity, weak services, and fragmented social policy (Virk et al., 2024). The review argues that Nigerian youth programs commonly emphasize training and job creation through ad hoc, selectively targeted schemes while structural and implementation constraints remain. This critique does not imply that skills are irrelevant. It means that training supply cannot create demand, safe mobility, electricity, firm capacity, or trustworthy placement services. A certificate becomes valuable only when a local production system uses the capability and when the program can trace a participant from enrollment to completion, placement, retention, and earnings.

Age boundaries also matter. The NLFS NEET measure used here concerns ages 15–24, while Nigerian youth policies and programs may use broader upper limits (National Bureau of Statistics, 2024b). Mixing those populations can make program reach appear larger or outcomes appear inconsistent. The administrative solution is not to choose one universal definition, but to publish the denominator for every objective: school-to-work transition at 15–24, early-career progression at 25–34, or a legally defined program population. Cohort and transition measures are more informative than a single total of “youth beneficiaries.”

State variation changes the policy diagnosis

The annual 2023 NLFS provides state-representative estimates and shows why neither a simple north–south narrative nor the national mean is sufficient. The national NEET rate for ages 15–24 was 15.6 percent, but estimates ranged from 4.5 percent in Zamfara and 6.0 percent in Bauchi to 36.0 percent in Rivers and 38.1 percent in Abia. Time-related underemployment ranged from 0.3 percent in Nasarawa and 1.2 percent in Sokoto to 31.1 percent in Kaduna and 33.9 percent in Plateau. The employment-to-population ratio ranged from 55.7 percent in Rivers to 88.4 percent in Bauchi. Among employees, only 26.3 percent nationally reported entitlement to either pension or health insurance, with estimates ranging from 3.9 percent in Abia to 65.9 percent in Zamfara (National Bureau of Statistics, 2024c).

These ranges should not be converted into a league table of state government performance. Sector composition, agricultural seasonality, urbanization, conflict, education, household survival strategies, sample uncertainty, and the strict unemployment-search rule all affect the estimates. A low unemployment or NEET rate may reflect successful absorption, but it may also coexist with agricultural work, early withdrawal from school, low earnings, or weak search institutions. Conversely, a high urban unemployment rate can partly reflect a labor market in which people can sustain active search for wage work. The correct use of state data is diagnostic: combine participation, LU2–LU4, NEET, employment status, informality, protection, earnings, and sector, then test the likely mechanism with administrative and qualitative evidence.

Disability makes the same point in sharper form. The 2024 Q2 participation gap of more than 40 percentage points cannot plausibly be repaired by entrepreneurship finance alone (National Bureau of Statistics, 2024b). Accessible transport and workplaces, inclusive schooling, assistive technology, non-discriminatory recruitment, benefit rules that do not punish work, accessible digital systems, and enforcement all affect whether a person can enter and remain in employment. Because disability status is self-reported and survey samples become thin after state-by-disability disaggregation, national surveys should publish uncertainty and avoid unstable rankings. Administrative systems should record accommodations and service completion without substituting registered beneficiaries for population-based outcome measurement.

An intersectional delivery model

The policy implication is to replace categorical program inventories with a transition-and-barriers model. For women, the relevant package may combine safe transport or workspace, childcare, property and finance access, enforceable workplace rights, and portable protection. For young entrants, it may combine foundational remediation, apprenticeship standards, employer-linked placement, mobility support, and income protection during search. For people with disabilities, accessibility and reasonable accommodation must be built into mainstream employment services rather than confined to a small separate scheme. State governments should adapt delivery to local sector demand and institutions while using common federal definitions and outcome measures.

Accountability should follow the transition. Every program should identify its eligible denominator, barrier diagnosis, responsible delivery chain, counterfactual or comparison strategy where feasible, and outcomes at six and twelve months. Completion and disbursement are intermediate outputs. The outcomes are employment status, hours, real earnings, job retention, movement into wage or higher-productivity self-employment, protection coverage, and closing of group gaps. Publishing these results by sex, age, disability, state, and urban-rural residence would expose whether a program reaches easier-to-serve participants while excluding those with multiple constraints.

This approach also clarifies the federal division of labor. The federal government can set survey standards, labor rights, program safeguards, portable social-protection architecture, and interoperable reporting. States and local institutions control many of the schools, transport conditions, markets, enterprise services, and enforcement interfaces that determine access. Employers, unions, cooperatives, and worker associations supply information that administrative records miss. Coordination is successful only when a person can move through these institutions without repeatedly proving identity, eligibility, disability, training, and placement status to disconnected systems—and when consent and grievance mechanisms protect against harmful data sharing.

Nigeria's labor-market inequality is thus not one average gap. It is a set of unequal transitions from school, care, disability, place, and informal work into more secure livelihoods. The next section shifts from people to the demand side of that transition: whether agriculture, industry, and services generate jobs whose productivity and skill requirements can sustain upward movement.
