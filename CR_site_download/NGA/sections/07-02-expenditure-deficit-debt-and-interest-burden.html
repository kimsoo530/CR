7.2 Fiscal Balance, Debt, and Interest Burden

Nigeria's debt problem is easily misdiagnosed. Its public-debt ratio is not exceptionally high by the standards of many emerging economies, yet interest payments absorb an unusually large share of federal revenue. The apparent contradiction disappears once the numerator, denominator, and institutional pathway are separated. Debt sustainability depends not only on the stock of liabilities relative to GDP, but also on the revenue actually controlled by the Federal Government of Nigeria (FGN), the currency and maturity composition of borrowing, the credibility of the budget, and whether borrowed funds become productive assets or recurrent and off-budget obligations. Nigeria's binding constraint is therefore better described as a revenue-and-execution problem expressed through debt service than as a debt-stock problem alone.

A moderate debt ratio can coexist with a severe interest burden

The IMF's latest internally consistent fiscal accounts illustrate this distinction. Consolidated-government financing needs rose from 2.4 percent of GDP in 2024 to an estimated 4.4 percent in 2025, even though the above-the-line deficit was only 1.8 percent. The difference was an estimated statistical discrepancy of 2.7 percent of GDP, which the IMF considers consistent with expenditure not yet captured by the Office of the Accountant General of the Federation. At the same time, public gross debt fell from 39.3 to 36.1 percent of GDP. That decline did not represent a comparable reduction in the nominal debt stock or in annual budget pressure: it reflected a favorable interest-growth differential, naira appreciation, and a 34 percent upward revision to 2024 nominal GDP following rebasing. In contrast, FGN interest payments rose from 40.8 to 53.2 percent of FGN revenue (International Monetary Fund, 2026).

This is why a debt-to-GDP threshold alone is an unreliable policy guide. The denominator contains economic activity that the federal tax system captures only weakly, whereas interest must be paid from much narrower cash revenue. Nigeria-specific time-series studies also do not support a simple proposition that all borrowing is growth-reducing. Yusuf and Mohd (2021) find different short- and long-run effects for domestic and external debt, while Onuoha (2025) finds that public spending partly mediates the relationship between domestic debt and growth. Oyadeyi et al. (2024) identify nonlinear relationships whose estimated thresholds vary substantially when debt is divided by GDP, revenue, or exports. These studies use historical aggregate data and cannot identify the effect of a particular recent loan or reform; their value here is more limited but still important. They show that the fiscal denominator, financing source, and use of proceeds matter. More generally, a negative debt-growth correlation can disappear after addressing endogeneity, warning against treating weak growth as proof that debt caused it (Panizza & Presbitero, 2014).

The relevant Nigerian mechanism is thus fiscal compression. When more than half of FGN revenue is pre-empted by interest, personnel and other statutory obligations become difficult to adjust quickly, leaving capital expenditure and non-salary service inputs as residual buffers. In 2025, the oil-revenue shortfall was partly accommodated through under-execution of reported federal capital spending. The World Bank similarly finds a marked federal-subnational asymmetry: interest and wages dominate the federal expenditure structure, while states devote a larger reported share to capital expenditure, although state execution and project quality remain heterogeneous (World Bank, 2026). Debt service therefore affects development less through an abstract national debt ceiling than through the annual cash sequence that determines which authorized services and projects are actually funded.

The stock conceals currency, refinancing, and accounting risks

The Debt Management Office (DMO) supplies the nominal portfolio view that the GDP ratio cannot. At 31 March 2026, total public debt reported by the DMO was ₦159.35 trillion (US$114.95 billion): domestic debt accounted for 54.85 percent and external debt for 45.15 percent. FGN domestic obligations represented 52.01 percent of the total portfolio, while states and the Federal Capital Territory represented 2.84 percent (Debt Management Office, 2026a). These are point-in-time debt-stock shares, not measures of the consolidated deficit or interest burden. They nevertheless reveal that debt strategy must manage two distinct exposures. Domestic issuance reduces direct foreign-currency risk but can be expensive when monetary policy is tight and refinancing needs are concentrated. External borrowing can offer longer maturities or concessional terms, but commercial foreign-currency debt transmits exchange-rate depreciation and global risk premia into naira-denominated fiscal costs.

Actual 2025 service payments show the price of that composition. DMO records ₦8.24 trillion in interest on FGN domestic debt and ₦8.61 trillion in total domestic debt service after including promissory-note principal. Treasury bills alone generated ₦2.55 trillion in interest, and FGN bonds generated ₦5.49 trillion. External public debt service totaled US$5.15 billion, comprising US$3.06 billion in principal, US$2.03 billion in interest, and US$59 million in other charges; commercial creditors accounted for almost half of the total, mainly Eurobonds (Debt Management Office, 2026b, 2026c). The domestic and external totals cannot be added without a dated exchange rate, and neither equals the IMF's accrual-based interest ratio. Read separately, however, they show simultaneous exposure to high local-currency carrying costs and foreign-currency refinancing.

Portfolio risk also extends beyond conventional bonds and loans. The 2025 deficit was financed through domestic securities, a Eurobond, syndicated infrastructure loans, and a CBN deposit drawdown equal to 1.1 percent of GDP. The IMF judges the liquidity effect of that drawdown to be similar to the earlier *Ways and Means* overdraft financing. It also warns that collateralized or otherwise complex financing instruments can create margin-call and contingent-liability risks that ordinary debt-stock tables may not reveal (International Monetary Fund, 2026). The fiscal risk register must therefore cover CBN-related financing, guarantees, state-owned enterprises, oil-backed or collateralized facilities, electricity-sector arrears, and public-private partnerships—not merely securities formally issued under the DMO mandate.

Exchange-rate valuation illustrates why transparency about composition is indispensable. A naira depreciation increases the domestic-currency value and service cost of foreign-currency debt even if no new dollar is borrowed; appreciation produces the reverse. Yet a favorable valuation movement or GDP rebasing can lower the reported debt-to-GDP ratio without releasing cash for hospitals, schools, security, or infrastructure. Conversely, domestic borrowing avoids that mechanical currency translation but may raise interest expense and deepen the sovereign-bank nexus when banks and institutional investors hold more government paper. The policy objective should not be to maximize either domestic or external borrowing. It should be to minimize cost subject to refinancing, currency, market-access, and contingent-liability risks while preserving credit and fiscal space for productive activity.

Budget credibility determines whether borrowing creates assets or arrears

Nigeria's debt dynamics begin before a bond is issued. Khalid's review of federal Budget Implementation Reports for 2011–2023 finds persistent optimism in oil-production and revenue forecasts and systematic under-execution of capital budgets. Forecast errors weaken the budget as a binding statement of policy because approved projects exceed the cash envelope that realistic revenue can support (Khalid, 2025). The World Bank's public finance review reaches the same institutional diagnosis for an earlier period: recurrent expenditure was protected more consistently than capital expenditure, while revenue shortfalls and delays made capital spending the de facto adjustment item. It estimates that the FGN deficit exceeded its budgeted level by an average of 1.4 percent of GDP from 2016 to 2021 (World Bank, 2022). These historical findings remain relevant because the 2025 accounts again combine an oil-revenue shortfall, capital under-execution, and a large financing-to-operations discrepancy.

The mechanism is cumulative. An optimistic revenue baseline permits an expansive appropriation; cash shortfalls then delay or ration releases; projects are extended, repriced, or moved outside the ordinary budget; and unrecorded commitments or exceptional financing enlarge future obligations. The January 2026 repeal-and-re-enactment legislation incorporated some previously executed expenditures into the 2024 and 2025 budgets after the fact. That may improve legal coverage, but ex post regularization is not equivalent to ex ante authorization, commitment control, and timely reconciliation. A 2.7 percent-of-GDP statistical discrepancy is consequently not just a measurement nuisance. It weakens the government's ability to know its true financing requirement, compare projects, enforce borrowing limits, and explain why approved services were not delivered (International Monetary Fund, 2026).

Policy should follow this causal sequence. First, fiscal reports should reconcile appropriation, commitment, cash release, payment, and financing on the same institutional perimeter, with a bridge between above-the-line operations and below-the-line financing. Second, the macro-fiscal unit should publish forecast-error histories and use conservative oil-production, price, exchange-rate, and tax-yield assumptions; major revisions should trigger a transparent supplementary budget rather than parallel execution. Third, each new borrowing proposal should disclose currency, effective interest cost, maturity and refinancing profile, collateral, guarantees, and the project or liability financed. Fourth, debt strategy should lengthen and smooth maturities where cost permits, favor concessional external finance for projects able to meet appraisal and procurement standards, and avoid instruments whose collateral or margin calls constrain later fiscal and exchange-rate choices. Finally, capital and social spending should be protected by selecting a smaller set of appraised projects and essential service commitments that can be financed through the medium-term cash envelope. The test is not the size of the approved capital budget but the share completed on time, within cost, and with a verified service outcome.

For understanding Nigeria, the implication is precise: the headline debt ratio can improve while fiscal capacity deteriorates. A credible assessment must jointly track the financing-based deficit, the revenue denominator, actual debt service, portfolio risks, and the conversion of borrowing into assets and services. Fiscal consolidation that simply cuts capital releases after optimistic budgeting would lower reported expenditure without repairing this mechanism. Sustainable adjustment instead requires realistic revenue forecasts, complete fiscal reporting, disciplined debt composition, and protected high-value spending—so that lower financing pressure results from better institutions and project choices rather than the repeated postponement of development.
