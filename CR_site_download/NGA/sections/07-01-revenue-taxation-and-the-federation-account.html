7.1 Government Revenue, Taxation, and the Federation Account

Nigeria's fiscal problem is not simply that government spends more than it collects. It is that the state must finance extensive federal, state, and local responsibilities from a revenue base that remains small, volatile, and unevenly administered. Oil and gas receipts, federally collected taxes, state internally generated revenue (IGR), and transfers through the Federation Account follow different rules and respond to different shocks. The central question is therefore whether recent revenue gains represent a durable expansion of fiscal capacity or a temporary improvement produced by exchange-rate valuation, oil-sector receipts, and exceptional transfers.

A revenue recovery that remains structurally narrow

The most recent IMF accounts show a clear recovery from the low point of the early 2020s, but also warn against treating that recovery as complete. Consolidated-government revenue and grants increased from 6.6 percent of GDP in 2022 to 10.8 percent in 2024. Non-oil revenue accounted for most of the increase, rising from 3.5 to 6.8 percent of GDP. Expenditure nevertheless remained above revenue, and the IMF estimates that consolidated revenue slipped to 10.2 percent of GDP in 2025 while expenditure remained at 12.0 percent. More importantly, the financing-based deficit reached 4.4 percent of GDP in 2025, well above the 1.8 percent above-the-line deficit, because the accounts contained an estimated statistical discrepancy of 2.7 percent of GDP. The discrepancy may capture spending outside the reporting perimeter and incomplete reconciliation across fiscal institutions; it is therefore a problem of fiscal information and control, not merely an accounting footnote (International Monetary Fund, 2026).

This improvement should also be distinguished from tax deepening. On the internationally comparable definition used in *Revenue Statistics in Africa 2025*, Nigeria's tax-to-GDP ratio was 8.2 percent in 2023, compared with an unweighted average of 16.1 percent across the 38 African countries covered. Nigeria's ratio was almost unchanged from 8.3 percent in 2013, whereas the African average rose by 1.4 percentage points over the decade (OECD/AUC/ATAF, 2025). The OECD tax measure is not interchangeable with the IMF's broader revenue-and-grants series: the former counts compulsory unrequited payments and has its own subnational coverage, while the latter also includes oil and other non-tax revenue. Read together, however, they show that recent aggregate revenue gains have not yet erased Nigeria's long-standing dependence on a narrow set of sources.

The composition of the 2024 increase reinforces that interpretation. World Bank calculations attribute the rise to the removal of the implicit foreign-exchange subsidy, improved tax administration, and stronger remittance of revenue by government-owned entities. Gross receipts collected by the principal Federation revenue agencies rose from ₦16.5 trillion in 2023 to ₦29.5 trillion in 2024. Yet gross collection is not the amount available for budgets: 43.5 percent of the 2024 total, or ₦12.8 trillion, was deducted before distribution, including collection costs, refunds, transfers, and other deductions. The same diagnostic found that NNPCL remitted only ₦0.6 trillion to FAAC in 2024, down from ₦1.1 trillion in 2023, while fuel-subsidy and arrears claims complicated the passage from oil receipts to usable public revenue (World Bank, 2025d). This is why claims based on gross agency collections, net Federation revenue, consolidated government revenue, and tax revenue can all be numerically different without one necessarily being false. Transparent reconciliation among them is itself a measure of fiscal capacity.

The Federation Account transmits both resources and risk

Section 162 of the Constitution places most federally collected revenue in the Federation Account for distribution among the three tiers of government. Under the subsisting vertical formula for net distributable Federation Account revenue excluding VAT, the federal government receives 52.68 percent, states 26.72 percent, and local governments 20.60 percent; oil-producing states also receive the constitutionally required 13 percent derivation from natural-resource revenue. VAT follows a separate sharing rule (National Bureau of Statistics, 2020). This system performs a necessary equalization function in a federation where taxable economic activity and administrative capacity are concentrated geographically. At the same time, it separates many subnational spending responsibilities from the taxes paid directly to the government delivering the service. The political and developmental consequences of centrally collected petroleum revenue and negotiated allocation rules have been identified for decades: the core problem is not transfer sharing by itself, but the combination of vertical fiscal imbalance, unstable resource revenue, and allocation rules whose implementation and development effects are politically contested (Ekpo, 1994; Phillips, 1991).

Recent state data show both sides of this arrangement. For the 34 states with complete quarterly budget implementation reports, total revenue increased from ₦7.2 trillion in 2023 to ₦13.9 trillion in 2024. FAAC transfers rose from ₦4.8 trillion to ₦9.5 trillion, while IGR reached ₦2.9 trillion. Oil-producing states captured a disproportionate share of the transfer windfall through derivation and oil-related refunds (World Bank, 2025d). The national IGR distribution was already highly concentrated before that windfall: the NBS recorded ₦2.43 trillion for the 36 states and the FCT in 2023, of which Lagos alone generated ₦815.86 billion, roughly one-third (National Bureau of Statistics, 2024). The contrast does not imply that every state should reproduce Lagos. Lagos combines a large formal payroll, dense property and consumption bases, and sustained investment in revenue administration that most states do not possess. It instead shows why a uniform demand for “more IGR” is analytically weak: the feasible tax base, administrative cost, and risk of coercive multiple taxation vary greatly across states.

Transfer dependence can weaken the incentive to build a taxpayer register and a visible fiscal contract, but the relationship is not automatic. Lagos research finds that linking direct taxation to recognizable service delivery can strengthen the perceived exchange between citizens and government, although the effect varies across social groups (Gatt & Owen, 2018). A recent field experiment in 112 Lagos markets further found that appeals delivered by state revenue officials sometimes increased registration and payment, whereas messages delivered by market associations did not, despite the associations' embedded local position (Gottlieb et al., 2025). Research in informal settlements adds an important qualification: trust was associated with tax morale but not necessarily with actual payment; ability to pay, legal liability, and community organization also mattered (Rosenzweig & Wilson, 2023). These studies are not nationally representative templates, but they establish a mechanism that aggregate revenue ratios cannot reveal. Administrative legibility, credible enforcement, service experience, and taxpayer treatment jointly shape compliance.

From legal consolidation to measurable collection capacity

The four Tax Acts signed in June 2025 and implemented from 2026 attempt to address fragmentation at the national level. The Nigeria Tax Act consolidates substantive tax rules; the Nigeria Tax Administration Act creates a more uniform administrative framework; the Nigeria Revenue Service (Establishment) Act replaces FIRS with the Nigeria Revenue Service; and the Joint Revenue Board (Establishment) Act formalizes coordination across federal, state, and local revenue authorities (State House, 2025). Transition guidelines apply the Nigeria Tax Act from 1 January 2026 and preserve pre-2026 liabilities under the former rules, thereby limiting retrospective application (Federal Ministry of Finance, 2026). A February 2026 executive order also directed specified oil and gas payments under production-sharing contracts to be remitted directly to the Federation Account, bypassing NNPCL (State House, 2026).

These changes improve the legal architecture, but enacted coordination is not yet demonstrated administrative integration. The IMF reports that the new package contains both revenue-raising measures and deliberate relief: the authorities estimated gains of 3.9 percent of GDP from policy changes and 3.1 percent from administration over three years, offset by 2.4 percent of GDP from expanded input credits, zero rating, and thresholds, for a net projected gain of 4.6 percent. These are ex ante estimates, not collected revenue. The IMF accordingly treats the yield from fiscalization, e-invoicing, Tax ID consolidation, and data cross-checking conservatively, while warning that additional rate or base changes should be sequenced with a funded and functioning cash-transfer system so that adjustment does not fall disproportionately on poor households (International Monetary Fund, 2026).

The appropriate policy test is therefore narrower and more demanding than a headline tax-to-GDP target. First, petroleum receipts, statutory deductions, refunds, and final distributions should be published in a reconciled chain so that Parliament, states, and citizens can trace gross collection into spendable revenue. Second, the new revenue institutions should be judged by operational measures—unique and validated Tax IDs, filing-to-payment gaps, e-invoice matching, audit yield net of reversals, dispute-resolution time, and the incidence of duplicate or unauthorized levies—rather than registrations or assessments alone. Third, subnational reform should combine national interoperability with tax instruments suited to local bases, especially well-administered personal-income and property taxation, while protecting informal and low-income taxpayers from repeated collection by overlapping authorities. In Nigeria's institutional setting, fiscal capacity will be strengthened not when every tier maximizes extraction independently, but when revenue assignment, transfer predictability, taxpayer rights, and visible service obligations reinforce one another.
