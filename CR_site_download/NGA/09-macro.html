9.1 GDP Scale, Growth, and Income Level

Nigeria's macroeconomic puzzle is not that the economy is small. It is that continental scale has not produced a comparably high or steadily rising command over resources per person. Three measures that are often collapsed into a single growth narrative therefore need to be kept separate. Nominal GDP measures the money value of production and helps describe the size of markets and some fiscal denominators. Real GDP growth removes domestic price change and shows whether the volume of output is expanding. Real output per person asks the more demanding question: whether production is growing faster than the population that must live from it. None of the three directly measures household income distribution, consumption, public-service quality, or poverty. A credible account of Nigerian growth must move among these measures without allowing one to stand in for the others.

A larger statistical economy is not an overnight increase in welfare

The first complication is measurement. In 2025 the National Bureau of Statistics (NBS) moved the national-accounts base year from 2010 to 2019 and expanded the production boundary and source information to reflect activities whose economic weight had changed. On the new basis, nominal GDP was estimated at ₦205.09 trillion in 2019—41.7 percent above the estimate produced with the previous base—and ₦372.82 trillion in 2024. Real growth in the rebased series was 3.38 percent in 2024. The full-year release for 2025 subsequently reported nominal GDP of approximately ₦441.5 trillion and real growth of 3.87 percent; the first-quarter 2026 release recorded year-on-year real growth of 3.89 percent (National Bureau of Statistics, 2025; National Bureau of Statistics, 2026a).

Rebasing improves the map of the economy; it does not create the newly measured output in the year the revision is published. Digital and financial services, real estate, informal production, and newer data sources may receive more appropriate weights, while historical growth rates and sector shares can change because the benchmark and methods changed. Jerven's study of national-account revisions in Sub-Saharan Africa shows why large revisions can reveal genuine undermeasurement while also reducing comparability across vintages, countries, and policy periods (Jerven, 2013). Nigeria's 2025 revision should thus change ratios whose denominator is GDP—revenue, debt, credit, health spending, or trade openness—without being interpreted as an equivalent improvement in tax administration, debt-service capacity, financial depth, or household welfare. For policy evaluation, the NBS and agencies using its data need published bridge tables that show the old and new series, coverage changes, deflators, and the point at which quarterly estimates become revised annual accounts.

Dollar GDP records exchange-rate translation as well as production

The second complication is currency conversion. World Bank Open Data reports Nigerian GDP of about US$290.79 billion and GDP per capita of US$1,224 in 2025, alongside real GDP growth of about 4.0 percent (World Bank Open Data, 2026). The simultaneous rise in real output and much lower dollar valuation than at the end of the 2010s is not contradictory. Current-dollar GDP converts a nominal naira aggregate at the exchange rate applicable to the series. A large naira depreciation can reduce the dollar value of GDP and GDP per capita even when the domestic volume of production is rising; domestic inflation can push nominal naira GDP in the opposite direction. Changes in population estimates and the national-accounts benchmark add further revisions. Current-dollar GDP is relevant to the foreign-currency scale of imports, external debt, and international market comparisons, but it is a poor stand-alone measure of changes in Nigerian living standards.

This is an exchange-rate-sensitive current-dollar series, not real purchasing power or median household income. Its post-2014 decline reflects the interaction of naira depreciation, nominal output, population growth, and data revisions; it should not be read as a one-for-one change in domestic real production.

The figure makes this distinction visible. It traces the World Development Indicators series for GDP per capita in current US dollars through 2024, not real purchasing power or the median Nigerian's income (World Bank, 2025a). The steep decline after the mid-2010s chiefly records the interaction of exchange-rate translation, nominal output, population growth, and data revisions. It should not be read as a one-for-one fall in real domestic production. Conversely, a future appreciation of the naira could lift current-dollar GDP without an equivalent productivity gain. This is why the government's aspiration for a trillion-dollar economy cannot be evaluated by a dollar threshold alone: the exchange rate and domestic price level can move the headline even when the underlying capacity to produce goods, services, jobs, and public revenue changes much less.

Purchasing-power-parity (PPP) measures answer a different comparative question by valuing output at a common price structure. The May 2025 Nigeria Development Update placed 2023 GDP per capita at US$6,207 in current international dollars, ranking Nigeria 146th among 191 economies with data and at about 30 percent of Botswana's level, the highest in continental Africa on that measure (World Bank, 2025). PPP is more suitable than market-exchange-rate conversion for comparing the volume of locally purchased goods and services, but it is still an average production measure. It neither shows distribution within Nigeria nor converts directly into the foreign currency available for machinery, medicine, debt service, or other imports. The large gap between current-dollar and PPP values is therefore not a choice between a “correct” and an “incorrect” GDP; it identifies two different constraints—domestic purchasing power and external command over resources.

Aggregate recovery remains too slow to transform income per person

The third complication is demographic arithmetic. A growth rate near 4 percent can sound rapid in a slow-growing high-income economy, but Nigeria's population is expanding by roughly 2 to 3 percent a year. The October 2025 Nigeria Development Update estimated real GDP per capita growth at only 1.6 percent in 2025 even though aggregate real GDP was projected to grow by more than 4 percent (World Bank, 2025). The IMF's 2025 baseline similarly put medium-term aggregate growth near 3.5 percent, equivalent to about 1.3 percent per person—too little for rapid poverty reduction (International Monetary Fund, 2025). The latest NBS releases show that the post-2023 economy has avoided recession and gained some momentum; they do not yet establish a break from this low per-capita-growth regime.

This distinction explains why macroeconomic stabilization and household experience can diverge. Removing fuel-price distortions, ending central-bank deficit financing, and improving foreign-exchange price discovery can reduce fiscal and external imbalances, but the transition also changes transport, food, credit, and imported-input costs. The April 2026 Nigeria Development Update judged that stabilization had progressed and growth remained resilient, largely through services, while household incomes had not fully recovered and poverty remained high (World Bank Group, 2026). That assessment is not evidence that reform cannot work. It shows that stabilization is an enabling condition whose distributive and productive transmission must be demonstrated: firms must invest and expand, workers must move into more productive activities, real earnings must rise, and public revenue gains must become reliable services or well-targeted protection.

The mechanism behind Nigeria's weak conversion of scale into income is cumulative. Rapid population growth dilutes modest output gains; high and volatile inflation shortens investment horizons and erodes real earnings; exchange-rate instability raises the naira cost of imported capital and intermediate goods; insecurity and unreliable power reduce productivity; and a large share of employment remains in low-productivity or vulnerable activities. These constraints also interact. For example, a manufacturer facing unreliable electricity and expensive imported inputs cannot respond to a more competitive exchange rate as readily as a firm with stable power, logistics, credit, and access to export markets. The result is not simply “growth without inclusion” as an outcome label, but a weak transmission chain from macroeconomic adjustment to productivity, employment, and household purchasing power. The subsequent sections examine the price, sectoral, and structural links in that chain.

Policy should target the conversion of scale, not the headline alone

The appropriate policy dashboard follows directly from this diagnosis. Nigeria should retain real GDP and nominal GDP for national accounting, but judge economic progress with a compact set of non-interchangeable outcomes: real GDP per capita; labor productivity by sector; employment and real earnings in activities capable of sustained productivity growth; household consumption and poverty measured with transparent price deflators; and the revenue actually available for public investment and social protection. Each measure needs a published vintage, denominator, and revision history. A nominal naira or dollar target can remain a communication device, but it should not determine whether a policy is judged successful.

The same logic changes the design of growth policy. In the 2025 medium-term projections, growth near 3.5 to 4 percent left little margin above population growth (International Monetary Fund, 2025; World Bank, 2025). Policy must therefore raise both the rate and the productivity content of expansion. That means protecting macroeconomic credibility while removing identifiable constraints on production—power reliability, transport and port costs, security, access to finance, skills, and predictable regulation—and testing whether reforms generate additional output per worker rather than only higher asset values or nominal turnover. Social protection is not separate from this strategy when high food and transport costs force households to withdraw children from school, reduce nutrition, or sell productive assets; those responses can lower the human and physical capital on which later growth depends.

Nigeria's economic size remains a major advantage: it supports a large internal market, a diversified service base, fiscal potential, and regional influence. Yet scale becomes development only when national accounting improvements are matched by real per-person gains and when those gains reach households through productive work and public capability. The analytical baseline for the rest of this chapter is therefore deliberately demanding: positive aggregate growth is evidence of recovery, not by itself evidence of transformed living standards.

9.2 Inflation, Exchange Rate, and Macroeconomic Stabilization

Nigeria's recent stabilization cannot be judged from a single inflation number or from whether the naira is temporarily appreciating. The relevant question is whether the policy regime can absorb external shocks, transmit monetary decisions, and protect real incomes without recreating the foreign-exchange rationing and central-bank deficit financing that weakened the previous framework. Inflation, the exchange rate, liquidity management, fiscal operations, and reserve accumulation therefore form one institutional system. The reforms since mid-2023 improved price discovery and removed important distortions, but initially shifted large adjustment costs onto households and firms. By 2025–26, inflation and exchange-market pressures had moderated, yet the durability and social legitimacy of the stabilization remained conditional rather than assured (International Monetary Fund, 2026a; World Bank Group, 2026).

Disinflation is real, but the level shock has not been reversed

The initial price shock was exceptionally large. Annual-average consumer-price inflation reached 33.2 percent in 2024, while the GDP deflator rose by 14.1 percent. These indicators are not substitutes: the CPI measures changes in the cost of a household consumption basket, whereas the deflator covers prices of domestically produced final output and changes with the composition of GDP. Their divergence is therefore analytically useful. It indicates that the purchasing-power shock facing consumers was substantially larger than the economy-wide output-price measure suggests, especially where imported food, fuel, transport, and intermediate inputs entered retail prices (World Bank, 2025a; International Monetary Fund, 2026a).

The CPI tracks the price of a household consumption basket, whereas the GDP deflator tracks prices of domestically produced final output. Their divergence is economically meaningful, but this historical WDI figure ends before Nigeria's rebased monthly CPI beginning in 2025 and must not be spliced to that series without an official backcast.

The subsequent decline in inflation constitutes disinflation, not a return to the old price level. The IMF estimates annual-average CPI inflation at 23.0 percent in 2025, down from 33.2 percent in 2024. On the rebased CPI series, year-on-year inflation fell for more than a year to 15.1 percent in February 2026 before rising slightly to 15.4 percent in March; core inflation was 16.2 percent and food inflation 14.3 percent in March. Tight monetary policy, greater foreign-exchange stability, and a better harvest contributed to this moderation, while the March increase showed continued exposure to global fuel and food shocks (International Monetary Fund, 2026a). A lower inflation rate means that prices rise more slowly; it does not restore the real wages and household consumption lost during the preceding surge. This distinction helps explain why improving macroeconomic indicators coexist with high poverty and incomplete household-income recovery (World Bank Group, 2026).

The statistical break also requires care. Nigeria rebased the CPI beginning in January 2025 and subsequently revised the linking reference period between the old and new series. The IMF judged the revised linking method consistent with international guidance and found that it raised the measured 2025 inflation path but did not overturn the direction of disinflation. Consequently, 2024 and 2025 values can describe the change in the inflation environment, but should not be treated as a perfectly homogeneous time series without using the official backcast. The figure above stops in 2024 because its World Development Indicators series predates the rebased monthly CPI. It is historical context, not a substitute for the newer NBS series (International Monetary Fund, 2026a).

Inflation has also become less persistent and less broadly diffused. IMF rolling estimates place the persistence coefficient near one after the 2016 depreciation but show it declining from mid-2023 to roughly 0.1 by early 2026. More than half of CPI components had fallen below the 20 and 25 percent inflation thresholds by late 2025, although more than half still exceeded 15 percent. The timing matters: the decline began before CPI rebasing, suggesting a genuine change in price dynamics, while the rebasing may have amplified the measured shift. Exchange-rate unification, lower global commodity pressure, and monetary tightening together made shocks less self-perpetuating, but the still-wide diffusion of double-digit inflation counsels against declaring price stability achieved (International Monetary Fund, 2026b).

Exchange-rate unification exchanged hidden rationing for visible pass-through

Before June 2023, multiple exchange-rate windows and a large parallel-market premium insulated the official rate from market-clearing pressures. That arrangement suppressed some measured pass-through at the official rate, but it did so by rationing foreign exchange, encouraging arbitrage, delaying import payments, and weakening the information content of the official price. Unification and the move toward a floating regime sharply depreciated the naira—the World Development Indicators period-average official rate rose from about ₦461 per US dollar in 2022 to about ₦1,479 in 2024—but also restored a more transparent link between scarcity, the exchange rate, and domestic prices (World Bank, 2025a; Fernandes & Stumphius, 2026).

The cost channel was immediate because firms import machinery, refined inputs, medicines, transport equipment, and other intermediate goods, while fuel-price reform raised transport and distribution costs. Earlier Nigerian research found incomplete long-run exchange-rate pass-through to consumer prices, with elasticities of 0.24–0.30 and stronger transmission to import prices than to the CPI (Bada et al., 2016). That result should not be mechanically imposed on the post-2023 regime. Using data through 2025, Fernandes and Stumphius find that pass-through became positive and economically meaningful after unification, particularly following external financial shocks, because the market rate again affected actual transactions. This is a regime effect: greater pass-through is partly the counterpart of removing an administratively cheap but scarce official dollar.

The reform therefore presents a trade-off, not a simple choice between stability and flexibility. Fixing or heavily managing the rate can postpone inflation transmission, but persistent misalignment rebuilds backlogs, premiums, and reserve losses. A flexible rate reveals the shock earlier and can function as an external adjustment mechanism, but only if credible monetary and fiscal policies prevent a one-off depreciation from becoming recurrent wage-price and expectation dynamics. The policy objective should consequently be an orderly market with limited intervention against disorderly conditions, rather than defending an undeclared level. Predictable rules, publication of intervention data, and deepening of the interbank market matter as much as the spot rate itself.

Monetary transmission has strengthened, but the operational framework remains incomplete

The Central Bank of Nigeria responded to high inflation with a historically tight stance. It lowered the Monetary Policy Rate by 50 basis points to 26.5 percent in February 2026 and retained that rate in July, while maintaining a 45 percent cash-reserve requirement for deposit-money banks (Central Bank of Nigeria, 2026). The modest easing did not imply that stabilization was complete: it followed substantial disinflation and left the policy rate positive in real terms on contemporaneous measures.

The more important institutional change is that the policy rate has begun to transmit more effectively after exchange-rate unification. Yet transmission remains incomplete because short-term liquidity conditions, very high reserve requirements, segmentation across money markets, and fiscal cash movements can disconnect the announced MPR from lending and deposit rates. The IMF's preferred sequence is therefore operational rather than rhetorical: use a short-term open-market instrument to anchor the overnight rate near the policy rate, introduce reserve averaging to manage daily liquidity fluctuations, normalize the CRR gradually, strengthen forecasts, and separate the inflation-target-setting process from the annual budget cycle (International Monetary Fund, 2026a; Fernandes & Stumphius, 2026). This sequence is preferable to relying indefinitely on a high headline MPR that does not consistently govern marginal funding costs.

Fiscal policy is part of the transmission mechanism. Ending routine central-bank financing of deficits removed an important source of monetary expansion, but large government deposits or withdrawals can still inject or drain liquidity. The IMF estimates that a 2025 drawdown of CBN deposits had a liquidity effect similar to earlier overdraft financing, even though the formal zero limit on new “ways and means” advances was observed. Stabilization thus requires consolidated cash forecasting and transparent reconciliation of fiscal balances, not merely compliance with a legal ceiling. Otherwise monetary policy must offset fiscal liquidity shocks at high interest cost, weakening both credibility and private credit (International Monetary Fund, 2026a).

Reserves are stronger, but gross stocks overstate immediately usable protection

Nigeria's external buffer improved materially. On the CBN definition, gross reserves rose from US$40.2 billion at end-2024 to US$45.8 billion at end-2025; net international reserves increased from US$23 billion to about US$35 billion. Gross reserves reached approximately US$49 billion on a 30-day moving-average basis by end-March 2026, while the naira was 10 percent stronger against the dollar than a year earlier. The 2025 current-account surplus, reduced refined-fuel imports, non-resident purchases of CBN open-market instruments, and Eurobond issuance all contributed (International Monetary Fund, 2026a).

These figures warrant qualified optimism. The IMF's definition of gross international reserves was about US$8 billion below the CBN's official gross figure at end-2025 because pledged or otherwise encumbered assets are treated differently. Part of accumulation also reflected portfolio inflows into high-yield naira instruments and external borrowing, not only durable export diversification. The IMF nevertheless estimated official gross reserves at 157 percent of its reserve-adequacy metric, above the usual 100–150 percent precautionary range. The correct diagnosis is therefore that external resilience improved, while the composition, liquidity, and funding source of reserves remain essential to assessing how much intervention capacity is actually available (International Monetary Fund, 2026a).

Stabilization policy must connect credibility to distribution and production

The evidence supports a conditional policy package. First, monetary restraint should remain data-dependent until both inflation persistence and diffusion fall, not merely until headline inflation records several lower observations. Second, exchange-rate flexibility should be preserved while interventions are limited to smoothing disorderly market conditions and reported transparently. Third, fiscal authorities should align deficit financing and cash management with the monetary stance; otherwise a high MPR will coexist with liquidity injections and costly sterilization. Fourth, reserve policy should publish gross, net, encumbered, and liquid positions under consistent definitions so that markets can distinguish genuine buffer accumulation from borrowed liquidity.

Distributional protection is not an optional add-on. Poor households spend a large share of income on food and transport and cannot hedge exchange-rate or fuel-price shocks. Yet broad price subsidies would recreate fiscal and foreign-exchange distortions. The more coherent response is temporary, verifiable, and targeted transfers tied to a continuously improved social registry, combined with measures that relax the domestic supply constraints identified in the inflation diagnosis: security on food-producing corridors, logistics and storage, reliable electricity, competitive fuel distribution, and predictable import rules. The April 2026 Nigeria Development Update similarly emphasizes maintaining credible macroeconomic policy while protecting vulnerable households and translating stabilization into recovered livelihoods (World Bank Group, 2026).

Nigeria has thus moved from a regime that concealed scarcity through administered prices and rationed foreign exchange toward one in which adjustment is more visible and monetary policy is potentially more effective. The early gains—lower inflation persistence, improved market functioning, a stronger reserve position, and a more responsive interest-rate channel—are significant. They do not yet amount to a complete social or institutional stabilization. Success should be judged by whether lower and less dispersed inflation is sustained, the exchange market remains liquid without rebuilding a premium, reserve quality improves, monetary and fiscal operations are coordinated, and real household incomes begin to recover.

9.3 Industrial Structure: Oil, Agriculture, Manufacturing, and Services

Nigeria's production structure is more diversified than its export and public-finance structure, but it has not yet generated enough high-productivity employment. This distinction is central to interpreting the economy. Oil and gas account for only a small share of domestic value added, while agriculture and services dominate output and employment. Yet petroleum still supplies a disproportionate share of foreign exchange and remains embedded in federal revenue bargaining. At the same time, workers have moved from agriculture mainly into services rather than manufacturing, but much of the receiving service economy is informal trade and personal activity rather than scalable, tradable, high-productivity business services. Structural transformation should therefore be assessed through four linked tests: changes in value-added shares, labor reallocation, within-sector productivity, and the capacity to earn foreign exchange and fiscal revenue.

Rebasing changed the measured structure, not the underlying constraints

The 2025 national-accounts rebasing is indispensable for reading sector shares. Using 2019 as the new base year, the National Bureau of Statistics expanded coverage to 46 industries and 217 products and incorporated newer business, agricultural, living-standard, and informal-sector information. In the new 2019 benchmark, agriculture accounted for 25.83 percent of constant-price GDP, industry 21.08 percent, and services 53.09 percent. By 2024, their respective shares were 27.81, 16.67, and 55.52 percent. Oil and gas fell from 5.85 to 3.38 percent, while the non-oil economy rose to 96.62 percent. These are real-output shares at 2019 basic prices; they should not be mixed with current-price shares or the older 2010-base series (National Bureau of Statistics, 2025).

The rebasing also changed which activities appear economically important. Better coverage moved real estate ahead of crude petroleum and natural gas in the 2019 ranking and substantially revised water transport, administrative support, entertainment, health, livestock, and forestry. It raised the measured informal-sector contribution to GDP from 41.4 to 42.5 percent. This is partly a statistical correction: previously undercounted activity became visible without being newly created. As Jerven's work on African national accounts emphasizes, revisions can alter levels and sector rankings while leaving underlying productivity and institutional constraints unresolved (Jerven, 2013). The policy lesson is not to discount the new data, but to avoid treating a larger measured service economy as proof of newly achieved modernization.

Agriculture is large because labor and livelihoods remain rural, not because productivity is high

Agriculture's roughly 28 percent real-output share coexists with employment of more than one-third of the population and extensive rural poverty. This combination points to low average labor productivity rather than agricultural strength alone. Crop production dominates the sector, but livestock, forestry, and fishing are economically and regionally important. In 2024, real crop production grew by 2.91 percent, while livestock contracted by 2.14 percent and fishing by 0.61 percent. The aggregate sector grew only 1.69 percent, below population growth and far below the rate required to raise rural incomes rapidly (National Bureau of Statistics, 2025).

The causes are institutional and spatial. Rain dependence and limited irrigation expose output to weather; insecure land rights reduce incentives and collateral; insecurity disrupts cultivation and transport; weak extension systems slow technology adoption; and poor feeder roads, electricity, storage, cold chains, and market information raise losses and transaction costs. The World Bank's 2026 AGROW project assessment identifies precisely these complementary constraints and notes that they deter private investment as well as lower farm productivity (World Bank, 2026a). This means that distributing inputs or restricting imports in isolation cannot deliver structural transformation. If transport, storage, security, land access, and competition remain unresolved, subsidies raise fiscal cost without reliably creating marketable surpluses or agro-processing linkages.

Agricultural upgrading nevertheless remains central. Cross-country research shows that measured gaps between agricultural and non-agricultural productivity narrow substantially when hours worked are considered, but do not disappear; rural nonfarm activity is also strongly linked to farm production (McCullough, 2017). The implication for Nigeria is not simply to move people out of agriculture. It is to raise seasonal utilization and value added per worker while building agro-processing, logistics, finance, and business services around viable production corridors. Such within-sector productivity gains can release labor voluntarily, expand food supply, lower inflation pressure, and create demand for more productive rural and urban services.

Industry is too small and internally heterogeneous to serve as a broad employment engine

The decline of industry's real share from 21.08 percent in 2019 to 16.67 percent in 2024 does not describe manufacturing alone. The broad category combines mining, oil and gas, manufacturing, utilities, and construction. Summing the NBS manufacturing subsectors yields only about 8.3 percent of real basic-price GDP in 2024. Food, beverages, and tobacco and textiles, apparel, and footwear are the largest manufacturing components, while electrical equipment, motor-vehicle assembly, metals, chemicals, plastics, and oil refining remain comparatively small. Industrial growth therefore may rise because oil output or construction recovers even when technological upgrading and factory employment remain weak (National Bureau of Statistics, 2025).

Manufacturing faces a mutually reinforcing constraint set: unreliable and expensive electricity, dependence on imported machinery and intermediate inputs, exchange-rate and working-capital risk, congested ports and corridors, limited long-term finance, policy reversals, and weak standards and supplier networks. These conditions divide the sector between a small number of large firms able to self-provide infrastructure and hedge risk and a much larger set of micro and small producers with low capital intensity. Local-content rules or import restrictions can create demand for domestic firms, but without competitive entry, time limits, performance targets, and access to reliable inputs they can also protect high-cost incumbents and raise downstream prices.

Nigeria thus requires a selective but disciplined industrial policy. Selection should follow observable coordination failures and learning potential, not the political visibility of individual firms. Agro-processing can link farm productivity to urban demand; pharmaceuticals and basic consumer goods can build scale in a large domestic market; construction materials can leverage infrastructure demand; and digitally enabled professional services can support manufacturing logistics, finance, and design. Support should be conditional on investment, productivity, supplier development, employment quality, and export or import-competing performance. Sunset clauses and published evaluations are necessary because Nigeria's political settlement has historically enabled successful reform in some sectors, such as telecommunications, while fragmenting or blocking upgrading elsewhere (Usman, 2020).

A service economy can transform productivity only if its composition changes

Services supplied 55.52 percent of real GDP in 2024 and generated the fastest broad-sector growth, 4.43 percent. But “services” spans very different production models. Trade represented 17.73 percent of real basic-price GDP, real estate 13.58 percent, telecommunications 8.06 percent, and financial institutions 2.41 percent. Professional, scientific, and technical services, administration and support, transport, entertainment, health, education, hospitality, and public administration made up the remainder in widely differing combinations of skill, formality, and tradability (National Bureau of Statistics, 2025).

Labor has moved in the same broad direction. By 2021, services employed more than half of Nigerian workers, compared with about one-third two decades earlier, while agriculture's share moved in the opposite direction. Average service labor productivity was more than 70 percent above agriculture, but much of the employment gain occurred in wholesale and retail trade and hospitality, where productivity can be close to agriculture. High-productivity finance, information and communications technology, and professional services absorb fewer workers and require skills and infrastructure that are unevenly distributed (World Bank, 2024). The result is service-led reallocation without a commensurate increase in median earnings.

This pattern is not uniquely Nigerian. Mensah et al. show that, across sub-Saharan Africa, workers leaving agriculture have moved mainly into services and particularly trade; those activities are generally less productive and more volatile than manufacturing and modern business services. Their analysis also demonstrates why aggregate service shares are insufficient: structural change can produce static gains when workers enter somewhat more productive activities while simultaneously generating dynamic losses if the receiving sectors experience weak productivity growth (Mensah et al., 2023). Nigeria's service expansion should therefore be disaggregated into tradable and non-tradable, formal and informal, high- and low-skill, and high- and low-productivity components.

The policy opportunity lies in linking modern services to the rest of the economy rather than treating them as an enclave. Telecommunications, fintech, logistics, creative industries, and professional services can export, diffuse technology, improve payments, and reduce coordination costs for farms and factories. They will not absorb labor at sufficient scale unless broadband and electricity become reliable, firms can formalize at reasonable cost, education and vocational systems supply relevant skills, and small producers gain access to platforms and finance. Conversely, policies that celebrate digital start-ups while neglecting trade logistics, competition, worker transitions, and basic skills will widen the divide between a high-productivity urban segment and the mass service economy.

Oil dependence is a leverage problem, not a GDP-share problem

Oil's 3.38 percent share of real GDP in 2024 might suggest that Nigeria has already diversified. That conclusion confuses production share with strategic leverage. Hydrocarbons remain central to export receipts, foreign-exchange liquidity, fiscal transfers, and investor risk perceptions. An oil-price or production shock can therefore move the exchange rate and public budgets even when most domestic output is non-oil. Usman's political-settlements analysis explains the apparent paradox: non-oil services can expand while revenue and elite bargaining remain organized around concentrated oil rents, producing episodic reform rather than a stable coalition for industrial upgrading (Usman, 2020).

Diversification should consequently be evaluated against a dashboard rather than one ratio. Relevant outcomes include non-oil export earnings, internally generated tax revenue, formal employment, productivity within agriculture and services, manufacturing value added and survival, domestic supplier linkages, and resilience to an oil shock. Lowering oil's GDP share through statistical revision or faster growth elsewhere is welcome but insufficient if the budget, foreign-exchange market, and federation account remain highly exposed.

The strategic objective is a connected production system. Higher agricultural productivity should feed competitive processing and logistics; manufacturing should generate supplier, technology, and employment spillovers; modern services should lower transaction costs and become exportable; and oil revenue should finance public goods rather than recurrent protection of specific firms. Policies should be judged by whether they relax the binding constraints linking these sectors, not by whether a favored sector's headline share rises. Nigeria's economy has diversified in what it produces, but its next phase of transformation depends on diversifying productivity, jobs, exports, and public revenue at the same time.

9.4 Growth Model and Structural Vulnerabilities

Nigeria's growth model combines a very large domestic market, entrepreneurial services, extensive agriculture, hydrocarbon foreign-exchange earnings, and a federal state with exceptionally limited revenue and uneven implementation capacity. The model can generate episodes of rapid aggregate expansion, especially when oil prices, reform momentum, and capital inflows align. Its weakness is that those episodes have not consistently raised productivity, formal employment, or public investment faster than demographic pressure. The central vulnerability is therefore not one external deficit or one commodity price. It is a set of feedback loops through which oil volatility, fiscal weakness, low private investment, infrastructure deficits, informality, insecurity, and climate shocks reproduce low per-capita gains.

Stabilization has improved resilience, but prosperity requires a different test

The reforms since 2023 repaired important macroeconomic foundations. Exchange-rate unification improved price discovery, central-bank financing of fiscal deficits was curtailed, monetary policy tightened, and external reserves strengthened. Real GDP growth reached about 4.0 percent in 2025, and the IMF projects 4.1 percent in 2026. These are meaningful improvements over the stagnation that followed the 2014 oil-price shock. Yet the World Bank reports that household incomes have not fully recovered and poverty remains high. Stabilization is thus a necessary platform for growth, not evidence that the growth model has already become inclusive (International Monetary Fund, 2026a; World Bank Group, 2026).

The gap is a first-pass arithmetic indicator of potential per-capita output growth. Population values are estimate-based, and the gap does not measure distribution, labor productivity, employment quality, or welfare.

The relevant arithmetic is the sustained gap between real output growth and population growth. When GDP grows only slightly faster than population, per-capita gains are small and easily erased by inflation, conflict, or distributional inequality. The historical figure shows why occasional high-growth years are insufficient: the policy test is whether the growth–population gap remains positive through commodity and political cycles. It should also be interpreted conservatively because national population estimates remain projection-based in the absence of a recent census and the GDP series has been rebased.

World Bank analysis of the decade through 2023 reaches a similar conclusion from the labor side. Real value added grew more slowly than the labor force in most sectors, output per worker fell, and even agricultural output per worker rose only 6.3 percent over the decade. Nigeria therefore needs not only faster GDP growth but a composition that raises productivity and earnings for workers entering the labor market (World Bank, 2025). The distinction rules out a nominal GDP target, an oil-output rebound, or a services boom as sufficient measures of success.

Five reinforcing loops make the model vulnerable

The first loop connects oil, foreign exchange, and fiscal operations. Oil's domestic GDP share is small, but its export and revenue leverage remains large. A negative price or production shock reduces foreign-exchange supply, places pressure on the naira and reserves, increases imported inflation and debt-service costs, and compresses federal and state budgets. Procyclical spending during favorable periods and abrupt adjustment during downturns then weaken public investment. Sala-i-Martin and Subramanian attribute Nigeria's long-run resource-curse pattern primarily to the effect of oil rents on institutional quality, while Usman shows that the distribution of political power produces selective and episodic reform rather than a uniform predatory or developmental state (Sala-i-Martin & Subramanian, 2013; Usman, 2020). These perspectives imply that stabilization funds or fiscal rules work only if revenue measurement, withdrawals, federation-account deductions, and project selection are transparent enough to constrain coalition bargaining.

The second loop runs from low public revenue to low state capability. Nigeria's rebased GDP makes revenue and development spending ratios appear even smaller relative to the economy. Limited revenue constrains electricity, transport, security, schools, health, water, and social protection; weak services in turn reduce productivity, compliance, and confidence in taxation. Interest obligations absorb a large share of federal revenue even when the debt-to-GDP ratio appears moderate. The resulting state can formulate ambitious plans but lacks reliable finance for maintenance and implementation. Raising rates alone is not a solution: administrative improvements, a broader base, reduced exemptions and leakages, and transparent intergovernmental use of revenue must precede or accompany higher burdens so that fiscal extraction strengthens rather than weakens the social contract.

The third loop links firm structure, finance, and infrastructure. Nigeria lacks enough mid-sized and large productive firms capable of organizing suppliers, adopting technology, exporting, and providing stable wage employment. The 2026 Streamlined Country Diagnostic reports foreign direct investment of only 0.6 percent of GDP in 2024 and private-sector credit equal to 21.3 percent of GDP, compared with a 47 percent average for lower-middle-income economies. Fewer than 5 percent of micro, small, and medium enterprises had access to formal credit, and they received only 1 percent of bank lending. At the same time, high tariffs on raw materials, intermediate inputs, and capital goods raised production costs (World Bank, 2026). Firms respond by remaining small, informal, vertically integrated, or dependent on self-provided power and logistics; those responses then weaken the tax base, worker training, supplier networks, and demand for long-term finance.

The fourth loop is demographic. The World Bank's 2026 diagnostic estimates that Nigeria has 63 million people aged 15–29 and that almost 60 million young people will reach working age over the next decade (World Bank, 2026). More than 92 percent of employed youth work informally without contracts or benefits (World Bank, 2026). Demographic scale can enlarge markets and innovation, but it becomes a vulnerability when the annual flow of entrants exceeds the capacity of productive firms and public systems to provide jobs and skills. The relevant policy problem is not open unemployment alone. Low-hours work, own-account survival activity, discouraged labor-force exit, and low earnings can coexist with a high measured employment rate. Job policy must therefore track productivity, hours, earnings, formality, and worker transitions rather than a single unemployment statistic.

The fifth loop connects insecurity, climate, food prices, and poverty. Violence restricts access to farms and markets, destroys assets, changes crop choice, discourages investment, and raises transport and security costs. Climate variability amplifies these effects through flooding, heat, water stress, erosion, and coastal risk. Micro-evidence from the Boko Haram insurgency shows that greater attack intensity reduced agricultural output and productivity, particularly for staple crops, without a corresponding fall in land use—consistent with a productivity shock rather than simple abandonment (Adelaja & George, 2019). Because poor households devote a high share of expenditure to food and transport, these supply shocks rapidly become inflation and welfare shocks. The 2026 Nigeria Country Climate and Development Report therefore treats adaptation, low-emission investment, and institutional capacity as components of development strategy rather than an environmental addendum (World Bank, 2026b).

These loops interact. Insecurity lowers food supply; food inflation raises poverty and fiscal pressure for subsidies; broad subsidies reduce funds for infrastructure and targeted protection; infrastructure deficits keep agriculture and firms unproductive; weak productivity narrows the tax base; and fiscal weakness reduces the state's ability to provide security and resilience. A policy aimed at one price or sector can fail because it shifts rather than breaks the loop.

The binding constraint is coordinated implementation, not a shortage of plans

Nigeria's Agenda 2050 and sector strategies identify higher productivity, diversification, infrastructure, human capital, private investment, and poverty reduction as linked objectives (Federal Ministry of Budget and Economic Planning, 2023). The recurring problem is simultaneous ambition without binding prioritization. Electricity reform, transport corridors, agricultural commercialization, industrial policy, skills, finance, and social protection are complementary, but the federal government cannot implement all components uniformly across 36 states and 774 local governments (Federal Republic of Nigeria, 1999).

A more credible strategy would organize policy around a small number of spatial and sectoral production systems. An agricultural corridor, for example, requires security, land and water governance, feeder roads, storage, standards, finance, processing, and market access in the same places and sequence. A manufacturing or digital-service cluster requires reliable power, customs and ports, broadband, skills, supplier finance, and predictable regulation. Concentrating complementary interventions can create demonstrable productivity gains, after which successful arrangements can be adapted elsewhere. This is not a case for permanent enclaves: fiscal transfers, procurement, competition rules, and learning systems should diffuse benefits and prevent local capture.

The public sector's role is to solve coordination and credibility problems that individual firms cannot solve, while avoiding discretionary protection without performance discipline. Industrial incentives should specify the market failure, beneficiary, duration, measurable investment or export condition, and exit rule. Infrastructure should be evaluated on service reliability and maintenance, not only capital completion. Skills programs should be tied to verified employer demand and placement. Social protection should be regular, targeted, and shock-responsive so that reform costs do not force households to sell productive assets or withdraw children from school.

A falsifiable scorecard for a new growth model

The claim that Nigeria has shifted to a more resilient and inclusive growth model should be tested against outcomes that can disconfirm it. The following scorecard connects each vulnerability to an observable result rather than a project announcement.

DimensionEvidence of improvementWarning that the old model persists
Per-capita productivityReal GDP and output per worker grow persistently faster than population and labor-force growthAggregate GDP rises while per-capita output or median earnings stagnate
Jobs and firmsFormal wage employment, hours, earnings, and the number of productive mid-sized firms increaseEmployment growth is concentrated in low-earning informal trade and own-account work
Fiscal capabilityNon-oil revenue finances higher-quality infrastructure, human-capital, maintenance, and targeted protectionInterest and recurrent commitments crowd out development spending despite a moderate debt ratio
External resilienceNon-oil exports, durable FDI, net reserves, and domestic supplier capability deepenReserve gains depend mainly on borrowing or short-term portfolio inflows and oil still drives adjustment
InclusionReal household consumption recovers and poverty, food insecurity, and regional gaps fallInflation declines but lost purchasing power and unequal service access persist
Climate and securityCrop losses, displacement, outage time, and disaster recovery costs fall in exposed areasRecurrent shocks repeatedly trigger food inflation, emergency subsidies, and capital destruction

This scorecard also clarifies sequencing. Macroeconomic stability and predictable prices are the entry condition. Revenue administration, expenditure credibility, and shock-responsive protection make stabilization politically sustainable. Reliable infrastructure, finance, competition, and skills permit firms and farms to raise productivity. Export and supplier performance then test whether support generated capabilities rather than rents. Climate adaptation and security must be embedded throughout because they determine whether gains survive.

Nigeria's current position is therefore neither a failed reform story nor a completed transition. The post-2023 framework has reduced several distortions and created a better platform for investment, but the structural loops that connect oil, low revenue, small firms, demographic pressure, insecurity, and climate exposure remain. The next phase should be judged less by the announcement of a trillion-dollar economy or by a temporary macroeconomic surplus than by sustained per-capita productivity, more and better jobs, resilient non-oil revenue and exports, stronger public services, and measurable recovery in household welfare.
