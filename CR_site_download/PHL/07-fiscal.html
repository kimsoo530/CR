Fiscal capacity is the point at which state ambition becomes administratively real. In the Philippines, the structure of government revenue, tax revenue, and expenditure reveals how far the state can finance public services, infrastructure, social protection, public employment, debt service, and intergovernmental transfers through recurring fiscal resources rather than unstable borrowing, temporary windfalls, or administrative compression. For comparative public administration, the relevant issue is therefore not only the size of the budget but the relationship among revenue mobilization, expenditure commitments, debt-service obligations, public financial management, and the credibility of implementation.

The available World Bank indicator package suggests a state with moderate but still constrained fiscal capacity. Revenue excluding grants reached 16.70 percent of GDP in 2024, an increase of 2.26 percentage points from 2014. Tax revenue reached 14.37 percent of GDP in 2024, an increase of 1.356 percentage points from 2014. These figures imply that the Philippines has improved its ability to mobilize domestic revenue over the decade, but the increase remains administratively limited when set against the scale of development needs. A tax-to-GDP ratio in the mid-teens indicates that the formal fiscal state is able to collect recurring resources, yet it also suggests a relatively narrow fiscal margin for simultaneously expanding infrastructure, social protection, health, education, disaster resilience, and debt service. The administrative meaning of the trend is therefore mixed: revenue capacity has strengthened, but not enough to remove hard budget constraints from the development state.

Government expense provides the expenditure-side counterpart. Government expenses were 16.88 percent of GDP in 2023, having increased by 3.673 percentage points from 2013. This rise indicates that the expenditure responsibilities of the state have expanded faster than some measures of revenue capacity. Such an increase may reflect greater programmatic ambition, higher operating costs, expanded transfers, infrastructure-related implementation needs, or post-crisis spending pressures. Administratively, however, rising expenditure is not automatically equivalent to stronger state capacity. It improves capacity only when accompanied by reliable procurement, timely budget release, effective accounting, audit discipline, and the ability of agencies and local governments to convert appropriations into delivered services. If expenditure rises faster than the institutions that manage it, the result can be underspending in capital projects, weak maintenance, arrears, leakage, or politically visible programs that are difficult to sustain.

The distinction between formal rules and implementation practice is central. Formally, the Philippines operates through legal budget authorization, revenue laws, debt-management rules, audit institutions, procurement rules, and intergovernmental fiscal arrangements. These rules create the architecture of a modern public financial management system: taxes are legislated, budgets are appropriated, expenditures are classified, debt is recorded, and public agencies are subject to reporting and oversight. In practice, however, the effectiveness of this architecture depends on administrative enforcement. Tax laws may exist, but compliance, audit coverage, taxpayer registration, digital enforcement, customs administration, and the political treatment of exemptions determine actual collections. Budget rules may require discipline, but project selection, procurement delays, absorptive capacity, and local implementation quality determine whether expenditure produces development outcomes. The formal state defines fiscal authority; the implemented state determines fiscal capacity.

The revenue structure also reveals the institutional basis of state capacity. The gap between revenue excluding grants and tax revenue shows that non-tax revenue and other public income remain relevant, but tax revenue is the core recurring base for long-term public administration. Because tax revenue was 14.37 percent of GDP in 2024, the central question is whether tax policy and tax administration can generate enough predictable income without undermining investment, household welfare, or political legitimacy. A narrow or weakly enforced tax base constrains development strategy because it forces the government to choose among competing priorities: infrastructure versus social protection, salary increases versus maintenance, universal programs versus targeted transfers, and national projects versus local service delivery. A broader and more reliable tax base gives the state greater strategic autonomy; a weaker one increases dependence on borrowing, fees, asset sales, or politically negotiated expenditure cuts.

Debt and interest payments show the fiscal consequences of these choices. The available package reports central government debt at 43.43 percent of GDP in 2014, a decline of 28.17 percentage points from 2004. This historical decline indicates that, before more recent shocks and borrowing cycles, the Philippines had created fiscal space through debt reduction. The administrative significance of that earlier consolidation is important: lower debt can improve credibility, reduce rollover risk, and free resources for development expenditure. However, the package does not provide a newer central-government debt figure after 2014, so any current claim about the latest debt ratio should be checked against updated official or international data before being used as evidence. The section should therefore distinguish clearly between the documented historical improvement through 2014 and the unresolved question of the current debt position.

Interest payments are a more recent indicator of fiscal constraint. Interest payments were 17.29 percent of revenue in 2024, an increase of 0.292 percentage points from 2014. The level is administratively significant because it shows that a substantial share of government revenue is pre-committed before agencies can finance new services, capital maintenance, or reform programs. Even though the decade-long increase is small, the level itself matters: when interest consumes a large portion of revenue, budget flexibility declines. This affects policy credibility because governments may announce ambitious programs while the usable fiscal envelope is already narrowed by debt-service obligations. It also increases sensitivity to interest-rate changes, exchange-rate movements, refinancing conditions, and revenue underperformance.

The macroeconomic context partly explains both opportunity and constraint. GDP reached US$461.62 billion in 2024, an increase of US$164.13 billion from 2014, while GDP per capita reached US$3,985 in 2024, an increase of US$1,118 from 2014. GDP growth was 5.692 percent in 2024, down 0.656 percentage points from 2014. These figures indicate that the fiscal base has expanded with the economy, giving the state a larger nominal revenue pool. Yet the per capita level also indicates that the Philippines remains a developing middle-income fiscal state: public expectations are rising, but the tax base is still limited compared with the demands of infrastructure, social mobility, climate adaptation, health, education, and regional equalization. Growth gives the state room to expand revenue without only raising rates, but slower or uneven growth would expose the weakness of relying on buoyant economic expansion rather than deep administrative improvements in taxation and expenditure management.

Public financial management is therefore the hinge between fiscal resources and state capacity. A government may raise revenue and appropriate expenditure, but state capacity emerges only when financial systems can allocate resources to priority sectors, release funds predictably, procure goods and infrastructure efficiently, prevent misuse, and evaluate outcomes. The Philippines' fiscal challenge is not merely collection but conversion: converting taxes into credible budgets, budgets into executed programs, and programs into measurable development outcomes. This is especially important in a decentralized and archipelagic administrative setting, where local capacity, geographic fragmentation, disaster exposure, and uneven economic development can weaken the link between national fiscal policy and service delivery on the ground.

The governance indicators in the package reinforce this interpretation. The World Governance Indicator for Government Effectiveness stood at 0.108 in 2023, down 0.244 from 2013, while Regulatory Quality stood at 0.115 in 2023, up 0.076 from 2013. These indicators should not be overinterpreted as precise measures of fiscal administration, but they are useful signals. The decline in government effectiveness suggests that administrative performance may not have improved in line with the expansion of expenditure responsibilities. The modest improvement in regulatory quality suggests some strengthening in the formal or policy environment, but this does not necessarily guarantee better implementation. The contrast captures a common comparative-administration dilemma: reforms may improve rules, frameworks, and market-facing regulation while the delivery apparatus remains uneven.

State enterprises and fiscal risks should be understood within this same framework, even where the supplied statistics do not give enterprise-specific values. Government-owned or controlled corporations, public financial institutions, infrastructure entities, and other state-linked enterprises can support development strategy by financing infrastructure, providing utilities, stabilizing strategic sectors, or extending services beyond what private markets supply. Yet they may also create contingent liabilities when they borrow, receive guarantees, accumulate losses, depend on transfers, or undertake quasi-fiscal activities outside the ordinary budget. The analytical issue is whether these entities are transparently incorporated into fiscal-risk reporting and public investment planning. Without such transparency, the formal deficit and debt indicators may understate the state's true exposure.

The historical and structural causes of the Philippine fiscal pattern are likely to include a combination of development needs, decentralization, informality, uneven regional capacity, disaster vulnerability, infrastructure backlogs, and the political difficulty of broadening tax collection. These structural conditions produce current institutional consequences. First, agencies face pressure to expand programs while maintaining fiscal discipline. Second, the government must balance growth-oriented capital spending with recurrent costs such as salaries, transfers, operations, maintenance, and interest. Third, reform credibility depends less on announcing fiscal targets than on demonstrating that revenue measures, procurement systems, debt management, and local implementation can operate consistently across political cycles. Fourth, fiscal risk management becomes a central component of administrative modernization, because shocks can rapidly convert hidden liabilities into explicit budgetary burdens.

For comparative administration, the Philippines should be read neither as a minimal state nor as a fully consolidated high-capacity fiscal state. It is better understood as a revenue-improving but expenditure-pressured state whose development strategy depends on whether fiscal institutions can keep pace with policy ambition. Compared with states that collect a much larger share of GDP in taxes, the Philippines has less room for universal welfare expansion and large-scale infrastructure without careful prioritization. Compared with lower-capacity states, however, the documented increase in revenue excluding grants and tax revenue suggests an administratively meaningful base on which further reform can build. The core comparative lesson is that fiscal capacity is cumulative: revenue administration, expenditure control, debt credibility, and public investment management reinforce one another over time, but weakness in any one of these areas can reduce the effectiveness of the whole fiscal system.

Several recent evidence checks would strengthen this section before final publication. The most important are updated figures for general government revenue, general government expenditure, fiscal balance, and debt, because the supplied central-government debt statistic ends in 2014 and cannot support claims about the current debt burden. The section should also verify the institutional coverage of "revenue excluding grants," "tax revenue," and "government expense," since World Bank central-government series may differ from broader general-government measures. Evidence should be checked from the World Bank or comparable international databases for revenue and expenditure as a percent of GDP, and from official fiscal or debt sources for the latest debt and interest burden. The best statistics to test the section's claims would include tax-to-GDP ratio, revenue excluding grants as a share of GDP, expenditure or expense as a share of GDP, fiscal deficit as a share of GDP, public debt as a share of GDP, interest payments as a share of revenue, capital expenditure execution rates, procurement delay indicators, tax compliance measures, and reported contingent liabilities from state enterprises or guarantees.

The institutional dilemma the section must resolve is therefore how the Philippines can expand development-oriented expenditure while preserving fiscal credibility and improving implementation quality. A narrow answer focused only on raising revenue would be incomplete, because more revenue without expenditure discipline may finance inefficiency. A narrow answer focused only on spending restraint would also be incomplete, because excessive restraint can weaken infrastructure, social services, and administrative reach. The more precise dilemma is how to build a fiscal state that is simultaneously more extractive, more developmental, more transparent, and more credible. Future reform risks arise if revenue gains stall, if interest obligations crowd out program spending, if state-enterprise liabilities remain insufficiently visible, or if public financial management reforms improve formal procedures without improving actual delivery. Conversely, successful reform would strengthen state capacity by widening the recurring revenue base, improving the execution of priority expenditure, containing fiscal risks, and making policy commitments more believable to citizens, investors, local governments, and international partners.

Fiscal balance, debt, and interest burden should be interpreted as mutually reinforcing indicators of the Philippine state's fiscal and administrative capacity. The fiscal balance indicates whether current revenues are sufficient to finance current expenditure commitments. Debt records the accumulated stock of past deficits, crisis responses, infrastructure financing, and debt-management choices. Interest payments reveal the extent to which present fiscal authority has already been pre-committed to obligations created by earlier policy decisions and macroeconomic conditions. For a comparative-administration country report, the central issue is therefore not only whether the Philippines can meet its formal debt-service obligations, but whether the fiscal system gives the public sector enough discretionary capacity to finance development, maintain credibility with creditors, respond to shocks, and deliver ordinary public services without recurring compression of maintenance, personnel, investment, or transfers.

The Philippines presents a fiscal profile of gradual revenue improvement, expanded expenditure commitments, persistent interest pressure, and incomplete evidence on the current debt stock. Revenue excluding grants reached 16.70 percent of GDP in 2024, an increase of 2.26 percentage points from 2014. Tax revenue reached 14.37 percent of GDP in 2024, up 1.356 percentage points from 2014. These figures suggest that domestic resource mobilization has strengthened over the decade, but the level remains administratively moderate relative to the country's development demands. The fiscal meaning of this trend is that the state has acquired somewhat more capacity to finance infrastructure, social protection, education, health, climate adaptation, and local transfers from domestic resources. However, the improvement is not large enough to remove the structural tension between the scale of policy commitments and the revenue base available to support them.

The distinction between revenue excluding grants and tax revenue is analytically important. Revenue excluding grants measures the broader recurring resource base available to the state, while tax revenue captures the compulsory fiscal extraction capacity of the public administration. In 2024, the gap between total revenue excluding grants at 16.70 percent of GDP and tax revenue at 14.37 percent of GDP indicates that non-tax revenues still form a meaningful supplement to taxation. From an administrative perspective, this matters because taxes are usually the more durable foundation of state capacity: they require registration, assessment, enforcement, compliance management, audit capacity, and political legitimacy. Non-tax revenues may be useful, but they do not necessarily create the same long-term administrative relationship between state and society. The reported rise in tax revenue therefore indicates institutional progress, but it also implies that the Philippines still faces the harder task of deepening a broad, reliable, and politically sustainable tax base.

Expenditure trends reveal the other side of the fiscal constraint. Government expenses reached 16.88 percent of GDP in 2023, an increase of 3.673 percentage points from 2013. This rise indicates a larger public-sector footprint. Administratively, the increase may reflect a combination of personnel costs, social expenditure, transfers, operations, debt-service-related spending, and development-related commitments. The available indicator does not disaggregate these categories, so it should not be used to claim that the increase is driven by any one component. Its main significance is that the Philippine state has taken on a broader expenditure role. When expenditure commitments expand faster than durable revenue capacity, fiscal balance becomes more dependent on borrowing, temporary revenues, optimistic growth assumptions, or future tax effort. The Philippines therefore illustrates a common middle-income administrative problem: citizens, firms, local governments, and development planners expect the state to perform more functions before the revenue system and implementation machinery have fully matured.

The available evidence does not provide a current debt ratio. Central government debt is reported at 43.43 percent of GDP in 2014, after a decline of 28.17 percentage points from 2004. This historical statistic is still useful because it indicates that the Philippines had achieved a significant period of debt consolidation before the later shocks and borrowing needs that are not captured by the supplied debt series. Yet the 2014 value is too old to support a direct conclusion about the present debt burden. A rigorous assessment must therefore update the debt evidence before making claims about contemporary sustainability. The most important missing statistics are current central-government debt, general-government gross debt, net debt where available, the overall fiscal balance, the primary balance, debt-service costs, debt maturity, refinancing needs, domestic versus external composition, currency exposure, and the share of fixed- versus floating-rate obligations.

The absence of a current debt-stock value should shape the interpretation rather than be treated as a minor data gap. Debt sustainability is not determined by one ratio alone. It depends on the interaction among growth, interest rates, exchange-rate conditions, primary balances, investor confidence, contingent liabilities, and the credibility of fiscal institutions. The Philippines had GDP of US$461.62 billion in 2024, an increase of US$164.13 billion from 2014. GDP per capita reached US$3,985 in 2024, up US$1,118 from 2014. Real GDP growth was 5.692 percent in 2024, 0.656 percentage points lower than in 2014. These figures indicate that the economy continued to expand, thereby enlarging the potential tax base and helping contain debt ratios through denominator growth. However, growth does not automatically produce fiscal resilience. If economic expansion occurs in sectors that are difficult to tax, if informality remains significant, if compliance capacity lags behind economic change, or if public spending commitments rise faster than revenue, fiscal pressure may persist despite strong aggregate growth.

The strongest current signal in the evidence package is the interest burden. Interest payments absorbed 17.29 percent of revenue in 2024, only 0.292 percentage points higher than in 2014. The small change over the decade suggests persistence rather than sudden deterioration. This is an important qualification: the evidence does not show an explosive rise in the interest burden over the period covered. Nevertheless, the level is administratively consequential. When roughly one-sixth of revenue is absorbed by interest payments, a significant portion of fiscal authority is already committed before agencies finance teachers, health workers, infrastructure maintenance, social programs, digital systems, local transfers, disaster preparation, or regulatory enforcement. Interest payments are politically and administratively rigid. Unlike some capital projects or discretionary programs, they cannot be easily postponed without damaging creditworthiness. The interest burden therefore acts as a prior claim on the budget and reduces the capacity of elected officials and bureaucratic agencies to reallocate resources in response to new priorities.

The interaction between revenue, expenditure, and interest payments helps explain the institutional dilemma. The Philippines has improved revenue mobilization, but the expenditure state has also expanded, and interest costs remain a substantial pre-commitment of public resources. The fiscal balance cannot therefore be understood as a narrow accounting result. It reflects the political settlement between taxpayers and beneficiaries, the implementation capacity of tax and budget agencies, the credibility of debt management, and the development strategy pursued by the state. A fiscal strategy that protects credibility by suppressing expenditure may preserve headline ratios but fail to finance infrastructure, human capital, disaster resilience, and administrative modernization. Conversely, a strategy that accelerates borrowing for development may produce short-term investment gains while increasing future debt-service pressure and reducing flexibility. The institutional problem is to finance a more capable developmental state without allowing debt service and contingent liabilities to crowd out the very administrative capacities that development requires.

A specialist should distinguish formal fiscal rules from implementation practice. Formally, fiscal balance is managed through revenue legislation, annual budget preparation, congressional authorization, debt-management procedures, auditing, procurement rules, cash management, and medium-term fiscal planning. These institutions define the official sequence by which revenues are projected, expenditures are authorized, borrowing is approved, funds are released, contracts are procured, and obligations are recorded. In practice, fiscal outcomes depend on whether revenue forecasts are realistic, tax administration is effective, procurement is timely, agencies can execute appropriations, capital projects are selected and completed efficiently, local governments coordinate with national priorities, and fiscal risks are reported before they become budgetary emergencies. The formal framework may state a credible fiscal path, but implementation practice determines whether that path is converted into actual collections, completed infrastructure, service delivery, and debt containment.

The governance indicators reinforce this distinction. Government Effectiveness stood at 0.108 in 2023, a decline of 0.244 from 2013. Regulatory Quality stood at 0.115 in 2023, an increase of 0.076 from 2013. The contrast suggests that the rule-setting environment may have improved modestly while perceived implementation effectiveness weakened. This does not prove that fiscal administration itself deteriorated, because these governance indicators are broad and not limited to public finance. However, they raise an important administrative caution: better rules, plans, or regulatory frameworks do not automatically generate stronger public-sector performance. Fiscal expansion improves state capacity only when funds are translated into reliable outputs. If implementation systems are weak, additional expenditure may produce arrears, delayed projects, low-quality procurement, fragmented programs, or under-maintained assets rather than durable development gains.

The Philippine fiscal position also has structural causes. The country combines a growing economy with substantial development needs, vulnerability to shocks, and uneven administrative reach. Rising GDP and GDP per capita expand the potential fiscal base, but the public sector must still convert that potential into actual revenue through compliance, enforcement, taxpayer services, and credible use of funds. Developmental demands include infrastructure, education, health, urban services, connectivity, social protection, and disaster resilience. These demands are recurrent rather than temporary. They require not only capital outlays but also maintenance budgets, staffing, monitoring, and intergovernmental coordination. A state that borrows to build infrastructure but fails to finance maintenance, right-of-way processes, procurement quality, or local implementation may increase liabilities without fully realizing development returns.

Public financial management is therefore central to the interpretation of fiscal balance. The administrative issue is not simply whether expenditure is high or low, but whether the budget process allocates scarce resources to high-return uses and whether agencies can execute those allocations. In a constrained fiscal environment, weak project appraisal, delayed procurement, cost overruns, or low absorptive capacity can be as damaging as insufficient revenue. They reduce the developmental value of each peso spent and weaken the political legitimacy required for future tax effort. Conversely, credible budgeting, transparent reporting, timely audit, and disciplined debt management can increase policy credibility even when fiscal space is limited. Public financial management is the mechanism through which fiscal capacity becomes state capacity.

State enterprises and other quasi-fiscal channels must also be integrated into the analysis. The headline budget does not capture all fiscal risks. Public corporations, government guarantees, public-private partnerships, regulated utilities, infrastructure commitments, and possible support for financially stressed entities can shift liabilities onto the state. Such entities may support development strategy by delivering infrastructure, finance, transport, energy, or other strategic services. However, they may also create hidden fiscal pressure if debts, guarantees, losses, or underpriced services eventually require public support. A comparative-administration assessment should therefore examine whether these risks are visible, quantified, reported, priced, and incorporated into medium-term fiscal planning. The strength of the fiscal system depends not only on the annual deficit but also on the state's capacity to discipline off-budget obligations.

The supplied statistics suggest several propositions that should be tested with recent evidence. First, revenue capacity has improved but remains moderate: this should be tested through updated revenue-to-GDP, tax-to-GDP, tax buoyancy, compliance, arrears, and tax-expenditure data. Second, expenditure commitments have expanded: this should be tested by separating personnel spending, maintenance and operating expenditure, transfers, capital expenditure, subsidies, and interest. Third, debt cannot be assessed with confidence using only the 2014 central-government debt figure: current central-government and general-government debt ratios are essential. Fourth, the interest burden is persistent and substantial: this should be tested using interest payments as a share of revenue, GDP, and total expenditure, as well as the effective interest rate on government debt. Fifth, implementation quality may be lagging behind formal fiscal ambition: this should be tested with budget execution rates, procurement timelines, project completion data, audit findings, and service-delivery indicators.

The fiscal balance also affects policy credibility. Creditors, investors, implementing agencies, and citizens evaluate whether the government's fiscal promises are believable. If revenue forecasts are overstated, expenditure commitments are repeatedly underfunded, or debt projections ignore contingent liabilities, credibility weakens. If fiscal consolidation is pursued through abrupt cuts to maintenance, investment, or local transfers, service delivery may deteriorate and reform legitimacy may erode. The credibility problem is therefore two-sided. The state must convince creditors that it can service debt and convince citizens that fiscal discipline does not mean abandoning development. This dual credibility requirement is especially important for a country seeking to sustain growth while expanding infrastructure and social capacity.

The interest burden has direct consequences for development strategy. A 17.29 percent interest-to-revenue ratio in 2024 indicates that a large share of revenue is unavailable for new policy choices. This constrains the sequencing of reforms. Programs that generate long-term returns but require upfront spending - such as infrastructure maintenance, education quality, health systems, disaster adaptation, and administrative digitalization - may face competition from mandatory debt service. The result can be a bias toward projects with visible short-term political value or externally financed initiatives, rather than less visible investments in bureaucratic capacity. Over time, this can weaken the state's ability to implement development strategy even when macroeconomic growth remains positive.

The section should therefore resolve a central institutional dilemma: how can the Philippines broaden fiscal capacity for development while preserving debt credibility and preventing interest costs from crowding out implementation capacity? This dilemma cannot be resolved by a simple preference for austerity or expansion. A low-spending state may maintain conservative fiscal ratios but fail to provide the infrastructure and human-capital foundations of long-term development. A high-borrowing state may accelerate investment but increase refinancing risk, interest pressure, and vulnerability to shifts in market sentiment. The more credible administrative solution requires a medium-term fiscal framework that links revenue measures, expenditure prioritization, debt management, and implementation capacity. It also requires explicit treatment of fiscal risks from state enterprises, guarantees, and public-private partnerships.

For comparative administration, the Philippines should be classified as a state with improving but constrained fiscal capacity. Revenue and tax ratios show progress, nominal GDP expansion has enlarged the potential fiscal base, and real growth remains supportive of debt sustainability. At the same time, the interest burden continues to absorb a substantial share of revenue, the current debt stock cannot be inferred from the supplied evidence, and broad governance indicators warn against assuming that expanded fiscal resources automatically produce stronger administrative performance. The country's fiscal system thus reveals a broader state-capacity challenge: the public sector must increase reliable revenue, protect priority expenditure, manage debt risks, discipline quasi-fiscal liabilities, and improve execution quality at the same time.

The most important evidence to check before finalizing the report is recent and disaggregated. The report should update the overall fiscal balance, primary balance, general-government gross debt, central-government debt, debt-service ratio, interest payments as a percentage of GDP, interest payments as a percentage of revenue, and interest payments as a percentage of expenditure. It should also examine debt maturity, refinancing concentration, domestic and external debt composition, currency risk, contingent liabilities, guarantees, public-private partnership exposure, and the financial position of major state enterprises. On the revenue side, the most useful indicators would be tax buoyancy, revenue by major tax type, tax expenditures, compliance gaps, and the distribution of collections across sectors. On the expenditure side, the key tests would be capital-expenditure execution, maintenance spending, personnel costs, subsidy burdens, arrears, procurement delays, and audit findings. These statistics would determine whether fiscal pressure is driven mainly by weak revenue mobilization, expanded recurrent commitments, development-related investment needs, higher interest costs, contingent liabilities, or implementation inefficiency.

The administrative conclusion is that fiscal balance, debt, and interest burden should be read as indicators of the state's room for maneuver. The Philippines has gained revenue capacity, but not enough to make fiscal space abundant. It has expanded expenditure, but the developmental quality of that expansion depends on implementation. It has a persistent interest burden that constrains discretion even without evidence of a sharp recent deterioration in the supplied series. It has historical evidence of debt consolidation, but the current debt position must be updated before any firm judgment is made. The future credibility of reform will depend on whether the government can convert a growing economy into reliable revenue, convert expenditure into visible public value, and maintain a debt path that preserves confidence while still financing the foundations of long-term development.

The fiscal system of the Philippines links revenue capacity to policy ambition, and public financial management is the administrative mechanism through which this link is made credible. A specialist should therefore understand Philippine budget reform not only as a technical effort to improve accounting, procurement, audit, and expenditure control, but as a central component of state capacity. Revenue, expenditure, debt, fiscal risk, state-owned and state-controlled enterprises, and the credibility of budget execution determine whether the state can finance development plans, maintain macroeconomic stability, and implement policy consistently across agencies and levels of government.

The available indicators suggest a fiscal system that has increased its resource base but still faces binding constraints. Revenue excluding grants reached 16.70 percent of GDP in 2024, an increase of 2.26 percentage points from 2014. Tax revenue reached 14.37 percent of GDP in 2024, up 1.356 percentage points from 2014. Administratively, this implies an improvement in domestic revenue mobilization, but not a transformation large enough to remove the tension between infrastructure, social policy, security spending, debt service, and subnational transfers. The fiscal basis of state capacity remains moderate: the government has more room than in an earlier low-revenue equilibrium, but its policy commitments still compete within a limited fiscal envelope. The central question is therefore whether revenue reform has produced a durable administrative capacity to assess, collect, and enforce taxes, or whether gains depend heavily on macroeconomic growth, consumption patterns, or temporary compliance improvements.

Expenditure trends reinforce this interpretation. Government expenses stood at 16.88 percent of GDP in 2023, an increase of 3.673 percentage points from 2013. This rise indicates that the Philippine state has expanded its spending role, but it also raises the administrative stakes of budget formulation and execution. A larger expenditure state requires stronger commitment controls, cash management, procurement discipline, internal audit, and performance monitoring. Without these systems, increased spending may produce arrears, delayed projects, weak value for money, or fragmented implementation rather than stronger service delivery. Budget reform should therefore be assessed less by the formal adoption of modern instruments than by whether agencies can convert appropriations into timely, lawful, and developmentally effective outputs.

A key distinction is between formal rules and implementation practice. Formally, public financial management systems usually rest on annual budget laws, expenditure ceilings, budget classification rules, procurement procedures, reporting requirements, audit mandates, and legislative oversight. These rules are intended to make public money traceable from authorization to obligation, disbursement, delivery, and audit. In practice, however, the effectiveness of these rules depends on the quality of revenue forecasting, the credibility of budget ceilings, the timeliness of cash releases, the capacity of line agencies to prepare and procure projects, and the ability of oversight bodies to detect and correct misuse. A formally balanced budget process can still be administratively weak if agencies underspend capital budgets, if procurement delays compress implementation into the end of the fiscal year, if audit findings do not lead to sanctions or management correction, or if fiscal risks are kept outside the central budget.

The historical and structural causes of these difficulties are not reducible to one reform failure. The Philippine fiscal system has long operated in a political economy marked by uneven tax effort, strong demands for local and sectoral allocation, infrastructure backlogs, exposure to natural disasters, and periodic pressure to use public spending for short-term stabilization or political accommodation. These conditions produce a recurring administrative dilemma: the central government must demonstrate fiscal discipline to maintain credibility with creditors and investors, while also expanding expenditure to address social inequality, territorial development gaps, climate vulnerability, and security needs. Public financial management reform is therefore situated between two imperatives: control and responsiveness. Excessive control can delay implementation; excessive discretion can weaken accountability.

Debt and interest costs are especially important for understanding this dilemma. Central government debt was 43.43 percent of GDP in 2014, having decreased by 28.17 percentage points from 2004. This earlier reduction suggests that fiscal consolidation and debt management had previously improved the state's macro-fiscal position. Yet interest payments still absorbed 17.29 percent of revenue in 2024, slightly higher than in 2014 by 0.292 percentage points. The administrative meaning of this indicator is significant: even when debt ratios appear manageable, debt service can reduce the discretionary fiscal space available for development spending. Interest payments function as a prior claim on revenue. They constrain the budget before policymakers choose between infrastructure, health, education, transfers, defense, or disaster recovery. A public financial management system that does not integrate medium-term debt analysis into budget planning risks treating annual appropriations as more flexible than they actually are.

Fiscal risk management is consequently central to budget reform. The visible budget does not capture all future claims on the state. Contingent liabilities may arise from state-owned enterprises, public-private partnerships, guarantees, natural disasters, pension obligations, financial-sector stress, local government borrowing, or emergency spending. The Philippines' exposure to climate and disaster shocks makes this issue particularly important, because large shocks can rapidly convert implicit liabilities into explicit fiscal costs. A credible fiscal system must therefore identify, report, and provision for risks before they become expenditure shocks. This requires institutional coordination among finance, budget, treasury, audit, sector agencies, local governments, and public corporations. The specialist should examine whether fiscal risk statements, debt sustainability analysis, and enterprise monitoring are integrated into budget decisions, or whether they exist as formal reports with limited influence over allocation.

State enterprises and government-controlled corporations are another crucial part of the fiscal system. They may support infrastructure, finance, utilities, transport, housing, or social objectives, but they can also generate hidden liabilities if their borrowing, guarantees, losses, or quasi-fiscal activities are not fully reflected in fiscal planning. The analytical issue is not whether state enterprises are inherently inefficient or necessary, but whether the government has reliable information on their financial position, investment plans, dividend behavior, subsidies, and contingent obligations. Where enterprise oversight is weak, the budget may appear disciplined while risk accumulates outside the core accounts. Where oversight is strong, state enterprises can become instruments of development strategy without undermining fiscal credibility.

The financial-sector indicators provide additional context for state capacity and development strategy. A development strategy that depends on infrastructure investment, private participation, and public borrowing needs credible fiscal reporting to maintain confidence among lenders, investors, and citizens.

Compared with fiscally stronger states, the Philippines faces a narrower margin for error: moderate revenue capacity, significant development demands, disaster exposure, and debt-service claims make execution quality unusually consequential.

In this sense, public financial management and budget reform reveal the fiscal foundations of Philippine state capacity. Future reform risks arise if formal systems continue to improve while implementation remains uneven. Future reform opportunities arise if revenue gains are consolidated, budget execution becomes more predictable, audit findings generate corrective action, and fiscal risks are integrated into medium-term planning. Revenue, expenditure, and the fiscal basis of security capacity

Security capacity depends on the state's ability to raise revenue predictably and allocate it credibly. The Philippines' revenue excluding grants at 16.70 percent of GDP in 2024 and tax revenue at 14.37 percent of GDP show that the country has a meaningful but limited domestic fiscal base. The increase in revenue since 2014 is positive for state capacity because it expands the resources available for defense, policing, disaster response, and justice institutions without relying solely on borrowing or external support. However, the tax ratio remains the more important structural indicator. Tax revenue represents the recurring domestic extraction capacity on which long-term personnel systems, procurement commitments, and maintenance budgets must rest.

The growth of GDP to US$461.62 billion in 2024, an increase of US$164.13 billion from 2014, enlarges the economic base from which public revenue can be drawn. GDP growth of 5.692 percent in 2024 also indicates continued economic dynamism, although it was 0.656 percentage points lower than in 2014. The implication is that security modernization is occurring in a growing economy, but growth alone does not guarantee fiscal capacity. The conversion of economic growth into usable public revenue depends on tax administration, compliance, tax-policy design, customs enforcement, expenditure control, and political acceptance of taxation. A specialist should therefore avoid reading defense affordability directly from GDP size. The more relevant test is whether revenue mobilization and budget execution keep pace with the recurrent costs produced by security commitments.

Government expenses at 16.88 percent of GDP in 2023, up 3.673 percentage points from 2013, point to a larger expenditure state. This may reflect broader developmental ambition and expanded public functions. For defense and public security, however, it also means stronger competition within the budget. If expenditure grows faster than durable revenue, security allocations may become vulnerable to compression during fiscal consolidation. Conversely, if security spending receives priority protection, adjustment may fall on maintenance, local service delivery, social programs, or capital projects. The institutional dilemma is therefore not whether the Philippines should spend on security, but how to maintain a credible security posture while avoiding a budget structure that underfunds the developmental and administrative foundations of state legitimacy.

Public financial management and implementation practice

Formally, defense and public-security spending are subject to the budget cycle, legislative authorization, audit rules, procurement requirements, and executive planning systems. These formal rules are designed to connect appropriations to legality, accountability, and policy priorities. In principle, they should prevent off-budget security commitments, require transparent procurement, discipline multi-year obligations, and align defense plans with macro-fiscal ceilings.

Implementation practice can diverge from this formal model in several ways. First, security procurement is technically complex and often vulnerable to delay, contract fragmentation, sole-source pressures, or weak lifecycle-cost estimation. Second, personnel and pension obligations can crowd out operations and maintenance even when headline budgets appear adequate. Third, security organizations may receive appropriations for acquisition without equivalent capacity for project management, auditing, logistics, or contract enforcement. Fourth, public-security expenditures may be distributed across defense, interior, police, coast guard, justice, intelligence, and local-government channels, making the full fiscal burden difficult to observe from military expenditure alone. These gaps between formal budgeting and practical execution are central to understanding state capacity.

Governance indicators reinforce this interpretation. Government Effectiveness stood at 0.108 in 2023, having decreased by 0.244 from 2013. For defense and public-security spending, this means that the binding problem may be less the absolute budget level than the conversion of appropriations into reliable capability, disciplined procurement, and accountable operations.

Debt, fiscal risk, and contingent obligations

The debt indicator in the evidence package requires careful interpretation. Central government debt is reported at 43.43 percent of GDP in 2014, a decrease of 28.17 percentage points from 2004. Because the latest value supplied for this indicator is 2014, it should not be used to characterize the current debt position without additional evidence. Its analytical value here is historical: it indicates that, prior to the more recent period, the Philippines had substantially reduced central government debt relative to GDP. That historical improvement may have supported policy credibility and widened fiscal space for development and security spending.

For the present section, the debt question should be framed as a fiscal-risk issue rather than a claim about the latest debt level. Defense modernization, public-security expansion, and disaster-response capabilities can create multi-year obligations that are not fully visible in annual expenditure ratios. These include procurement arrears, foreign-exchange exposure in imported equipment, maintenance liabilities, pension obligations, guarantees linked to state enterprises or public corporations involved in infrastructure and logistics, and emergency spending after natural disasters or security crises. A state may appear fiscally stable in one year while accumulating future rigidity through commitments that are politically difficult to reverse.

The role of state enterprises and public corporations should also be considered. Even when defense spending is formally recorded in the national budget, security capacity may depend on government-owned or government-controlled corporations, port authorities, airport authorities, transport entities, energy firms, and infrastructure agencies. These bodies can affect defense and public security indirectly by financing or operating strategic infrastructure, logistics corridors, communications systems, and resilience assets. The fiscal risk is that weak coordination between budgetary agencies and state enterprises can obscure the full cost of security-relevant infrastructure. Conversely, well-governed public enterprises can strengthen state capacity by supporting maritime logistics, energy security, and disaster response without overloading the core budget.

Historical and structural causes

Several structural features help explain the Philippines' defense and public-security spending profile. The country is an archipelagic state, which raises the administrative cost of territorial control, maritime monitoring, inter-island logistics, and disaster response. Security capacity must be distributed across islands and coastal zones rather than concentrated in a single land corridor. This geography creates high fixed costs for presence and coordination even when the defense budget is not large by international standards.

A second structural factor is the coexistence of external defense needs and internal public-security demands. Public order, insurgency-related legacies, maritime disputes, transnational crime, disaster response, and local security coordination can all compete for attention and resources. The result is a security sector that must be both military and administrative: it must defend territory, support civil authorities, coordinate with local governments, and operate within legal and fiscal constraints. This broad mandate increases the importance of public financial management because the same peso cannot simultaneously finance salaries, ships, aircraft, police mobility, court capacity, detention facilities, and local service delivery.

A third historical factor is the uneven development of state institutions. The evidence shows improvement in rule of law and control of corruption since 2013, but both remained negative in 2023. This indicates that formal institutional reforms may have produced gains without fully overcoming enforcement, integrity, and accountability deficits. In defense and public security, such deficits are especially consequential because secrecy, urgency, and technical complexity can weaken ordinary oversight. The administrative challenge is to protect legitimate operational confidentiality while preventing confidentiality from becoming a channel for weak procurement discipline or limited accountability.

Comparative-administration implications

Comparatively, the Philippines illustrates a common problem in middle-income democracies with strategic security pressures: the defense burden may be moderate in macroeconomic terms but still significant in administrative terms. A high-capacity state can often sustain security modernization because it has deep revenue systems, professional procurement agencies, effective audit institutions, and credible multi-year planning. A lower-capacity state may struggle even with lower defense ratios because budget execution, procurement integrity, maintenance systems, and interagency coordination are weaker.

The Philippine case also shows why defense spending should be analyzed together with public security rather than separately. Military expenditure captures only part of the security state. Police, coast guard, courts, corrections, intelligence, border management, cyber institutions, and local public-order mechanisms shape the real fiscal cost of security. A narrow defense-only measure may understate the total administrative burden. At the same time, aggregating all security-related spending without functional distinction can overstate militarization. A specialist should therefore distinguish external defense, internal security, law enforcement, justice administration, emergency management, and strategic infrastructure.

This section should resolve one institutional dilemma: how can the Philippines sustain credible defense and public-security capacity while preserving the fiscal basis of developmental state functions? The answer cannot be derived from spending levels alone. It requires evidence on whether resources are allocated toward the right mix of personnel, readiness, maintenance, capital modernization, civilian oversight, and local implementation. It also requires testing whether security allocations are protected by transparent fiscal planning or instead depend on ad hoc political prioritization.

Overall assessment

Defense and public-security spending in the Philippines reveals a state with moderate security expenditure, an improving but still limited revenue base, and mixed governance capacity. The country's 2024 military expenditure of 1.3 percent of GDP does not by itself indicate an excessive macro-fiscal burden. Its 5.4 percent share of general government expenditure, however, shows that defense competes meaningfully within the public budget. Revenue and tax ratios have improved since 2014, but expenditure responsibilities have also expanded. Governance indicators show partial institutional strengthening, especially in rule of law and control of corruption, yet government effectiveness has weakened over the decade covered by the evidence.

The main implication for state capacity is that fiscal credibility depends less on headline defense growth than on the quality of allocation and execution. A security budget that finances salaries without readiness, procurement without maintenance, or equipment without logistics will produce limited capability. Conversely, a moderate but well-managed budget can strengthen territorial control, disaster response, maritime governance, and public order. For development strategy, the central risk is crowding out: security modernization must not erode the fiscal space needed for human capital, infrastructure, and local service delivery. For policy credibility, the central test is whether the government can make security commitments that are transparent, affordable, and executable over multiple years. For future reform, the challenge is to integrate defense and public-security planning into a broader fiscal framework that recognizes opportunity costs, lifecycle liabilities, debt risks, and the administrative foundations of effective state power.

Academic and statistical strengthening note (2026). World Bank WDI/WGI data in the local evidence file put the 2024 population at 115.84 million and GDP at about US$461.62 billion, while the Philippine Statistics Authority's 2024 Census of Population count declared official in 2025 gives 112.73 million as of 1 July 2024; the WDI file also records 2024 real GDP growth of 5.69 percent, GDP per capita of US$3,985, revenue excluding grants of 16.70 percent of GDP, tax revenue of 14.37 percent of GDP, internet use of 67.26 percent, and WGI 2023 scores of 0.11 for government effectiveness, 0.11 for regulatory quality, -0.29 for rule of law, and -0.38 for control of corruption. In this section, the analysis should interpret revenue, expenditure, debt, intergovernmental finance, and public-sector liabilities as the fiscal foundations of state capacity and policy credibility. The statistics should be read with national statistical-office releases and the World Bank, IMF, ILO, and WHO series, so that macro or social averages are not mistaken for administrative reach. Primary country evidence is archived in `sources/documents/official/official_statistics_policy_upgrade_2026.md`, and the interpretive literature is summarized in `sources/documents/academic/academic_literature_upgrade_2026.md`.

Philippine fiscal capacity should be understood as the administrative ability of the state to mobilize recurring revenue, allocate expenditure through credible public financial management, carry debt without excessive interest pressure, and manage contingent liabilities arising from public corporations, infrastructure commitments, local governments, pensions, disasters, and security obligations. The issue is not only whether headline debt or deficit indicators appear manageable. It is whether the fiscal system provides a durable basis for state capacity: the capacity to finance schools, health services, transport infrastructure, disaster response, social protection, territorial security, and regulatory enforcement without repeatedly relying on short-term borrowing, politically fragile tax measures, or under-executed capital budgets.

The recent statistical evidence gives a mixed picture. Revenue excluding grants reached 16.70 percent of GDP in 2024, an increase of 2.26 percentage points from 2014. Tax revenue reached 14.37 percent of GDP in 2024, 1.356 percentage points higher than in 2014. These figures suggest that the tax state has strengthened, but only moderately. The administrative implication is that the Philippines has improved revenue mobilization without yet reaching the fiscal depth associated with states that can simultaneously sustain large infrastructure programs, extensive social insurance, and high-quality local service delivery. A tax ratio around the mid-teens creates a binding constraint: new development commitments must compete with debt service, transfers, personnel costs, and politically protected spending.

Expenditure has also risen. Government expenses reached 16.88 percent of GDP in 2023, up 3.673 percentage points from 2013. This increase is administratively important because it indicates that the state has been expanding its expenditure role faster than the tax ratio has risen over a similar period. The result is not necessarily fiscal crisis, but it does point to a recurring public-sector dilemma: expenditure expectations are growing faster than the institutional capacity to guarantee stable financing, high execution quality, and long-term maintenance. In a developing archipelagic economy, expenditure expansion is not merely discretionary. It reflects infrastructure gaps, disaster exposure, social-policy demands, defense and maritime-security needs, and the costs of decentralization. Yet each of these pressures requires not only budget authority but also procurement capacity, project appraisal, intergovernmental coordination, and audit discipline.

The distinction between formal fiscal rules and implementation practice is central. Formally, the fiscal system is organized through annual budgeting, revenue legislation, public procurement rules, audit institutions, debt-management procedures, and the legal separation of responsibilities across national agencies, local governments, and public corporations. These formal arrangements are designed to authorize spending, discipline borrowing, and make public officials accountable for the use of funds. In practice, however, fiscal capacity depends on whether agencies can prepare projects, obligate funds on time, procure competitively, monitor outputs, and maintain assets after completion. A budget may be legally appropriated but administratively weak if funds are delayed, projects are redesigned midstream, procurement is contested, or local governments lack implementation capacity.

This gap between authorization and execution is especially consequential for infrastructure and social-service delivery. The Philippines has used development strategy as a fiscal argument: higher public investment is expected to reduce logistics costs, integrate regions, attract private investment, and support long-run productivity. Yet the fiscal return from infrastructure depends on project selection and implementation quality. Poorly sequenced projects create future maintenance liabilities rather than productive assets. Delayed projects weaken policy credibility because citizens and investors observe appropriations without equivalent outputs. Therefore, public financial management should be evaluated not only by deficit and debt ratios but also by budget credibility, procurement performance, arrears control, project completion rates, and the maintenance of completed assets.

Debt indicators also require careful interpretation. The supplied central-government debt series reports 43.43 percent of GDP in 2014, a decrease of 28.17 percentage points from 2004. This historical decline indicates that the Philippines had previously created fiscal space through debt reduction. That earlier consolidation matters because it shaped later credibility: a state that reduces debt over time can borrow on better terms and respond more effectively to shocks. However, the supplied debt indicator is not recent enough to test current debt sustainability. For this section, the 2014 central-government debt figure should be treated as historical background rather than as evidence of the present debt burden. The most important recent statistics to check are current general-government gross debt, central-government debt, deficit, interest payments as a share of GDP, debt maturity structure, currency composition, and the share of revenue absorbed by interest payments.

The available interest-payment indicator is more current and points to a concrete fiscal constraint. Interest payments amounted to 17.29 percent of revenue in 2024, only slightly higher than in 2014. The small change suggests that interest pressure has not exploded over the decade, but the level remains administratively significant. When roughly one-sixth of revenue is committed to interest, the government has less discretionary space for classrooms, health facilities, disaster preparedness, agricultural support, infrastructure maintenance, and local transfers. The institutional consequence is that fiscal policy becomes more sensitive to interest-rate conditions, exchange-rate movements, and investor confidence. Even when debt is legally authorized and macroeconomically manageable, high interest claims reduce the service-delivery content of the budget.

Public-sector risks extend beyond direct debt. State-owned and controlled corporations, public-private partnerships, guarantees, pension obligations, local-government borrowing, disaster-related reconstruction, and energy or transport-sector liabilities may create contingent claims on the national budget. These risks are often less visible than annual appropriations because they may appear only when projects fail, corporations require support, guarantees are called, or disasters impose sudden reconstruction costs. A specialist should therefore examine whether the Philippines has transparent fiscal-risk statements, reliable reporting on government-owned and controlled corporations, rules for guarantees, monitoring of public-private partnership liabilities, and mechanisms for integrating disaster-risk financing into the medium-term fiscal framework.

The historical and structural causes of Philippine fiscal risk are rooted in state formation, political economy, and geography. Revenue mobilization has long been constrained by informality, uneven administrative reach, tax incentives, compliance problems, and the political difficulty of taxing powerful economic interests. Expenditure pressure reflects a different set of structural conditions: archipelagic geography raises the cost of infrastructure and service delivery; disaster exposure requires recurring emergency and reconstruction spending; decentralization creates coordination problems between national planning and local implementation; and inequality increases demand for redistributive and social-protection programs. The fiscal state therefore faces a double burden. It must finance development while also compensating for the costs of territorial fragmentation, environmental vulnerability, and uneven local administrative capacity.

The governance indicators reinforce this interpretation. The Government Effectiveness estimate was 0.108 in 2023, down 0.244 from 2013. This decline should not be overinterpreted as a direct fiscal measure, but it does warn that rising revenue and expenditure do not automatically translate into stronger public administration. A state can collect more and spend more while still struggling with project execution, regulatory coordination, personnel management, and service quality. Regulatory Quality was 0.115 in 2023, up 0.076 from 2013, indicating modest improvement in the rule environment for private-sector activity. Taken together, the two indicators suggest an institutional asymmetry: the environment for regulation and investment may be improving incrementally, while the effectiveness of public implementation remains a concern. This asymmetry matters for fiscal policy because development strategy relies on the state's ability to convert budgets into credible outputs.

The comparative-administration implication is that the Philippines should not be classified simply as fiscally weak or fiscally strong. It is better understood as a middle-capacity fiscal state: capable of mobilizing a meaningful but limited tax base, able to borrow and sustain development spending, yet constrained by expenditure rigidity, implementation bottlenecks, and exposure to contingent liabilities. Compared with high-capacity developmental states, its revenue ratio leaves less room for universalistic welfare expansion and large-scale infrastructure without careful prioritization. Compared with fiscally fragile states, however, the Philippines possesses a more substantial revenue base, a growing economy, and a record of earlier debt reduction. The policy problem is therefore one of fiscal deepening and implementation credibility rather than basic fiscal survival.

This section's claims would be best tested with a focused set of additional statistics. First, current general-government gross debt and central-government debt are needed to update the debt assessment beyond the historical 2014 figure. Second, the fiscal balance as a percent of GDP would show whether expenditure growth is being financed by durable revenue or by continued borrowing. Third, interest payments as a percent of both GDP and total expenditure would clarify whether debt service is crowding out public services. Fourth, tax composition should be checked: value-added tax, income tax, excise taxes, customs duties, and local taxes reveal whether revenue gains come from broad-based taxation or narrow, volatile sources. Fifth, budget-execution rates, procurement delays, public-investment completion rates, and audit findings would test whether appropriated spending becomes actual administrative output. Sixth, data on government-owned and controlled corporations, public-private partnership guarantees, pension liabilities, and disaster-related contingent liabilities would test whether the published budget captures the full public-sector risk profile.

The institutional dilemma to be resolved is the tension between development ambition and fiscal-administrative credibility. The Philippines needs higher and better-quality spending to support infrastructure, human capital, climate resilience, and territorial integration. Yet expanding expenditure without a stronger tax base, tighter fiscal-risk monitoring, and more reliable implementation may weaken credibility and increase future adjustment costs. Conversely, excessive fiscal restraint could protect debt indicators while underfinancing the very public goods needed for long-run growth. The central challenge is therefore to deepen revenue mobilization while improving expenditure quality, so that fiscal expansion produces visible public value rather than merely larger budgets and higher debt-service claims.

Future reform should be evaluated by whether it strengthens the fiscal basis of state capacity. Revenue reform should broaden the tax base, improve compliance, rationalize incentives, and strengthen digital administration without imposing excessive burdens on lower-income households. Expenditure reform should prioritize projects with clear economic and social returns, protect maintenance budgets, and improve coordination between national agencies and local governments. Debt management should preserve maturity and currency resilience while preventing interest payments from absorbing a growing share of revenue. Fiscal-risk management should bring contingent liabilities into regular reporting and decision-making. Public-enterprise oversight should distinguish commercially viable entities from entities that function as off-budget policy instruments. These reforms are mutually reinforcing: without revenue, the state cannot finance development; without public financial management, revenue does not become capacity; without risk control, today's development strategy may become tomorrow's fiscal constraint.
