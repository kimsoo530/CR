7.3 Public Financial Management and Budget Reform

ASEAN comparative lens (2026). The Philippines should be read in ASEAN comparison as a populous archipelagic democracy with strong local political mediation, labor migration, and maritime exposure. Compared with Indonesia, it has a smaller domestic market and weaker industrial depth; compared with Vietnam and Thailand, it is less manufacturing-centered; compared with Singapore and Malaysia, it has a more dispersed territorial-service problem and heavier reliance on external labor and services. For this section, the regional comparison should compare fiscal capacity, revenue extraction, debt pressure, and public-sector risk with ASEAN peers rather than treating fiscal ratios as isolated national facts. Local comparable indicators show: population 115.84 million (2024), rank 2 among the nine ASEAN reports in this workspace; GDP US$461.62 billion (2024), rank 5 among the nine ASEAN reports in this workspace; GDP per capita US$3,985 (2024), rank 6 among the nine ASEAN reports in this workspace; real GDP growth 5.69% (2024), rank 3 among the nine ASEAN reports in this workspace; government effectiveness 0.11 (2023), rank 5 among the nine ASEAN reports in this workspace; internet use 67.26% (2024), rank 7 among the nine ASEAN reports in this workspace. Academic and statistical strengthening note (2026). World Bank WDI/WGI data in the local evidence file put the 2024 population at 115.84 million and GDP at about US$461.62 billion, while the Philippine Statistics Authority's 2024 Census of Population count declared official in 2025 gives 112.73 million as of 1 July 2024; the WDI file also records 2024 real GDP growth of 5.69 percent, GDP per capita of US$3,985, revenue excluding grants of 16.70 percent of GDP, tax revenue of 14.37 percent of GDP, internet use of 67.26 percent, and WGI 2023 scores of 0.11 for government effectiveness, 0.11 for regulatory quality, -0.29 for rule of law, and -0.38 for control of corruption. In this section, the analysis should interpret revenue, expenditure, debt, intergovernmental finance, and public-sector liabilities as the fiscal foundations of state capacity and policy credibility. The statistics should be read with national statistical-office releases and the World Bank, IMF, ILO, and WHO series, so that macro or social averages are not mistaken for administrative reach. Primary country evidence is archived in `sources/documents/official/official_statistics_policy_upgrade_2026.md`, and the interpretive literature is summarized in `sources/documents/academic/academic_literature_upgrade_2026.md`.

The fiscal system of the Philippines links revenue capacity to policy ambition, and public financial management is the administrative mechanism through which this link is made credible. A specialist should therefore understand Philippine budget reform not only as a technical effort to improve accounting, procurement, audit, and expenditure control, but as a central component of state capacity. Revenue, expenditure, debt, fiscal risk, state-owned and state-controlled enterprises, and the credibility of budget execution determine whether the state can finance development plans, maintain macroeconomic stability, and implement policy consistently across agencies and levels of government.

The available indicators suggest a fiscal system that has increased its resource base but still faces binding constraints. Revenue excluding grants reached 16.70 percent of GDP in 2024, an increase of 2.26 percentage points from 2014. Tax revenue reached 14.37 percent of GDP in 2024, up 1.356 percentage points from 2014. Administratively, this implies an improvement in domestic revenue mobilization, but not a transformation large enough to remove the tension between infrastructure, social policy, security spending, debt service, and subnational transfers. The fiscal basis of state capacity remains moderate: the government has more room than in an earlier low-revenue equilibrium, but its policy commitments still compete within a limited fiscal envelope. The central question is therefore whether revenue reform has produced a durable administrative capacity to assess, collect, and enforce taxes, or whether gains depend heavily on macroeconomic growth, consumption patterns, or temporary compliance improvements.

Expenditure trends reinforce this interpretation. Government expenses stood at 16.88 percent of GDP in 2023, an increase of 3.673 percentage points from 2013. This rise indicates that the Philippine state has expanded its spending role, but it also raises the administrative stakes of budget formulation and execution. A larger expenditure state requires stronger commitment controls, cash management, procurement discipline, internal audit, and performance monitoring. Without these systems, increased spending may produce arrears, delayed projects, weak value for money, or fragmented implementation rather than stronger service delivery. Budget reform should therefore be assessed less by the formal adoption of modern instruments than by whether agencies can convert appropriations into timely, lawful, and developmentally effective outputs.

A key distinction is between formal rules and implementation practice. Formally, public financial management systems usually rest on annual budget laws, expenditure ceilings, budget classification rules, procurement procedures, reporting requirements, audit mandates, and legislative oversight. These rules are intended to make public money traceable from authorization to obligation, disbursement, delivery, and audit. In practice, however, the effectiveness of these rules depends on the quality of revenue forecasting, the credibility of budget ceilings, the timeliness of cash releases, the capacity of line agencies to prepare and procure projects, and the ability of oversight bodies to detect and correct misuse. A formally balanced budget process can still be administratively weak if agencies underspend capital budgets, if procurement delays compress implementation into the end of the fiscal year, if audit findings do not lead to sanctions or management correction, or if fiscal risks are kept outside the central budget.

The historical and structural causes of these difficulties are not reducible to one reform failure. The Philippine fiscal system has long operated in a political economy marked by uneven tax effort, strong demands for local and sectoral allocation, infrastructure backlogs, exposure to natural disasters, and periodic pressure to use public spending for short-term stabilization or political accommodation. These conditions produce a recurring administrative dilemma: the central government must demonstrate fiscal discipline to maintain credibility with creditors and investors, while also expanding expenditure to address social inequality, territorial development gaps, climate vulnerability, and security needs. Public financial management reform is therefore situated between two imperatives: control and responsiveness. Excessive control can delay implementation; excessive discretion can weaken accountability.

Debt and interest costs are especially important for understanding this dilemma. Central government debt was 43.43 percent of GDP in 2014, having decreased by 28.17 percentage points from 2004. This earlier reduction suggests that fiscal consolidation and debt management had previously improved the state's macro-fiscal position. Yet interest payments still absorbed 17.29 percent of revenue in 2024, slightly higher than in 2014 by 0.292 percentage points. The administrative meaning of this indicator is significant: even when debt ratios appear manageable, debt service can reduce the discretionary fiscal space available for development spending. Interest payments function as a prior claim on revenue. They constrain the budget before policymakers choose between infrastructure, health, education, transfers, defense, or disaster recovery. A public financial management system that does not integrate medium-term debt analysis into budget planning risks treating annual appropriations as more flexible than they actually are.

Fiscal risk management is consequently central to budget reform. The visible budget does not capture all future claims on the state. Contingent liabilities may arise from state-owned enterprises, public-private partnerships, guarantees, natural disasters, pension obligations, financial-sector stress, local government borrowing, or emergency spending. The Philippines' exposure to climate and disaster shocks makes this issue particularly important, because large shocks can rapidly convert implicit liabilities into explicit fiscal costs. A credible fiscal system must therefore identify, report, and provision for risks before they become expenditure shocks. This requires institutional coordination among finance, budget, treasury, audit, sector agencies, local governments, and public corporations. The specialist should examine whether fiscal risk statements, debt sustainability analysis, and enterprise monitoring are integrated into budget decisions, or whether they exist as formal reports with limited influence over allocation.

State enterprises and government-controlled corporations are another crucial part of the fiscal system. They may support infrastructure, finance, utilities, transport, housing, or social objectives, but they can also generate hidden liabilities if their borrowing, guarantees, losses, or quasi-fiscal activities are not fully reflected in fiscal planning. The analytical issue is not whether state enterprises are inherently inefficient or necessary, but whether the government has reliable information on their financial position, investment plans, dividend behavior, subsidies, and contingent obligations. Where enterprise oversight is weak, the budget may appear disciplined while risk accumulates outside the core accounts. Where oversight is strong, state enterprises can become instruments of development strategy without undermining fiscal credibility.

The financial-sector indicators provide additional context for state capacity and development strategy. Domestic credit to the private sector reached 49.85 percent of GDP in 2024, increasing by 12.27 percentage points from 2014. This suggests a deepening role for financial intermediation and a larger private-sector credit system. Administratively, stronger private credit can support growth and broaden the future tax base, but it also raises the importance of macroprudential oversight and fiscal contingency planning. By contrast, listed domestic companies' market capitalization was 54.55 percent of GDP in 2024, down 33.47 percentage points from 2014. This decline suggests that capital-market depth may not have strengthened in parallel with credit expansion. For fiscal policy, the implication is that domestic financing options, investor confidence, and the state's relationship with financial markets require close monitoring. A development strategy that depends on infrastructure investment, private participation, and public borrowing needs credible fiscal reporting to maintain confidence among lenders, investors, and citizens.

Security spending also belongs within public financial management, because it competes for fiscal space and reflects state priorities. Military expenditure reached 1.324 percent of GDP in 2024, increasing by 0.281 percentage points from 2014. Armed forces personnel represented 0.369 percent of the labor force in 2020, decreasing by 0.065 percentage points from 2010. The administrative interpretation is that defense spending has increased relative to output even though personnel intensity has declined. This may indicate a shift toward equipment, modernization, operations, or non-personnel costs. The fiscal relevance is not only the size of defense spending, but the capacity to plan, procure, audit, and sustain long-term defense commitments without crowding out civilian development priorities or creating opaque procurement risks.

Recent evidence that should be checked includes updated budget execution reports, audit findings, procurement performance data, cash release timeliness, arrears or payables, public debt composition, interest-rate exposure, fiscal risk statements, state-enterprise financial reports, and local government fiscal data. Evidence on budget reform should also examine whether performance-informed budgeting affects allocation decisions or merely adds documentation to the budget process. Evidence on audit should distinguish between the existence of audit findings and the administrative consequences of those findings: corrective action, recovery of funds, management sanctions, procurement reform, and legislative follow-up. Evidence on financial management should test whether digital systems improve transparency across the full budget cycle or only central reporting.

The most useful statistics for testing this section's claims would include revenue-to-GDP and tax-to-GDP trends; tax buoyancy; composition of tax revenue; expenditure by economic and functional classification; capital budget execution rates; procurement cycle duration; budget deviation between approved and actual spending; share of interest payments in revenue; debt-to-GDP by domestic and external composition; average maturity and interest-rate structure of debt; contingent liabilities from guarantees and public corporations; fiscal costs of disasters; state-enterprise subsidies, dividends, and debt; audit findings by agency and resolution rate; and local government own-source revenue. The missing account-ownership indicator should not be treated as evidence. Its absence is itself analytically relevant because financial inclusion can affect tax administration, transfer delivery, and the feasibility of digital public finance, but no value should be inferred from the unavailable indicator.

The institutional dilemma this section should resolve is how the Philippines can expand developmental spending while preserving fiscal credibility and strengthening implementation discipline. If the state emphasizes austerity, it may underinvest in infrastructure, human capital, resilience, and territorial inclusion. If it expands expenditure without stronger revenue administration, procurement capacity, fiscal-risk control, and audit follow-through, it may weaken debt sustainability and public trust. The comparative-administration implication is that Philippine reform should be evaluated by the fit between formal public financial management systems and the administrative environment in which they operate. Compared with fiscally stronger states, the Philippines faces a narrower margin for error: moderate revenue capacity, significant development demands, disaster exposure, and debt-service claims make execution quality unusually consequential.

In this sense, public financial management and budget reform reveal the fiscal foundations of Philippine state capacity. They show whether the state can make credible commitments, allocate resources strategically, manage risk, discipline agencies, and sustain development policy beyond annual political bargaining. The central issue is not merely whether the government has modern budget rules, but whether those rules shape behavior across revenue collection, expenditure execution, debt management, audit correction, and enterprise oversight. Future reform risks arise if formal systems continue to improve while implementation remains uneven. Future reform opportunities arise if revenue gains are consolidated, budget execution becomes more predictable, audit findings generate corrective action, and fiscal risks are integrated into medium-term planning. The credibility of the Philippine development strategy depends on this integration: the state must be able to raise resources, spend them well, disclose risks honestly, and maintain the confidence of citizens, creditors, investors, and implementing agencies.
