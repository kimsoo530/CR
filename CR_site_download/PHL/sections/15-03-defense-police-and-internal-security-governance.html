15.3 Defense, Police, and Internal Security Governance

Compared with Indonesia, it has a smaller domestic market and weaker industrial depth; compared with Vietnam and Thailand, it is less manufacturing-centered; compared with Singapore and Malaysia, it has a more dispersed territorial-service problem and heavier reliance on external labor and services. Defense, police, and internal security governance in the Philippines should be understood as a central interface between foreign relations, domestic state capacity, and archipelagic political geography. The country's security administration is shaped by three overlapping tasks: external defense in a contested maritime environment; internal security in areas affected by insurgency, terrorism, clan violence, criminal networks, and local political coercion; and civilian policing in a democratic system where formal accountability rules coexist with uneven implementation. In comparative-administration terms, the Philippines is neither a purely external-defense case nor a purely internal-order case. It is a maritime, disaster-exposed, alliance-dependent, decentralized, and socially plural polity whose security institutions must manage the South China Sea, porous maritime borders, Mindanao security, transnational crime, and police legitimacy at the same time.

The formal rules of security governance distinguish the military, the police, civilian ministries, courts, and elected officials. In principle, the armed forces defend territorial integrity and support civil authorities only under lawful conditions; the police maintain public order and investigate crime; elected civilian leaders set policy; oversight bodies, courts, audit institutions, and legislative committees are expected to prevent abuse, corruption, and political capture. This formal design is important because it expresses the constitutional aspiration to subordinate coercive power to civilian, legal, and budgetary control. However, implementation practice is more complex. The boundaries between external defense, internal security, counterinsurgency, counterterrorism, anti-crime operations, maritime enforcement, and disaster response often blur. Military units may be used for internal stabilization, police agencies may face paramilitary-style tasks, and local governments may depend on national security forces when routine administrative capacity is weak. The resulting governance problem is not merely organizational overlap; it is the difficulty of maintaining legality, professionalism, and public trust when the same state must respond simultaneously to geopolitical pressure, insurgency, criminal violence, natural hazards, and local political fragmentation.

Recent statistical evidence reinforces this interpretation. Military expenditure stood at 1.324 percent of GDP in 2024, an increase of 0.281 percentage points from 2014. Administratively, this indicates a moderate strengthening of defense effort rather than a wholesale militarization of the state. The increase is consistent with a system trying to modernize defense capabilities, especially maritime and external-defense capacities, while still operating within fiscal limits imposed by development needs, infrastructure demands, social spending, disaster recovery, and debt-management pressures. The key implication is that the Philippines must prioritize carefully: it cannot simply purchase comprehensive deterrence, full territorial surveillance, and internal security capacity at the same time without trade-offs. The most important administrative issue is therefore how defense spending is translated into deployable capability, maintenance, logistics, personnel readiness, procurement integrity, and interagency coordination.

Armed forces personnel represented 0.369 percent of the total labor force in 2020, a decline of 0.065 percentage points from 2010. This trend suggests that the state's coercive apparatus has not expanded primarily through mass military manpower. For administration, the implication is significant: capability must increasingly depend on technology, intelligence, mobility, interoperability, professional training, maritime domain awareness, and coordination with civilian agencies rather than sheer personnel scale. In a geographically fragmented archipelago, a relatively limited personnel base increases the importance of strategic placement, reserve systems, coast guard and naval coordination, air and sea lift, local intelligence, and rapid-response capacity. It also means that poorly governed deployment decisions can quickly create overstretch, especially when external defense, internal security, election security, disaster response, and border control demands arise concurrently.

The Philippines' geography makes defense governance inseparable from foreign policy. The country lies along major sea lanes and near contested maritime spaces, making maritime security central to diplomacy, alliance management, and economic development. Strategic corridors include the South China Sea, the Luzon Strait, the Sulu and Celebes Seas, and the maritime approaches connecting the Philippines to Taiwan, Japan, Southeast Asia, and the wider Indo-Pacific. These corridors are not abstract geopolitical spaces. They affect fisheries, energy exploration, coast guard operations, naval modernization, commercial shipping, undersea infrastructure, disaster response routes, and the credibility of territorial claims. Defense administration therefore has a foreign-policy function: procurement choices, basing arrangements, exercises with partners, coast guard deployments, and rules of engagement all signal the country's diplomatic posture. Conversely, foreign policy commitments create administrative burdens: interoperability, host-community management, legal compliance, information security, and crisis communication must be handled by institutions that may already be stretched.

Borders in the Philippine case are primarily maritime, and this makes internal security both a domestic and transnational matter. Porous sea borders complicate the control of trafficking, piracy, smuggling, arms movement, irregular migration, illegal fishing, and militant mobility. Mindanao and the Sulu archipelago are especially important because they connect internal security governance to neighboring states and regional maritime networks. A specialist should therefore treat border management as a multi-agency administrative problem involving the navy, coast guard, police, customs, immigration, fisheries authorities, local governments, and diplomatic channels. Formal jurisdictional lines may be clear on paper, but practical effectiveness depends on information sharing, vessel tracking, prosecution capacity, port governance, local cooperation, and the ability to distinguish criminal, insurgent, livelihood, and migration dynamics without indiscriminate securitization.

Internal security governance also has deep historical and structural causes. Colonial policing traditions, postcolonial centralization, local bossism, land inequality, communist insurgency, Muslim separatist conflict, martial-law legacies, and uneven regional development have all shaped the relationship between coercive institutions and society. These legacies help explain why the armed forces and police are often judged not only by their operational performance but also by their relationship to legality, human rights, corruption, and local political power. The rule of law estimate was -0.293 in 2023, although it had improved by 0.504 from 2013. This improvement suggests some strengthening of legal order relative to the previous decade, but the negative level still points to persistent constraints in law enforcement, judicial credibility, protection of rights, and impartial implementation. For security governance, the administrative meaning is that coercive capacity cannot be equated with state capacity unless it is anchored in credible legal process.

The governance indicators show a mixed institutional picture. Political stability and absence of violence/terrorism registered -0.396 in 2023, improving by 1.447 from 2013. This is a major positive shift in trend terms and suggests that the security environment has become less unstable than it was a decade earlier. Yet the estimate remains below zero, indicating that violence, terrorism risk, and political instability have not disappeared as constraints on public administration. A specialist should interpret this as a partial stabilization trajectory: the state may be better able to manage some threats, but the remaining risk environment still affects investment, local development, service delivery, and citizen confidence, especially in conflict-affected or geographically peripheral areas.

Government effectiveness was 0.108 in 2023, a decline of 0.244 from 2013. This is analytically important because it qualifies any claim of improving security conditions. A country may become more stable while still experiencing deterioration in the perceived quality of public services, policy implementation, bureaucratic competence, or administrative consistency. In defense and police governance, this creates a credibility problem. Security agencies may be asked to compensate for weaknesses in civilian administration, but relying on coercive institutions to fill administrative gaps can weaken democratic governance, crowd out developmental solutions, and normalize emergency modes of rule. The current institutional consequence is a need to strengthen civilian planning, procurement, local governance, courts, social services, and conflict-resolution mechanisms alongside security-sector modernization.

Regulatory quality was 0.115 in 2023, having increased by 0.076 from 2013. This modest improvement suggests that the policy and regulatory environment may have become somewhat more capable of supporting private-sector activity and rule-bound administration. In security terms, better regulatory quality matters for procurement, defense industrial policy, cybersecurity rules, private security regulation, port governance, energy projects, telecommunications infrastructure, and disaster-resilient investment. Yet regulatory quality alone cannot resolve implementation problems if corruption, weak prosecution, politicized enforcement, or interagency rivalry persist. Control of corruption stood at -0.376 in 2023, despite an improvement of 0.27 from 2013. The administrative interpretation is again mixed: anti-corruption performance may have improved relative to the past, but corruption remains a structural constraint on procurement credibility, police discipline, border enforcement, infrastructure security, and public confidence.

Voice and accountability was -0.012 in 2023, down by 0.023 from 2013. The small decline and near-zero level suggest neither complete closure nor robust accountability. For police and internal security governance, this indicator is especially relevant because durable security reform depends on public scrutiny, independent media, civil society monitoring, complaint mechanisms, legislative oversight, and judicial remedies. Where accountability is weak or politically selective, formal rules may remain intact while implementation practice becomes discretionary. This affects police legitimacy: citizens may comply out of fear or necessity, but development-oriented governance requires trust, reporting of crimes, cooperation with investigations, and confidence that security forces will not be used as instruments of partisan or local coercion.

Climate risk adds another layer to security governance. The Philippines' exposure to typhoons, flooding, sea-level rise, landslides, and coastal hazards makes the armed forces, police, coast guard, and local governments central to disaster response. This expands the definition of security beyond territorial defense and crime control. Climate events can damage bases, ports, roads, communications systems, and local government facilities; displace populations; disrupt food and water systems; intensify competition over land and fisheries; and create openings for trafficking, corruption, and local disorder. For administration, the challenge is to avoid treating disasters only as emergency-response episodes. Climate resilience must be integrated into defense infrastructure, logistics planning, local policing, maritime safety, evacuation systems, and reconstruction governance. Otherwise, repeated disasters will drain security budgets, reduce readiness, and weaken policy credibility.

Resource governance is similarly connected to defense and internal security. Fisheries, offshore energy potential, mineral resources, ports, and maritime transport routes all require credible protection and regulation. Where resources are located in disputed waters or conflict-affected regions, the distinction between economic policy and security policy narrows. Weak enforcement can produce illegal extraction, environmental degradation, revenue loss, and conflict with local communities. Excessively coercive enforcement, however, can damage livelihoods and legitimacy. The institutional dilemma is therefore how to protect strategic resources and borders without converting development problems into purely military or police problems.

The best recent evidence to check for this section would include defense budget execution, procurement delays, naval and coast guard asset readiness, maritime incident data, police complaints and disciplinary outcomes, homicide and crime clearance rates, terrorism and insurgency incident trends, disaster-response deployment records, border interdiction statistics, prosecution rates for trafficking and smuggling, and public trust in police and military institutions. The most useful statistics would test whether higher defense spending has produced operational capability; whether improvements in political stability are reflected in lower violence at the provincial level; whether rule-of-law improvements translate into prosecution and accountability rather than only enforcement activity; whether police performance varies by region; and whether climate disasters reduce security readiness or redirect resources away from modernization. The missing remittance indicators should not be filled in by assumption. If available, remittance data would help evaluate whether overseas labor, household resilience, and migration networks shape security and development capacity, but no value should be cited here without verified data.

Comparatively, the Philippines illustrates a common but difficult administrative pattern among maritime democracies with internal conflict legacies: external security threats encourage modernization and alliance cooperation, while domestic security demands require legitimacy, legal restraint, and local knowledge. The state must be strong enough to deter coercion, police borders, and suppress violence, yet constrained enough to maintain democratic credibility. The central reform risk is that geopolitical urgency may justify faster spending and expanded security powers without corresponding improvements in procurement control, police accountability, judicial capacity, and civilian oversight. The central reform opportunity is that maritime defense, disaster resilience, police professionalization, and local development can be integrated into a coherent state-capacity agenda.

The institutional dilemma this section should resolve is how the Philippines can build credible defense and internal security capacity without weakening the civilian, legal, and developmental foundations that make such capacity legitimate. The answer lies in treating defense modernization, police reform, border governance, climate resilience, and conflict-sensitive development as mutually dependent administrative tasks. A security state that cannot implement law impartially will struggle to sustain public trust; a developmental state that cannot protect territory, corridors, and communities will struggle to sustain sovereignty and investment. The Philippines' future reform trajectory will therefore depend on whether formal rules are matched by implementation capacity: transparent budgets, disciplined procurement, accountable policing, professional armed forces, interoperable maritime agencies, resilient infrastructure, and civilian institutions capable of governing security rather than merely invoking it.
