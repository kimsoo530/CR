7.4 Defense and Public Security Spending

ASEAN comparative lens (2026). The Philippines should be read in ASEAN comparison as a populous archipelagic democracy with strong local political mediation, labor migration, and maritime exposure. Compared with Indonesia, it has a smaller domestic market and weaker industrial depth; compared with Vietnam and Thailand, it is less manufacturing-centered; compared with Singapore and Malaysia, it has a more dispersed territorial-service problem and heavier reliance on external labor and services. For this section, the regional comparison should compare fiscal capacity, revenue extraction, debt pressure, and public-sector risk with ASEAN peers rather than treating fiscal ratios as isolated national facts. Academic and statistical strengthening note (2026). World Bank WDI/WGI data in the local evidence file put the 2024 population at 115.84 million and GDP at about US$461.62 billion, while the Philippine Statistics Authority's 2024 Census of Population count declared official in 2025 gives 112.73 million as of 1 July 2024; the WDI file also records 2024 real GDP growth of 5.69 percent, GDP per capita of US$3,985, revenue excluding grants of 16.70 percent of GDP, tax revenue of 14.37 percent of GDP, internet use of 67.26 percent, and WGI 2023 scores of 0.11 for government effectiveness, 0.11 for regulatory quality, -0.29 for rule of law, and -0.38 for control of corruption. In this section, the analysis should interpret revenue, expenditure, debt, intergovernmental finance, and public-sector liabilities as the fiscal foundations of state capacity and policy credibility. The statistics should be read with national statistical-office releases and the World Bank, IMF, ILO, and WHO series, so that macro or social averages are not mistaken for administrative reach. Primary country evidence is archived in `sources/documents/official/official_statistics_policy_upgrade_2026.md`, and the interpretive literature is summarized in `sources/documents/academic/academic_literature_upgrade_2026.md`.

Defense and public-security spending should be interpreted as part of the Philippines' fiscal system rather than as a narrow sectoral item. The central analytical issue is how security commitments are financed, how they compete with other public functions, and how effectively formal budget rules convert appropriations into credible defense, policing, disaster-response, border-protection, and internal-security capacity. In a comparative-administration perspective, the Philippines is not primarily a case of exceptionally large military spending relative to the economy. The more important issue is whether a medium-sized revenue state, facing archipelagic security demands and persistent internal-public-order needs, can finance security modernization without weakening social expenditure, infrastructure maintenance, debt sustainability, or the credibility of public financial management.

The current quantitative profile suggests a state with expanding fiscal capacity but continuing administrative constraints. Revenue excluding grants reached 16.70 percent of GDP in 2024, an increase of 2.26 percentage points from 2014. Tax revenue reached 14.37 percent of GDP in 2024, up 1.356 percentage points from 2014. These figures indicate that the Philippines has strengthened its domestic revenue base, but the improvement remains moderate rather than transformative. Security spending therefore has to be understood inside a constrained expenditure envelope. Government expenses reached 16.88 percent of GDP in 2023, rising by 3.673 percentage points from 2013. The administrative meaning of this trend is that expenditure responsibilities have expanded faster than the tax ratio. Even where fiscal space has improved, the state must still rank defense, public order, social protection, infrastructure, debt service, and local-government transfers against one another.

The military indicators in the current section are important because they show the scale of the uniformed claim on the labor force and budget. Armed forces personnel accounted for 0.4 percent of the total labor force in the latest World Bank series reported here. Military expenditure stood at 1.3 percent of GDP in 2024 and 5.4 percent of general government expenditure in 2024. These values do not imply a fiscally militarized state. They instead suggest a state attempting to sustain defense and security functions within a relatively modest macro-fiscal burden. The distinction matters. A defense budget of 1.3 percent of GDP may appear limited in aggregate terms, but 5.4 percent of government expenditure is a more direct measure of competition inside the public budget. Since the Philippines' revenue and expenditure ratios are not high by the standards of many consolidated administrative states, even moderate security allocations can impose visible opportunity costs.

Armed forces personnel

Source: World Bank WDI table ID `MS.MIL.TOTL.TF.ZS`. Unit: percent of total labor force.

The personnel indicator should be treated as a labor-market and administrative-capacity measure, not merely as a military statistic. A value of 0.4 percent of the total labor force indicates that the armed forces do not absorb an unusually large share of national labor. This may be fiscally reassuring because personnel costs are often rigid once salaries, pensions, housing, allowances, training, and career structures are established. However, a modest labor-force share does not automatically imply low personnel pressure inside the defense budget. The critical administrative test is whether the compensation and pension structure leaves sufficient room for maintenance, readiness, logistics, cyber capacity, maritime surveillance, and capital acquisition.

This distinction is especially relevant for the Philippines because defense and public security have both external and internal dimensions. Archipelagic geography raises the cost of maritime presence, territorial surveillance, coast guard coordination, transport, disaster response, and dispersed logistics. Internal-security and public-order functions also affect fiscal administration because they require personnel-intensive operations, local coordination, intelligence, detention and justice interfaces, and recurrent operational funding. In such a setting, the number of uniformed personnel is only one component of the state's security burden. The budgetary question is whether personnel, equipment, operations, and civilian oversight are balanced in a way that produces usable capacity rather than only formal establishment strength.

Military expenditure burden

Source: World Bank WDI table IDs `MS.MIL.XPND.GD.ZS` and `MS.MIL.XPND.ZS`. Units: percent of GDP and percent of government expenditure; interpret as separate measures.

Military expenditure of 1.3 percent of GDP in 2024 indicates a moderate macroeconomic burden. Interpreted alone, this figure could suggest that defense spending is fiscally manageable. Yet military expenditure at 5.4 percent of general government expenditure gives a different administrative signal: defense absorbs a non-trivial share of a public-expenditure envelope that must also finance infrastructure, education, health, social assistance, justice, climate resilience, and local administration. The two indicators therefore answer different questions. The GDP ratio measures macro-fiscal affordability; the expenditure-share ratio measures budgetary competition.

The reported five-year increase of 0.2 percentage points in both measures suggests gradual rather than abrupt militarization. Its administrative significance lies in the direction of movement. Even a small increase can be consequential when procurement pipelines are long, maintenance obligations accumulate, and defense modernization creates future recurrent costs. Capital acquisition is rarely a one-year expense. New platforms require trained personnel, spare parts, basing, information systems, interoperability arrangements, and multi-year maintenance contracts. The fiscal risk is therefore not only the first-year appropriation but the downstream expenditure path generated by modernization decisions.

Revenue, expenditure, and the fiscal basis of security capacity

Security capacity depends on the state's ability to raise revenue predictably and allocate it credibly. The Philippines' revenue excluding grants at 16.70 percent of GDP in 2024 and tax revenue at 14.37 percent of GDP show that the country has a meaningful but limited domestic fiscal base. The increase in revenue since 2014 is positive for state capacity because it expands the resources available for defense, policing, disaster response, and justice institutions without relying solely on borrowing or external support. However, the tax ratio remains the more important structural indicator. Tax revenue represents the recurring domestic extraction capacity on which long-term personnel systems, procurement commitments, and maintenance budgets must rest.

The growth of GDP to US$461.62 billion in 2024, an increase of US$164.13 billion from 2014, enlarges the economic base from which public revenue can be drawn. GDP growth of 5.692 percent in 2024 also indicates continued economic dynamism, although it was 0.656 percentage points lower than in 2014. The implication is that security modernization is occurring in a growing economy, but growth alone does not guarantee fiscal capacity. The conversion of economic growth into usable public revenue depends on tax administration, compliance, tax-policy design, customs enforcement, expenditure control, and political acceptance of taxation. A specialist should therefore avoid reading defense affordability directly from GDP size. The more relevant test is whether revenue mobilization and budget execution keep pace with the recurrent costs produced by security commitments.

Government expenses at 16.88 percent of GDP in 2023, up 3.673 percentage points from 2013, point to a larger expenditure state. This may reflect broader developmental ambition and expanded public functions. For defense and public security, however, it also means stronger competition within the budget. If expenditure grows faster than durable revenue, security allocations may become vulnerable to compression during fiscal consolidation. Conversely, if security spending receives priority protection, adjustment may fall on maintenance, local service delivery, social programs, or capital projects. The institutional dilemma is therefore not whether the Philippines should spend on security, but how to maintain a credible security posture while avoiding a budget structure that underfunds the developmental and administrative foundations of state legitimacy.

Public financial management and implementation practice

Formally, defense and public-security spending are subject to the budget cycle, legislative authorization, audit rules, procurement requirements, and executive planning systems. These formal rules are designed to connect appropriations to legality, accountability, and policy priorities. In principle, they should prevent off-budget security commitments, require transparent procurement, discipline multi-year obligations, and align defense plans with macro-fiscal ceilings.

Implementation practice can diverge from this formal model in several ways. First, security procurement is technically complex and often vulnerable to delay, contract fragmentation, sole-source pressures, or weak lifecycle-cost estimation. Second, personnel and pension obligations can crowd out operations and maintenance even when headline budgets appear adequate. Third, security organizations may receive appropriations for acquisition without equivalent capacity for project management, auditing, logistics, or contract enforcement. Fourth, public-security expenditures may be distributed across defense, interior, police, coast guard, justice, intelligence, and local-government channels, making the full fiscal burden difficult to observe from military expenditure alone. These gaps between formal budgeting and practical execution are central to understanding state capacity.

Governance indicators reinforce this interpretation. Government Effectiveness stood at 0.108 in 2023, having decreased by 0.244 from 2013. This deterioration is administratively significant: it suggests that increased fiscal resources do not automatically translate into stronger implementation capacity. Regulatory Quality stood at 0.115 in 2023, up 0.076 from 2013, implying some improvement in rule-making and regulatory credibility. Control of corruption remained negative at -0.376 in 2023, although it improved by 0.27 from 2013. Rule of law also remained negative at -0.293 in 2023, despite improving by 0.504 from 2013. The combined pattern is mixed. The Philippines shows some institutional improvement in legal and corruption-control indicators, but implementation effectiveness remains a constraint. For defense and public-security spending, this means that the binding problem may be less the absolute budget level than the conversion of appropriations into reliable capability, disciplined procurement, and accountable operations.

Debt, fiscal risk, and contingent obligations

The debt indicator in the evidence package requires careful interpretation. Central government debt is reported at 43.43 percent of GDP in 2014, a decrease of 28.17 percentage points from 2004. Because the latest value supplied for this indicator is 2014, it should not be used to characterize the current debt position without additional evidence. Its analytical value here is historical: it indicates that, prior to the more recent period, the Philippines had substantially reduced central government debt relative to GDP. That historical improvement may have supported policy credibility and widened fiscal space for development and security spending.

For the present section, the debt question should be framed as a fiscal-risk issue rather than a claim about the latest debt level. Defense modernization, public-security expansion, and disaster-response capabilities can create multi-year obligations that are not fully visible in annual expenditure ratios. These include procurement arrears, foreign-exchange exposure in imported equipment, maintenance liabilities, pension obligations, guarantees linked to state enterprises or public corporations involved in infrastructure and logistics, and emergency spending after natural disasters or security crises. A state may appear fiscally stable in one year while accumulating future rigidity through commitments that are politically difficult to reverse.

The role of state enterprises and public corporations should also be considered. Even when defense spending is formally recorded in the national budget, security capacity may depend on government-owned or government-controlled corporations, port authorities, airport authorities, transport entities, energy firms, and infrastructure agencies. These bodies can affect defense and public security indirectly by financing or operating strategic infrastructure, logistics corridors, communications systems, and resilience assets. The fiscal risk is that weak coordination between budgetary agencies and state enterprises can obscure the full cost of security-relevant infrastructure. Conversely, well-governed public enterprises can strengthen state capacity by supporting maritime logistics, energy security, and disaster response without overloading the core budget.

Historical and structural causes

Several structural features help explain the Philippines' defense and public-security spending profile. The country is an archipelagic state, which raises the administrative cost of territorial control, maritime monitoring, inter-island logistics, and disaster response. Security capacity must be distributed across islands and coastal zones rather than concentrated in a single land corridor. This geography creates high fixed costs for presence and coordination even when the defense budget is not large by international standards.

A second structural factor is the coexistence of external defense needs and internal public-security demands. Public order, insurgency-related legacies, maritime disputes, transnational crime, disaster response, and local security coordination can all compete for attention and resources. The result is a security sector that must be both military and administrative: it must defend territory, support civil authorities, coordinate with local governments, and operate within legal and fiscal constraints. This broad mandate increases the importance of public financial management because the same peso cannot simultaneously finance salaries, ships, aircraft, police mobility, court capacity, detention facilities, and local service delivery.

A third historical factor is the uneven development of state institutions. The evidence shows improvement in rule of law and control of corruption since 2013, but both remained negative in 2023. This indicates that formal institutional reforms may have produced gains without fully overcoming enforcement, integrity, and accountability deficits. In defense and public security, such deficits are especially consequential because secrecy, urgency, and technical complexity can weaken ordinary oversight. The administrative challenge is to protect legitimate operational confidentiality while preventing confidentiality from becoming a channel for weak procurement discipline or limited accountability.

Comparative-administration implications

Comparatively, the Philippines illustrates a common problem in middle-income democracies with strategic security pressures: the defense burden may be moderate in macroeconomic terms but still significant in administrative terms. A high-capacity state can often sustain security modernization because it has deep revenue systems, professional procurement agencies, effective audit institutions, and credible multi-year planning. A lower-capacity state may struggle even with lower defense ratios because budget execution, procurement integrity, maintenance systems, and interagency coordination are weaker.

The Philippine case also shows why defense spending should be analyzed together with public security rather than separately. Military expenditure captures only part of the security state. Police, coast guard, courts, corrections, intelligence, border management, cyber institutions, and local public-order mechanisms shape the real fiscal cost of security. A narrow defense-only measure may understate the total administrative burden. At the same time, aggregating all security-related spending without functional distinction can overstate militarization. A specialist should therefore distinguish external defense, internal security, law enforcement, justice administration, emergency management, and strategic infrastructure.

This section should resolve one institutional dilemma: how can the Philippines sustain credible defense and public-security capacity while preserving the fiscal basis of developmental state functions? The answer cannot be derived from spending levels alone. It requires evidence on whether resources are allocated toward the right mix of personnel, readiness, maintenance, capital modernization, civilian oversight, and local implementation. It also requires testing whether security allocations are protected by transparent fiscal planning or instead depend on ad hoc political prioritization.

Evidence to check and statistics that would test the claims

Several recent indicators should be checked before drawing stronger conclusions. First, the latest defense, police, coast guard, justice, corrections, intelligence, and disaster-response appropriations should be compared to actual obligations and disbursements. This would test whether formal budget authorization is converted into implementation. Second, the composition of security spending should be separated into personnel, pensions, operations and maintenance, capital outlays, and procurement commitments. This would reveal whether the state is buying capability or mainly financing establishment costs. Third, military expenditure should be compared with education, health, infrastructure maintenance, and social protection expenditure to measure opportunity costs inside the development strategy.

Fourth, updated armed forces, police, and coast guard personnel data should be checked against total employment and public-sector employment. The current armed-forces indicator reports 0.4 percent of the total labor force in 2020, but it does not capture the broader public-security labor claim. Fifth, procurement data should be examined for project delays, cost overruns, foreign-exchange exposure, and lifecycle maintenance liabilities. Sixth, debt and fiscal-risk data should be updated beyond the supplied 2014 central-government debt figure before making any current claim about debt sustainability. Seventh, audit findings and budget-execution reports would be especially useful for distinguishing formal rules from implementation practice.

The strongest statistical tests for this section would therefore include: military expenditure as a percentage of GDP; military expenditure as a percentage of general government expenditure; armed forces personnel as a percentage of the labor force; total public-order and safety spending as a percentage of GDP and total expenditure; personnel compensation as a share of defense and police budgets; capital outlays and maintenance spending as shares of security budgets; budget execution rates for defense and interior agencies; procurement completion rates; pension liabilities; debt-service ratios; and tax revenue as a percentage of GDP. Together, these indicators would show whether the Philippines is building durable state capacity or accumulating fiscal rigidity.

Overall assessment

Defense and public-security spending in the Philippines reveals a state with moderate security expenditure, an improving but still limited revenue base, and mixed governance capacity. The country's 2024 military expenditure of 1.3 percent of GDP does not by itself indicate an excessive macro-fiscal burden. Its 5.4 percent share of general government expenditure, however, shows that defense competes meaningfully within the public budget. Revenue and tax ratios have improved since 2014, but expenditure responsibilities have also expanded. Governance indicators show partial institutional strengthening, especially in rule of law and control of corruption, yet government effectiveness has weakened over the decade covered by the evidence.

The main implication for state capacity is that fiscal credibility depends less on headline defense growth than on the quality of allocation and execution. A security budget that finances salaries without readiness, procurement without maintenance, or equipment without logistics will produce limited capability. Conversely, a moderate but well-managed budget can strengthen territorial control, disaster response, maritime governance, and public order. For development strategy, the central risk is crowding out: security modernization must not erode the fiscal space needed for human capital, infrastructure, and local service delivery. For policy credibility, the central test is whether the government can make security commitments that are transparent, affordable, and executable over multiple years. For future reform, the challenge is to integrate defense and public-security planning into a broader fiscal framework that recognizes opportunity costs, lifecycle liabilities, debt risks, and the administrative foundations of effective state power.
